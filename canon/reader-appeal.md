# Reader appeal

From Appendix A, Part 1 and Part 2 (E). Evidence labels: [EE] established
evidence, [RO] reader observation, [CI] craft inference, [CH] creative
hypothesis.

## The promise

Romantasy readers buy a promise: in a dangerous world, one person is seen
accurately, chosen deliberately, and made stronger by love. The tools that
deliver it best: specific curiosity gaps, suspense about characters readers
already like, and intimacy built from disclosure met with understanding.

## What readers buy [RO+EE]

Being seen and chosen, safety inside danger, empowerment. Radway's readers
sought escape and vicarious nurturing; romantasy adds the heroine's own power.
Readers rejected stories where the hero's cruelty went unredeemed: the
pleasure is the transformation.

## What readers remember [RO]

Moments, not plots: the first act of protection, the love interest's private
vulnerability, a line that reframes everything, the final sacrifice. Plan at
least one of each per book.

## Reader-appeal map

| Appeal | Need served | Compelling when | Goes wrong when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Being seen accurately | Understood without performing [EE] | Observation is specific and costly | "Not like other girls"; mind-reading |
| Being chosen | Worth, security | Choice has a visible price | Possessiveness disguised as choice |
| Hidden power | Escaping smallness | Power brings obligations and moral risk | No change, only revelation [RO] |
| Safety inside danger | Protection without diminishment | She protects him too | Rescue that strips her agency |
| Desired unchanged | Acceptance | Desire includes her "monstrous" parts | Purely physical |
| Found family | Belonging | Each member has an arc | Interchangeable quippy sidekicks |
| Choosing one's fate | Autonomy | Freedom costs something loved | No consequence |
| Worthy despite the past | Shame relief | The past has real victims; repair needed | Trauma as decoration |
| Love that challenges beliefs | Growth | She changes a belief on the page | Change off-page |
| Competence | Efficacy | Visible skill growth | Unearned power-ups |
| Moral intensity | Meaning | Choices between legitimate values | Strawman villains |
| Sensual fulfilment | Desire without shame | Sex changes the relationship or plot | Interchangeable scenes that stall plot |

Additional fantasies to try [CH]: the competent one finally relied upon; being
given a choice for the first time; forgiven without being excused; rest, being
with someone whose presence lets vigilance stop.

## Tropes [RO]

- Favourites (2025 survey of 2,000 US romance readers): forbidden romance 45%,
  friends-to-lovers 44%, enemies-to-lovers 42%.
- Most disliked: love triangles 23%, office romance 22%, fake dating 21%.
- Enduring: forbidden, friends-to-lovers, enemies-to-lovers, slow burn, forced
  proximity. Reading as interchangeable to critics: fae courts, brooding
  morally grey males, deadly trials. Use them only with a specific twist.
- Best combinations [CI]: one trope supplies proximity, another opposition.
  Enemies + forced proximity; arranged marriage + secret mission; tournament +
  alliance with expiry; fated bond + resistance to it.
- Beloved vs badly executed: a reason specific to these characters, a
  transition readers can track, and a price. Readers audit labels ("not really
  enemies to lovers").

## Four engines [CI]

**Arena** (survival pressure), **court** (information and power gaps),
**gothic** (threat from within), **rule** (one metaphysical constraint).
Readers of one engine don't automatically like another. Each series picks one
primary engine and states it in the bible.

## Comp lessons [CI]

Describe qualities; never imitate prose or reuse invented terms.

- A series can reread its own first romance as a warning.
- Relentless external threat makes readers "predictable but super hooked".
- Romance as a contest of wits.
- The rules, not attitudes, make them enemies.
- Momentum can outweigh prose complaints (never an excuse for weak prose).
- Shifting romantic focus mid-series splits readers.
- A planted betrayal puts a clock on every tender scene.
- A single costly rule can generate a book.
- Magic styles can express compatibility.
- "Is it real?" as sustained uncertainty.

## Memorable leads [CI]

Desirable through protective competence, frightening through capacity for harm
aimed elsewhere, vulnerable through a private cost, unforgettable through a
value they won't trade. Attachment comes from goal clarity, vulnerability,
competence and moral choice under pressure.

## Common complaints to avoid [RO]

Cheesy dialogue, immature prose, "painful monologues", predictability,
stereotyped characters; heroines who don't change, only reveal "inner
awesomeness"; status obsession.

## Segments

Heat level, darkness, age (this factory writes adult only), reader origin.
Romance-first readers prioritise the relationship arc and a happy ending;
fantasy-first readers audit the worldbuilding. Serve both.

## Subgenre map

| Subgenre | Promise | Heat | Ending |
|---|---|---|---|
| Romantasy (core) | Romance and fantasy equal weight | Varies, often explicit | HEA/HFN per couple; series cliffhangers common |
| Fantasy romance | Romance arc is primary | Varies | HEA required |
| Dark romantasy | Danger, taboo, antihero | High | HEA, often morally compromised |
| Gothic romance | Dread, secrets | Low to moderate | Ambiguous to HEA |
| Paranormal romance | Contemporary + supernatural | Moderate to high | HEA |
| Cozy romantasy | Comfort, low stakes | Low | HEA |
| Literary fantasy with romance | Theme, image, voice | Low | Bittersweet allowed |

## Market context (October 2026)

The category is moving from land-grab to competition on quality: US print
romance rose 24% in H1 2025 but only 3.9% for full-year 2025; fantasy fell
8.7%. Appetite is shifting darker and hybrid; "romantasy fatigue" is forecast
in a crowded field. In Kindle Unlimited, series read-through and release pace
matter more than in trade publishing.

## Market gaps (all hypotheses) [CH]

1. Heroines who change a belief, not just reveal power.
2. Non-aristocratic love interests whose power is competence or office.
3. Older protagonists; neurodivergence.
4. Conflict from value disagreement rather than miscommunication.
5. Non-European folklore and non-court settings.

## Most promising opportunities

1. Intimate-act world rules (Oath-Law, Name-Ownership, Warmth Economy). Build
   from a real institution like contract law or debt.
2. Values-collision couples: legitimate disagreement replaces miscommunication.
3. The changed heroine: name her opening belief and the choice that proves it
   changed.
4. Grief and remembrance romance: darkness from loss rather than abuse.
5. Sustained dramatic irony: each lead holds half the secret.
