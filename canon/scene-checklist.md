# Scene checklist

From Appendix A, Part 8. The drafting agent plans every scene against it; the
critic scores against it.

Every scene answers:

1. What does each character want in this scene?
2. What changes by the end: power, knowledge or relationship?
3. What question does the reader carry out?
4. What does the reader know that the character doesn't?
5. What does this interaction reveal that exposition couldn't?
6. What does the next decision cost emotionally?
7. Does the relationship change because of this event?
8. What detail might gain new meaning later?
9. Is suspense from real uncertainty or an artificially withheld answer?
10. Does the scene do at least two jobs?

## Failure diagnostics

- Does chemistry exist without narration saying so?
- Why these two specifically?
- Would the misunderstanding survive one honest conversation? If not, cut it
  or make speaking costly (a world rule or a loyalty).
- Is magic solving a problem it wasn't set up to solve?
- Does each romantic refusal have a new reason?
- Would a smart version of this person do this?

## Romance movement rule

In every scene where both leads appear, the relationship changes, and the
reason it changes is new. Replaying the same refusal is repetitive tension and
fails critique.
