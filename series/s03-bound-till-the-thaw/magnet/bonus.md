# The Captain's Count

*A The Winter Passes bonus story*

The first thing Rohan noticed about the guide was that she did not look at the wool.

Six wardens were working the goat pen under the salt house. They drove iron probe spikes into Bindu's raw wool bales, pulled them out, and drove them in again. It was the ordinary sweep, due every tenth day. The yard of Kothi Shilak had learned to stand through it with its hands folded and its face shut.

People who hide things look at the thing. They cannot help it. Rohan had watched a hundred of them. They stared at the false-bottomed cart or the loose floorboard, then at the sky, then at the cart again. He had once arrested a man because his eyes kept returning to a sack of onions.

The woman beside the gatepost looked at Rohan.

She was perhaps thirty, with a cropped black braid and a brown pattu worn thin at the elbows. She stood with her weight on her heels. Her lips moved a little. It took him a moment to see that she was counting. Every time a spike went in, her mouth shaped a number.

"Nine," she said, aloud this time, to nobody. "Ten."

"Is there a reason you're counting, Guide?" Rohan asked.

"There are eleven bales," she said. "Your men have probed ten. I like to know when you're nearly done."

It was not an answer to the question he had asked. It was an answer to a better one, and it was true. He had learned to hear the difference. Her eyes did not leave his face. She was not watching a warden. She was reading a gauge.

"The captain should know," said Varun cheerfully, from the pen rail, "that the goat-folk love the law. They told me so on the road. Weren't you saying it, Guide?"

Varun was thirty-eight and wore his uniform like a man who had ironed it himself. He had a new brass button on his coat, small and domed, among the old pewter ones. He had told Rohan that morning that a pedlar sold it to him after he lost one on the stair. He touched it now, as he touched it often.

"I said my mother died of the grey cough," the woman said. "Ten winters ago. The medicine was on the wrong side of a line somebody drew on a map. I don't say I love anything."

Varun laughed. The yard did not. An old man by the wall, with three fingers on his left hand, looked at his boots.

"Hakam," the woman said, without turning. She tipped her head at the old man. "Frost took the other two. He was carrying salt to his daughter. If you want to know what the Closing Law is for, Captain, ask Hakam. He's very polite about it."

"I am asking you," Rohan said.

"Then it's for the Salt Guild," she said. "And the regent's grain carts. Not for Hakam."

That was a pointed thing to say to a warden in front of six wardens. Anyone who spoke so had decided they had nothing to lose, or had decided what they stood to lose was not in the yard. Rohan filed that away. Then he looked at the left gatepost. The Closing Law notice was nailed there, black letters on yellow paper gone brittle with weather. Past the gate stood the deodar with its pegs, one hammered into the trunk for every hanging. Two were pale from last winter.

He did not look at the tree. He knew what it said.

The sweep finished. Eleven bales, eleven probes to the arm's depth, nothing found. Varun called it so across the pen. "Wool, Captain-ji. Wool and more wool. And Bindu there says she'll weep if we cut it open."

"We don't cut," Rohan said. "It's a week's work to spin that again."

A thin woman by the pen door let out a breath. He noticed that too.

When the wardens had gone out to the horses, he went back into the pen alone. He said he wanted to check the rail. He stood by the eleventh bale and pulled the spike from where a warden had left it standing in the wool. The tip came out with a dust on it, fine and red-brown.

It smelled of bitter bark.

He knew the smell. He had last met it in the crown stores at Taravan, in a locked room, labelled for the queen's physicians. He rubbed the dust between finger and thumb. The wool had not been probed deep enough to find the packets. But the spike had gone in at the edge of something, and something had left a trace.

Redbark. The cure for the grey cough, in a goat pen at Kothi Shilak, on the day he rode in with the queen's seal sewn into his collar.

He had two duties and they pointed in opposite directions. The first was the seal. It had to reach the Snow Fair in twenty days. The second was the Closing Law, which said a warden who found a known load must take it. A warden who let it pass was a harbourer by the letter, and the letter carried the same rope as the load.

He folded the dust into a scrap torn from his glove lining and put it in his coat. He wrote no report. Eleven years in the service, and it was the first thing he had ever left unwritten.

Dilan would have laughed at him. Dilan had laughed at everything, including his own oath. Rohan did not think of that. He thought of the widow Puja instead, whose eighteen silver he had paid through the post clerk two nights before, unsigned, for salt found in a dead husband's lean-to. It was a thing he did and did not discuss. He was a man who held that rules did not bend, and he kept a short, private list of the times he had bent one. He was not proud of how many lines it held.

He walked out of the pen. The yard had thinned. The guide stood at the gate with the old goat handler, Jassi, who was telling her something about a goat. She had her arms folded and her head down. Her lips were moving again.

"Guide," Rohan said.

She looked up. Her face closed, neatly, like a latch.

"Captain."

"The crossing party is Varun and myself, with two guides. Jassi has the line. The others return to the post."

"Jassi does not cross with a stranger and no second," she said. "Not in this weather. I come."

"That wasn't offered."

"It wasn't asked either." She said it flatly. She did not say please. He could tell she had never once said it to a man with a badge, and was not going to start for him. "He's forty years old and the Saddle has a long memory. I carry the second rope."

He looked at her hands, the habit of three winters of reports. They were nicked across the knuckles, the nails cut short, one old burn-scar white along the forearm where the sleeve rode up. Ordinary hands.

She might be a very fine liar. Or she might be an honest woman, which on the Shilak was rarer and more dangerous. He could not tell. He knew only that when she had said the Salt Guild owned the law, nothing in her voice had moved.

"Your name?" he said.

"Meera."

"Then you may come, Guide," he said. "I'll thank you to keep the counting for the road."

The corner of her mouth moved, a quarter of an inch, and was gone.

He rode out of the yard half a length behind Varun, with the red dust in his coat and a line of numbers going on behind him in a voice he had not decided to listen to.

* * *

He woke in the Rope-Hut to the smell of juniper and the sense that someone had been carrying him for a long time.

It took a moment to find the ceiling. Stones, black with a hundred winters of smoke. A shaft of grey light from the one slit under the roof. The cough was still in his chest like a dry hinge, but he could draw a breath to the bottom without it catching. That had not been true the last time he had counted.

He lay still and took the inventory, as he did every morning. The ribs, the seventh and eighth on the left. The arm that would not rise past his shoulder. The fever, gone or going. Under him, the shelf and a pattu. Over him, another. The hearth held a banked red eye.

Then the rope. It lay in a loose red curve across the floor from his wrist, eight arm-spans of wool, ending at the wrist of a woman asleep sitting up.

She had her back against the wall by the door and her knees drawn up. Her head had dropped sideways onto her shoulder. One hand lay open on the floor, palm up, nicked and red across the knuckles. She had not undone the rope. She had not gone farther than it let her. She looked like someone who had sat down for a moment and lost the battle.

He had been ill for three days. He remembered pieces. A hot tin cup at his mouth. A voice saying *Drink it, Captain, I didn't carry this up here to watch you spit it on the floor.* A hand, quick and cold, at the neck of his coat. That one he remembered clearly because it had frightened him.

The coat lay folded under his head. He turned onto his good side, slowly, and put his hand to the nape. The seam.

The pocket was there. The stone was there, a hard thumb-joint of lapis behind the wool. The cord that held it came out of the seam with a snug little double hitch. Two turns round the standing part, a tuck, a locking half-turn.

He lay and looked at the ceiling.

It was his hitch. He had tied it himself a hundred times, and it was the only knot he knew that a stranger could not fumble. But the half-turn at the end had been pulled tighter than he pulled it. A woman who was frightened had done that. She had tied it back as she found it, and then, at the end, hauled on it as if to be sure.

So she had found it. She had opened the seam, or felt for what a feverish man was clutching, and taken it out. She knew. It was the only thing that made the hand at his collar make sense.

He did what he always did with a fact he could not yet bear. He listed what had been available to her.

Three days. The man with the seal helpless on the shelf. Hunters in the valley who had been told where to find him. Somewhere below, a regent who would pay a lordship for a lump of blue stone. She was a smuggler's guide on a pass the law had made her enemy. She had already, by his own reckoning, a lie in her mouth and a load in her pack. She could have walked it down the hill. She had a rope that said she could not go farther than eight spans from his wrist, but she had a knife, and a rope can be argued with. She could have sold him for the price of the stone and the price of her brother's whole village.

She had not.

He turned his head. Beside her knee lay a waxed notebook, open, held down against the draught by a flat stone. He should have looked away. He read it upside down instead, the way he had read a thousand other people's files.

Her hand was small and square and very clear. The last entry was dated by the days of the crossing. Beneath a line of numbers about packets, it said: *One captain. Fever, lung. Redbark, half-pinch, two. Price: none.*

*Price: none.*

He read it three times. He did not know what it meant. A woman who kept a ledger of every person she had carried would have a price for everything. A half-pinch of the medicine she had risked a rope for, spent on the man who hunted her crew. She had spent it. She had written it down. And beside it, in the column where the figure belonged, she had left the word *none*.

He did not understand what he was looking at. He thought of it as a debt of hers he had not been told about. He would be wrong, but not for a long time.

What he understood was smaller. In the whole of his service, nobody had held the seal and put it back.

Rohan did not trust people. He believed in reasons. A man did what he was bound to do or what he was paid to do, and the interesting questions came afterwards. But he lay on that shelf, with the light creeping across the floor, and found there was no reason in the world he could write for what she had done. No order, no price, no gain. There was only the knot, tied back a little too hard.

He found that he had stopped asking what she wanted from him. It was an odd feeling. It was like a floor going quiet underfoot.

He decided it the way he decided most things. He did not say it to himself. He simply took her off the list of people he was watching, and put her on a different, shorter one, and found the shorter list was already in his hand. It held his sister, and Dilan, and a few others. He had never before added to it by choice.

"You're staring," Meera said.

Her eyes were closed. He had not seen them open. She sat exactly as she had slept, head on her shoulder.

"I was reading," he said.

"The count is private."

"Then you ought not to leave it open beside a man with a fever."

"I left it open beside a man who was unconscious. You weren't supposed to wake up and develop opinions." She opened her eyes. They were red-rimmed and very dark. She looked at his face, then at his hand where it lay on the nape of his coat. "Ah."

He said nothing. Silence is free. He had been practising it for three days, and it was the only thing between them that cost nothing to give.

"I looked," she said. "I want you to know I looked. I felt the stone through the seam, and I opened the seam, and I took it out, and I held it for some time. Then I put it back."

The rope lay slack and red between them. He watched her fingers on the floor. They stayed the colour of her hand. No white at the tips, not even the first thin line of it.

"I know," he said.

"You can't know. You were fevered."

"I know the knot. You tied my hitch."

"Bindu taught me a flat reef when I was five." She shrugged, stiffly, the shrug of a woman who has slept sitting up. "I undid it and did yours. It was the only thing to do. I didn't want you waking and thinking I'd left it loose."

"You pulled it tight."

"I was scared," Meera said, and her hands stayed warm, and she looked at them. "I'd never held a thing like that. It's a very small stone. You'd think it would be heavier."

He should have said thank you. He could feel the words behind his teeth. He had never learned to say them without wanting to leave the room, and there was nowhere to go. The rope was eight spans and the door was barred.

"Thank you," he said.

"Don't." She said it quickly. "It wasn't the rope. I want that clear. If something had hummed I'd have told you it was the rope. Nothing hummed. I had a stone in my hand and I wanted to put it back, and I did."

"Then I owe you for the half-pinch."

"You owe me nothing. That's the word I wrote." She heard herself say it, and her mouth went tight. He had read the line and she knew it. "Never mind what I wrote."

"I wasn't going to ask."

"No. You were going to burn a stick on it."

"You set the price of a refusal," Rohan said gravely. "I was only following the rules."

She stared at him. Then she laughed once, short and helpless, like a cough. It broke something in her face. She put the heel of her hand against her eyes and sat there with her shoulders shaking, and he understood that she had been holding herself rigid for three days.

He did not reach for her. The rope did not allow it, and he did not yet know the right of it. He lay on the shelf and let her finish. He listened to the fire settle and kept his hands where she could see them.

After a while she wiped her face on her sleeve. "You've stopped rattling," she said.

"I believe so."

"Good. Eat something." She nudged a clay bowl toward him with her boot. "It's sattu, and it's awful, and there are twenty-one sticks of juniper between us and the rest of the winter."

He ate it. It was awful. He added a line to a list that existed only in his head, the one he had kept since Dilan, and it said: *Meera. Seal returned. Half-pinch spent. Owed.* He did not write a figure, either.

* * *

The night after the Snow Fair, Rohan measured a sleeping woman's hand with a length of cord.

It was the cord Meera used for the woollen flasks, a hand-spun brown thing with knots every thumb's breadth. He had borrowed it from her pack with the knowledge that she would object and the sense not to ask. The guest-house at Garh Neshar gave them a high room under the eaves with a rope-bed, a brazier, and a window that looked down on the frozen meadow, where the stalls of the Fair were coming down in the dark. The bond held them to eight spans. The room was eleven paces across, so the rope had been laid in a loose red circle and neither of them thought about it.

Meera slept on her back, one arm outside the blanket. Her left hand lay palm down in the lamplight.

Nimmi had said two days. She would come on the second grain cart the hill council allowed over the lower road, and the ring and little fingers must come off. The middle finger would live. The dead ones were already dark to the first joint, waxy, and had no feeling in them. She had not flinched when the lamp touched them. He had checked, twice, with the corner of a blanket, and hated the checking.

He knelt beside the bed. He took the cord and laid it against her wrist, then across the palm, and tied a knot at each place. Wrist. Heel of the hand. Base of the first finger. The third. He did the same on the right, because the hands matched, and because he wanted to be sure of the left.

She stirred. He stopped.

Her fingers curled once in her sleep and the three live ones closed on the cord. She held it, loosely, as she held a rope. Then she let it go.

He stayed where he was.

He knew what he was doing. He was making a thing for a hand that did not yet exist. He was measuring ahead of the knife, preparing for what the knife would leave, because he could not do anything about the knife.

It was the oldest weakness he had. Dilan used to call it the Captain's disease: if he could not mend the thing, he tried to plan around it. Two winters of rope end in his coat were the proof. He had not been able to mend Dilan. He had planned a vengeance, and carried it every day, and it had been pointed at a boy in a bed in Ranu who had dragged himself through a winter to cry on Rohan's chest.

*I did not reach for her hand.* He had told her that. She had told him it was the right thing. He did not think it was right. He thought it was the thing that had been left to him once she had made it clear she wanted her lie kept.

He tied the last knot and sat back on his heels. In the lamplight the hand on the blanket looked like any hand. He wondered if she would hate the glove. She was not a woman who liked to be fussed at. She had never said please to anyone, and he doubted she would say thank you to him. But the dead fingers were numb, and a numb hand cannot feel frost. On the Shilak the cold took what you could not feel.

He tucked the cord inside his coat, next to Dilan's rope, and stayed up the rest of the night keeping the brazier alive.

* * *

Ila arrived at mid-morning with a glover, a bolt of felt, and a boy.

"This table is a hand short," his sister said, from the doorway.

"Good morning, Ila."

"The builder swore it was eight spans. It is seven and a hand." She had her measuring chain over one shoulder and ink on her cuff, and she was thirty and looked fifteen when she was pleased. She had been in Garh Neshar since first snow, and the cold had put colour in her face. "This is Bimla. She makes the best gloves in the lower town. I asked three people and none of them said so, which is how I knew."

The glover was a broad, patient woman of fifty with grey in her braids and a roll of cut leather under one arm. She looked at Rohan's grey coat and did not lower her eyes.

"I have a rule," Bimla said. "I don't work for wardens."

"I understand."

"You don't. I had a nephew. He carried barley over the Saddle, and your people took the barley and half his teeth. I don't ask you to say it was wrong. I tell you why the rule."

Rohan stood and kept his hands open. "I can't leave this room," he said. "I'm bound to someone who can't be moved, and the law I serve is going to be rewritten by people I haven't met. I've no right to ask a favour. I'm asking one."

"What for?"

"For a woman with three fingers on her left hand. By tomorrow night." He said it plainly. "A glove of felt, lined with lambskin, cut for a thumb and three fingers. The little finger and the ring finger are being taken off. She has no feeling in them now. I need a hand that will keep what's left of her warm in the snow, where she can't feel it fail."

Bimla looked past him at the bed. Meera lay as she had slept, face turned to the wall, the mended blanket drawn to her chin.

"Is she your wife?"

"Not yet."

"What happened to the hand?"

"She told a lie," Rohan said, "to save a person she loves. It cost her the fingers. She would tell it again." He did not look away. "I was asked a question I should not have asked, and she answered it in the only way she could. The cold is mine in a way I can't undo."

A silence followed. In the corner, the boy who had carried the felt was looking hard at the floor.

He was twenty-four and thin as a rake, with a torn ear and a way of keeping his shoulders at his collar. Kiran had walked down from the walnut terraces on a salt-road pony and had not spoken above a murmur since the Fair. He sat now on the end of the window bench with his knees up and his hands tucked between them.

"Two silver," said Bimla, "and no lie. If she asks, you tell her."

"She will ask. I'll tell her."

"Then take off your coat and let me see your hands. I have to know how a man holds a woman's glove before I make it."

It was an absurd request. He did it. Bimla turned his right hand over, then the left, with the wrist that still would not rise, and clicked her tongue. She laid out the felt on the table. Ila, who could not stand a table she disapproved of, began to pace it, muttering. Bimla worked at the cord. She measured Meera's right hand against his knotted lengths without waking her. She did not need to be told twice which finger was which.

By noon she had cut the palm and the thumb. By evening she had the lining pinned. She sat on the floor with her back to the brazier, sewing in tiny stitches, and the room smelled of lambskin and warm wool. Meera slept through all of it.

Kiran came to the table when Ila had gone for bread.

"Does she know?" he said.

"Not yet. I won't tell her until it's finished."

"That's not a lie."

"It's a silence. They are different animals." Rohan said it gently. "She taught me."

Kiran put out one finger and touched the felt. His eyes were on the empty finger-sleeve, the one that would sit useless beside the thumb. "She did it for me," he said. "I'd like to pay for the lambskin."

"No."

"Rohan."

"You'll pay in another coin," Rohan said. "You will let her carry something for you without saying a word of gratitude. She'll hate it. It will cost you a great deal." He took a breath. It hurt, but less than it had. "It is the best way I know to be loved by that family."

For a moment Kiran's face did something complicated. He looked down, and his hand rose to the ruined rim of his ear, and fell. Rohan knew that gesture. It was the same one he made himself with the rope end.

"He'd have hated all this," Kiran said. He did not use the name. He did not need to. "The fuss. The glove."

"He would have sung," Rohan said. "Badly."

Kiran made a sound, half laugh and half something else, and turned his face to the window. Rohan did not move. He had learned that much from the mountain. There is a kind of silence you give a person as a gift.

He thought: *this one is going to try to pay.* He did not know what the debt was or to whom. He only saw it plainly, as he saw a bad ledge: the boy sat like a man who had a ledger of his own, with a single line in it, and the figure at the bottom was a life.

He put it away to think about later. Later turned out to be a long way off.

At midnight Bimla bit the last thread and held the glove up to the lamp. It was felted grey, lambskin pale inside, with a small leather loop at the wrist and four neat sleeves where there should have been five. The empty ones had been sewn shut and stitched flat, so they would not catch.

"Good enough?" she said.

"It's perfect."

"It isn't. It's a glove." But she smoothed the cuff with her thumb before she gave it to him. "Tell her the stitching will want a week to soften. And tell her I charged you double."

"Did you?"

"No," said Bimla, "but she'll like to hear it."

He slid the glove inside his coat, against the rope end, and sat down on the floor beside the bed. The cold of the window came through the wall. The rope lay across his knee in its loose red curve. Meera did not wake.

He took out the private list, in his head, and added: *Glove. Paid.* It looked absurd. It looked like the first honest thing he had written in a year.

* * *

The stones spoke at the thaw, and by midnight the post at Kothi Shilak had gone quiet in the way of a building that has been told something and does not know what to do with it.

Rohan did not sleep. He sat in the warden hall with the post book open before him, where Gopal, grey at the temples and nine winters his man, had entered the declaration in a firm clerk's hand. *Captain Rohan declined to deliver the guide Meera. Witnessed.* Beneath it a smaller line said: *The stones spoke. All present felt it.*

He read that second line several times. *The Old Bargain is unpaid.* He had felt the words arrive in his hands, in the cold itself, not in his ears. *After the fourth winter, the pass will close and not open again. Unless someone pays.* They had not sounded angry. They had sounded tired, like a creditor who has waited a long time and is willing to wait a little longer.

*Someone.* He had seen Meera's face when it ended. She was not looking at the stones. She was looking at the road, where Kiran had not been. Kiran had stayed at Garh Neshar to mend. Her hand, in its grey glove, had gone to her pocket, and he had known without being told that she was thinking of the way her brother had sat at the drafting table, silent, and nodded when Rohan said *it is for the next one.*

He closed the book.

The wind had dropped. When he stepped out, the cairn stood in the dark at the foot of the road, a ring of flat stones to his chest, and a thin smell of snowmelt came off it. In the stones, for the first time in his life, water was running. He could hear it tick.

He went to the ring and put the lantern on the capstone. On the inner face, in the pale light, the old carving looked back at him. Two hands bound by one rope. He had seen it, as every warden had, a hundred times. He had never really looked.

One hand was broad, a working hand with stubby fingers. The other was longer, narrow, careful. The rope between them was drawn with great tenderness, each twist exact. Near the foot of the stone, the rope's end had been cut. Or untied. It could be read either way.

*Two brothers in arms*, said the chronicle. He did not think so. These were not brothers. He did not think they had ever been the same trade, the same blood, or the same road.

The cream rope was still on his wrist. Two arm-spans, undyed, tied in his warden's hitch. It did not bind him to anything. He had worn it all evening anyway.

"You should be asleep," Meera said, behind him.

She had come across the frost in her good boots. She wore her brown pattu over the new grey glove, and her braid was coming loose. Under her arm was the count. She never went anywhere without it now, although she wrote in it less, and on some days not at all.

"So should you."

"I tried." She came to stand beside him and looked at the carving. "I kept counting. It's a bad habit. I got to two hundred and seven and then I had to start again, because the number has changed, and I don't know what to do about it."

"Add one."

"I did. Then the stones talked and I wanted to add another."

He did not ask. She did not say. After a time she put her glove on the capstone beside his lantern, and the felt was grey against the dark rock. Four sleeves, one stitched flat. She had said very little about the glove. She had said, *you measured my hand while I was asleep*, in a voice that wanted to be angry. Then she had gone out onto the landing for a quarter of an hour. He had stayed where he was. Silence is free. He had learned to give it.

"Kiran," she said now.

"I know."

"He sat there and nodded. And I thought, he's going to do something stupid and I won't be there."

"You will not be alone in it," Rohan said. "That is a promise I can't make. But it is an intention, and you may write it down." He held up the bare hand with the cream rope on its wrist. "Watch my hands."

She watched. They stayed warm. There was no bond to test them against, and there never would be again, and she looked at them anyway with the open face of someone who had decided to believe a thing on its own merits.

"They're not cold," she said.

"They never will be, on that account."

She turned her head. In the lantern, with her braid half down and her breath showing, she looked tired and entirely herself. "I'm still frightened," she said. "Of the law. Of the queen's pen. Of the day someone reads the Roll and sees my name."

"I know."

"You'll be with me."

"I will be walking to Taravan beside you, with the law in your hand," Rohan said. "And a warden's stubbornness at your back, if you'll have it."

She smiled, the quarter-inch smile, and then more than that.

They stood a while. Frost whitened the wall. The water in the stones ticked and ticked. Somewhere down the dark road a goat bell was ringing, the big green one Bindu had brought up for the thaw. He thought of Jassi and of a kid-bell buried with a child. He thought of Dilan, and his bad hymn, and a rope cut clean. And he thought that he had never in his life been so happy to stand in the cold.

The courier came at first light.

Gopal brought the letter to the cairn himself, not trusting it to a boy. It had come over the pass in the first open week, sealed with Ila's thumb-print in green wax, folded small and tied with her own surveyor's cord. It was dated three days before the thaw. It had waited at the post for someone to walk up the road.

Rohan read it standing, with Meera at his shoulder. His sister's hand was fast and square, and it did not waste ink.

*Rohan.*

*Forgive the haste. The weather has turned and I am packing the instruments in case the road opens. I have good news and a puzzle.*

*The good news: the queen has confirmed my commission. I am to finish the map of every high pass, the hidden ones too, and she wants them named by autumn. She wrote it herself, in a shaking hand, and says I shall be Chief Surveyor if I can do it. I should be dancing.*

*The puzzle: the commission came under an archive seal I have never seen. It is stamped, not waxed, with a mark like two hands on one rope. I asked the courier what it meant. He said it was in the old registry and nobody had used it in sixty winters. I asked the queen's clerk, who is a thin careful man with a way of looking at his hands. He told me the seal was chosen on purpose. Then he asked me a question I have not been able to put down.*

*He asked whether our grandfather was a man who kept his gloves.*

*I said I didn't know what he meant. He said it did not matter, and changed the subject. But Rohan, I have never in my life been asked about him by anyone in the palace. Do you remember anything? Anything father said when he drank?*

*Give my love to your guide. Tell her the north wall at Garh Neshar is two spans out of true, and that I told you so.*

*Ila.*

Rohan read it twice.

The wind had come back, a thin one off the high snow, smelling of wet stone. Meera said something beside him, a question, but the words reached him from a long way off.

*Our grandfather was found at the thaw. His gloves laid one on the other on a flat stone beside him.*

He had never understood why. His father had told it once, drunk, and then not again. A man freezing does not fold his gloves. A man caught in a storm throws them off, or loses them, or clutches them to his chest. He does not set them down on a stone.

Unless he is not caught. Unless he has finished with them.

"Rohan," Meera said again, quietly. "What is it?"

He looked down. He had taken off his gloves without noticing, and he was holding them in his two hands, one laid on the other, as if on a stone, and he could not remember taking them off.
