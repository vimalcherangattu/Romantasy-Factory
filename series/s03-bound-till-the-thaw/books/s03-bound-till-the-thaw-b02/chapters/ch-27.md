# Chapter 27: Past the Rope

The cleft had no fire, and by the second hour Ila could not feel her feet.

She had stopped trying to. She sat against the gravel wall where Kunal had set her, knees drawn up, hands pushed into her armpits. The cold in the cleft was dry and patient. It came up out of the rock and did not care whose it was.

The blue stones ran away into the dark like a string of beads. They were the only light. Kunal sat across from her with eight spans of undyed wool lying slack on the gravel between them. He had not spoken since he told her not to say anything true.

His hands lay open on his knees. In the faint light she could see the blisters had burst, and the palms shone wet. The fingertips on the right were grey. He did not tuck them away or blow on them. He looked at them the way she had seen him look at the Drum from the shore, as if they were a distance he was measuring.

She had told herself a number of things to pass the time. That the toes would go. That two toes were a fair price for a prince's life, and a fair price was a thing she understood. That he was in pain and she should let him be.

None of it moved the cold, and none of it moved him.

"Kunal."

He did not look up.

"You'll want to see to your hands. There's a strip left on my hem. I can't reach it. You could—"

"Ila." He said it quietly. "Don't."

She stopped.

After a while he got up. He did it slowly, a hand on the wall, the way a man gets up from a sickbed. He stood looking down the passage toward the mouth, toward the hollow under the ice lip and the lake beyond it. Dhanu's goat-horn whistle hung on his coat. He had lifted it off the lintel nail at the hut, with the note still pinned beside it, and he had not taken it off since.

Then he raised his left wrist and put the rope's knot to his teeth.

She understood what he was doing a moment too late. The wool was wet and the knot was hers, the warden's double hitch she had first tied at the Meadow Cairn with cold fingers and a good deal of hope. He worked it with his teeth and the heel of his hand. It took him a long time. She watched every turn of it come loose.

Her own end stayed tied at her wrist. His came free. He did not drop it. He wound the loose end twice round his fist and closed the fist on it.

"It ends nothing," said Ila. Her voice came out cracked. "You know it ends nothing."

"I know."

"Then—"

"Dhanu is out there on the ice with a scrap of wool on his chest and nobody has shut his eyes." He did not turn round. "His sister put her hand on him as if she were testing bread, and then she ran, because I told her to. Raghu is still there. I'm going to walk back."

"They're bound pairs on the cornice. Four of them, with a spyglass."

"Yes."

"You'll be one man with no paper."

"Yes."

He walked.

She watched his back go down the passage between the blue stones. The slack slid off the gravel and followed him in a long pale curve. At the sixth stone it began to straighten. At the eighth span it snapped taut, and she felt it at her wrist, a thin hard tug, with his fist at the far end of it.

The cold hit her then.

It was nothing like lie-cold. That had come in at the fingertips, a blade laid flat. This came from everywhere at once, as though the whole cleft had drawn a breath and held it. Her breath smoked. Her chest tightened. The heat she had been hoarding in her armpits drained out of her like water from a cracked jug.

The blue stones dimmed. She watched it happen. The faint line that had run so steadily down the passage went thin, and thinner, and the stone nearest her guttered like a lamp in a draught.

His shoulders jerked. He stood at the end of the wool, not yet past it, and did not turn.

*One long pull,* she thought.

It was Meera's signal, one long pull for *come*, and every pair in the high valleys knew it. The rope was taut and she had it at her wrist. She could haul on it with both arms and her whole weight against the wall, and it would drag at his fist like a halter. It was not even a lie. It was only a pull. He would turn, because the rope would turn him.

She took hold of the wool with her swollen hands. It was a long way off from her body, and she held it a long time.

Then she let it go slack in her fingers.

It would have been easy to call it kindness. It was closer to arithmetic. If she hauled him back, she would have a man at her side who had been hauled, and he would sit across from her until the thaw knowing she had done it. She had spent every day since the Meadow Cairn learning this man's habits. He could bear a good deal, but he hated to be made to stay.

Far down the passage, his fist opened. The loose end of the wool fell and lay across the gravel like something shed. He walked on.

She filled her lungs. The air hurt going in.

"Kunal!"

His step did not break.

"I lied to keep you alive," said Ila. The cleft threw the words back at her, one at a time, down the long dark. "I told you the sheets were ash, and they weren't. I'd do it again."

Nothing happened. Her fingers in her armpits stayed numb, but they were only cold. No new chill came. The stones did not ring. The truth went out of her and the mountain let it.

"I'd do it again," she said, quieter, to herself. "Every time."

He was a shape between the blue stones, small now. She counted him. It was the only thing she could do. *Eleven spans. Twelve.* The cold had gone from a hand to a fist. Her teeth had begun to knock.

*Thirteen.*

She could not feel her legs at all now, only a heavy, drowned ache from the hip down. She had told him that at twice the length the path vanished. She had read it in every chronicle the survey office held and thought it quaint.

*Fourteen. Fifteen.*

He stopped.

She could not see his face. His shape had stopped, the shoulders had come down, and he stood very still in the thin blue dark, with the passage opening ahead of him toward the lake and the dead and Raghu's lantern.

She did not call again. There was nothing true left that she hadn't said.

Then he put his ruined hands to his own face.

A long time went by. The cold squeezed. Somewhere deep in the rock the meltwater ticked on stone, once, twice, a slow, hateful sound.

Kunal turned round.

* * *

He came back slowly, like a man walking up out of deep water. The blue stones brightened as he passed them, one after another, and the iron fist of cold loosened in her chest by degrees. Her breath came easier. Her face was wet before she knew why, and then the tears went cold on her cheeks.

He stopped a pace from her and stayed standing.

"Your life is behind me," said Kunal. His voice was rough and very low. "That's the sum." He looked at his hands. "I'd have done it. I want you to know I wanted to."

"I know."

"Don't be kind about it."

"I'm not," said Ila. "I'm cold."

It startled a breath of laughter out of him, a cracked and ugly thing. He sat down heavily on the gravel. For a moment she thought he would put his head on his knees and stay that way.

"I don't forgive you," he said.

"I didn't ask you to."

"You lied to me. You lied about the one thing I begged you for. You stood there with your hands in your sleeves and said *gone,* and I believed it because I wanted to, and a man died on the ice while I knelt at a hearth counting cinders." He drew a breath. It shook. "You say you'd do it again. I believe you. That's what I can't—" He stopped.

"Say it."

"You'd do it again," said Kunal, "and you'd be right, and I hate it."

She had no answer to that. It was true, so she let it stand.

What she would remember after the thaw, when she turned the night over, was that he stayed without forgiving her. He reached out with one blistered hand, and with the other he took hold of her ankle. He drew her bad foot toward him through the gravel, as one drags a stray lamb closer to the fold.

"Kunal, the cold—"

"I know what it is. I've had it." He worked the boot off, the one he had pulled back onto her at the shingle, then the sound one, the laces already cut. He unbuttoned his coat and the shirt under it. Then he lay down on his side along the gravel, back to the wall, and pulled both her feet against the bare skin of his belly. He drew the coat shut over them.

She gasped. He was warm. He was warm like a hearth.

"Don't talk," he said into the dark. "I can't be kind and not talk to you at once. Pick one."

"Not talking."

"Good."

The hands lay on her ankles, burnt and rough, and held. His breath moved slowly under her heels. After a while his grip stopped being the grip of a man who held a thing and became the grip of a man who held on.

They lay like that for hours.

* * *

At some point the dark went thin and she was awake, and the cold had changed.

Her feet hurt. That was good. A foot that hurt had a foot attached. She lay with her cheek on the gravel and her eyes open and watched the blue stones pulse, slow and faint, as the mountain breathed.

She thought of her grandfather.

She had not meant to. She had spent years not meaning to, ever since she first read the chronicle entry over her father's shoulder. Warden Harish, who went out in a night storm to check the cairns for a lost party. Her father, over his clock and his one good eye, saying the gloves were like a pair set out for the morning. A flat stone on the slope above the third bend. Two gloves laid palms together, square to the edge, the fingers pointing the way he had been walking.

The gloves had come back to her in the queen's hand, plain as a receipt. A man did not take off his gloves at the end of a storm night by accident, and he did not lay them square by accident. It was a payment-sign. It said *here, this is given.*

Why anyone would choose it, she had not understood. Now she did, and it was worse. Her hands had gone past hurting, and all she wanted was to set them down somewhere square and be finished.

A flat stone lay beside her head. It had come out of the cleft wall at some point, a slab as long as her forearm, tilted a little toward the passage. She stared at it.

Slowly, with fingers she could not feel, she pulled off the left glove, then the right. She laid them on the stone. She turned them palm to palm, the left on the right, and set the fingers pointing down the passage, toward the dark, the way a person would walk.

She stopped with her hand a finger's breadth above them.

It would be so easy to finish. The mountain was asking nothing she had not already half agreed to. She was cold, and she was alone in a way that had nothing to do with the man at her feet. He had said he did not forgive her, and he meant it. He held her because letting go would have killed her, and that was a different thing from love.

Her grandfather's answer lay on the stone in front of her. It was clear, and it was cold, and she was so tired.

She picked up the gloves.

Without ceremony, the way she would put a pencil back in her pocket. She worked her hands into the wool, one finger at a time, and sat with them in her lap.

*Not yet,* thought Ila. *I know that door. I'm not walking through it.*

Against her feet, Kunal's belly rose and fell. He had not stirred. She thought he was asleep, and then she thought he was not.

"What were you doing?" he said into the dark.

"Counting."

He said nothing. The rope lay across the gravel, its free end where he had let it fall. Neither of them touched it.

* * *

The light came late.

First it was only a greyness at the end of the passage, where the ice lip hung over the hollow. Then it was a line. The blue stones went pale and ordinary in it. Her feet had swollen against Kunal's belly and ached in the long, outrageous way that meant blood. He had finally slept, mouth slightly open, one fist bunched against her ankle.

Boots sounded before she saw anything.

They were light. They came up from the hollow, picking over the glaze, a person who knew the way. Ila lay rigid. She could do nothing else. She had no knife and no feet. Kunal's hand closed on her ankle and she felt the whole length of him wake.

The shape in the cleft mouth was small and broad. A shawl was wound round the head and shoulders. Both hands held something against her chest, a clay pot with a lid pierced in a ring of holes, and out of the holes came a thread of smoke that smelled of juniper.

"Gauri," said Kunal.

His voice broke on it.

She came in without rushing and knelt at the edge of the gravel and set the pot down on the flat stone, the one where the gloves had not stayed. The embers glowed orange through the holes. Ila felt the heat on her face like a hand.

The woman's wrists were wrapped in rag. Her eyes were red-rimmed and very clear. She looked at Kunal's burnt hands, and at the grey on Ila's bare ankle where the sock had come off, and said nothing about either of them.

"I came back along the shore in the dark," said Gauri. "The captain's men were gone from the fold by moonset. They took the boy's rope and what was left of their pairs, and they went up the Kuhl side." Her voice was level. It was the voice of someone who had cried everything out of herself in the night and had nothing but the next thing to do. "I sat with my brother a time."

"Gauri," said Kunal. "I should have—"

"No."

She said it so flatly that he stopped.

Gauri looked at the two of them in the first light, the prince with his shirt open and the surveyor in her coat, and a rope lying between them untied.

"I turned his coat back," she said. "To close his eyes. That's how I found his side, where the shirt was cut." She drew a slow breath. "Dhanu was dead before they wrote it."

Nobody moved. In the pot the embers ticked and settled.

"Then there was never a trade," said Ila.

But Kunal had already gone white to the lips, one hand shut over the whistle he had taken off the nail.
