# Chapter 31: The Shared Map

On the thirty-fifth morning the rope went quiet.

Ila felt it before she woke. The hum had lain under her wrist for five weeks, so low she had stopped hearing it, the way a miller stops hearing the wheel. Now there was only the chulha ticking and Kunal's breath beside her. She lay with her eyes shut and counted to forty. Nothing came back.

"It's gone," she said.

"I know." He was already awake. "It went at moonset. I didn't want to be the one to say."

They went out into the yard in their boots, without their coats, like children told it was a holiday. The rope was a plain grey cord on the boards. Ila picked it up and found it weighed what wool weighs.

The herders' fold stood at the foot of the cairn path, a dry-stone ring with its gate hung on a loop of hide. She sent him to it. Sixteen spans by her reckoning, which meant twice the rope, which meant the place where the mountain had once taken the path away from both of them.

Kunal walked out on the packed snow, upright and unhurried. At eight spans he did not slow. At twelve he glanced back at her, and she saw him wait for the cold. At sixteen he stood with one dead-tipped hand on the fold wall and breathed out a long white cloud.

"Nothing," he called. "Warm as a loaf."

"Say something false."

"What?"

"Anything. Something small. I want to see the hand."

He thought. "I have never once wanted to turn back."

His fingertips stayed grey, as they had been since the Eye. Grey was all they would ever be. She could not read a lie in them. She could not read a thing in them, and it came to her that this was what he had meant, in the dark under the cairn, when he put out the lamp.

"Come back," she said.

He came back at his own pace, and she did not count it.

* * *

They worked twelve days in the salt house top room.

It had a long table that Bindu's people cleared of rope and set under the south window. Ila pinned two sheets side by side, the same size and the same weight of paper, ruled by the same rule. She drew a line on one. Kunal watched her. Then she drew the same line on the other.

It was slow, and she had never been so glad of slow work. Every peak she had measured, she drew twice. Every bearing she carried over from the field book had to match on both sheets to the hair. Where one drifted, she scraped it and began again.

Then Kunal gave her the names.

He did it from the stool beside her, in the hill speech, and she wrote each one as he said it, in the old square hand he had taught her, and she did not tidy a single word. The black notched pinnacle north of the lambing wall was Old Woman's Tooth. The long seam at the Drum's cliff foot, where the ice rang, was Where the Drum Answers. The crag she had named for a healer in a Ranu storehouse was the Widow's Knuckle, for the hand a widow laid on the rock while she waited. She had loved naming it. She wrote *Widow's Knuckle* in its place and felt something give in her chest, a small thing, like a stitch coming out.

Nimmi's Knuckle stayed in her field book, in pencil, in the margin where nobody looked.

On the fifth day Gauri's word came by a boy from the Kuhl side. It was short. Kunal read it aloud and had to stop twice. The hamlets had talked. They wanted the Widow's Ladder on the map. The Long Braid, the hundred and sixty steps, the song. They had kept it from people below for three hundred winters, and they would rather the crown learn it from a surveyor who had climbed it than from a spy who had not.

"They're sure?" Ila said.

"Gauri says the old women voted. She says it took an afternoon and one shouting match." He folded the paper. "They'd rather it was drawn true."

She drew it true. Four pitches, three ledges, the leaning pitch with its fifty-seven steps. She wrote *Widow's Ladder* in the hill hand beside the crown's word, because both were its names. Her pencil did not shake. It was only a line, and it was the heaviest line she had ever put on paper.

On the ninth day there was one crag left with no name at all.

It stood east of the Drum, a low grey tooth above the shore, and Kunal had walked past it all his life without a word for it. He sat looking at the sheet a long while.

"You give it one," he said. "That's the habit, isn't it?"

"I'm out of the habit."

"Then I'm asking you back into it. Once." He did not look up. "It's above the fold where he laid the nets."

She understood. She wrote it small, in the hill hand, on both sheets, and he leaned over to watch the letters come.

*Dhanu's Rest.*

Neither of them said anything for some time. Then Kunal put his hand flat on the table, beside hers, not touching.

That afternoon she went out to re-measure her pace.

The yard was fifty spans end to end, laid out with the chalk line. Her left foot was missing two toes and the right still ached from the swelling. She walked the line with her crutch under her arm, three times, and wrote the figures on her sleeve. One and three-quarter spans. Not two and a half. Her father's chalk line, the brass rule at Taravan, nineteen years of her own body, and it had all gone short in a single winter.

Kunal walked beside her the whole way, counting under his breath. When she lurched, he lurched. When she stopped to write, he stopped.

"One and three-quarters," she said. "It'll change again when the foot mends."

"Then measure it again."

"I will. But I'm not going to chain a hundred miles of mountain in a pace that changes with my feet." She looked at the sheet in her hand, then up at the rope coiled over the yard post. "Everyone in the hills knows a rope. Eight spans of the guide's arm. It's a unit a child can carry in her head."

"You want to put a rope on the scale bar."

"I want to give it both. Spans for the crown. Ropes for the hills. Same bar, same length." She wrote it on her sleeve, and under it, *Ila's pace, after.* "Nobody can say which of us the map is for."

* * *

She told Rohan on the thirteenth evening, in the post room.

It was a bare room with a stove, a table scarred by forty winters of wardens' knives, and a shelf of post books. He sat at the table with his coat open. In front of him, on the wood, lay Dilan's rope end, the clean-cut end toward him. He had not touched it since she came in.

She laid the rubbing on the table. She did not explain it. She told him in order, as she would report a survey: the Under-Cairn, the door, the fourteen marks, the shelf of gloves. The last name, cut in a plain hand with no flourish on the H. The queen's letter, which she had not opened till the Glass Lakes. What the letter said.

"Grandfather went in to be the payment," she said. "Prince Udai took him. He walked in on his own feet, Rohan. The thing in the chronicle, *carried down at the thaw*, is true, but not the way we were told. He walked out again. He laid his gloves on a stone on the hillside where we'd find them. He knew somebody would."

Rohan did not move. His hand came out at last and settled over the grey wool, not lifting it.

"The gloves," he said. "Father told me, once, when he was drinking. One on the other. Square to the stone." He drew breath. "I spent twelve years thinking it was the last tidy thing a cold man did."

"It was the last tidy thing a brave one did."

"The law was written the next spring." He said it to the rope. "For him. Every hanging I signed was for him."

"For the debt they were hiding. Not for him."

"No." He looked up, and his face was not one she had often seen. "I'd have liked to know that before I signed it."

She did not touch him. She had long ago learned that her brother wanted a witness, not a hand. She sat across from him while the stove ticked, and when he spoke again it was only to say, "Tell Meera. She'll want to write him down."

Meera was in the doorway. Ila had not heard her come. She had the count open on her left forearm, the felted glove steadying the page, and a pen in her right.

"Harish," said Meera, to no one in particular. "Warden. Went in sixty winters back, and came out." She wrote it. "That's one I carried and didn't know."

"You didn't carry him," said Rohan.

"Somebody did." Meera blotted the line with her sleeve. "It says *carried* in the song. I'm counting it."

* * *

The thaw came to Shilak on the forty-eighth day, a thin bright thread of water running down the cairn's south face while Gopal watched with his hat in his hand.

The queen came by litter from Taravan on the third day after. It took eight bearers and two mules. Sarala was sixty-two, and the winter had not been kind. She was small in the cushions, with a thin grey face and a stick laid across her knees, and when she climbed out at the foot of the cairn path she did it without help and without hurry. Her eyes went over the ring of stones and the crowd, then found Ila at once. She said nothing.

The hill council had come over the Long Saddle as bound pairs, one rope to a pair, every pair guided by one of Meera's crew. Roop went first with Old Bhim, and Chand came behind with a woman from the upper terraces. Meera brought up the last pair, a farmer and his daughter, with the licence cord swinging at her belt. They stood on the Neshar side of the ring in their blue-corded wool, and the queen's courtiers stood on the other, in slate, and between them the cairn hummed.

Ila had a table set inside the ring. Two chairs, two copies, one pen.

She felt very calm, which she had not expected. She had gone over her oath so many times that the words no longer meant anything. *I swear to show the crown every way, and no other. I swear to draw for the crown alone.* She had sworn it at nineteen, in the Taravan office, with a clock on the wall.

"Majesty," she said. "Old Bhim. This is the map of the high passes of Hemkot and Neshar. There are two copies. They are the same."

She took off her gloves.

She laid them on the table, one on the other, palms together. It was not meant for anyone but herself. Kunal, beside her, glanced at them and then at her face, and she saw him know it.

"My hands are warm," she said, and held them out where the whole ring could see. "That tells you nothing. The bond ended on the thirty-fifth day. Nothing I say here can be checked by the mountain. So you will have to check it on the paper."

She dipped the pen and signed the first copy. *Ila, surveyor.* Under it Kunal signed, left-handed, in his clumsy hill script. *Kunal of Neshar.* The names went on both sheets, in two hands, over every peak and way and hidden stair.

She laid the sheets before them, one before each, at the same moment. The queen's bearers set the paper down in front of Sarala. Old Bhim took his in both hands and held it as a man holds a child.

The silence was long. A boy near the back coughed.

"You swore the crown's work was the crown's," said the queen, quite quietly.

"I did."

"You broke it."

"Before you. On purpose. So you could see it done." Ila did not look away. "I kept a promise I made to him, and no stone in the mountain made me. I'd rather break the oath I swore than the one I chose."

Sarala's mouth thinned. She looked at the sheet. Her eyes went down it the way an archivist's eyes do, from the peaks to the lines to the names, and Ila watched them stop at *Widow's Ladder* and *Dhanu's Rest* and go on.

"We are angry," said the queen. "Understand that. We are very angry. A surveyor's oath is the one thing a crown can buy that does not need watching." She laid her palm on the sheet. "We are keeping the map."

Then Kunal stepped forward.

He had a petition. He had written it with his own clumsy hand, and he read it aloud without once looking down. Self-rule for Neshar under the crown, by its own council. No garrison in any hill village, now or in any year. A seat for Neshar at the council in Taravan. And every way on the map, as drawn, open to the queen's eyes and the council's alike, so that neither could keep from the other what both had been given.

He did not soften it. Ila saw his dead fingers curl at his side.

"Neshar asks nothing it cannot bear to see written," he finished. "We have kept our ways for three hundred winters because we believed whoever knew them would own us. I was taught that. I believed it." He drew a breath. "I'd rather be owned by no one and known by everyone."

The queen did not answer at once. Her gaze moved from his face to Ila's, to Rohan at the rear with his arms folded, to Meera by the cairn with her left hand tucked in its glove.

"Granted," said Sarala. "All four." She said it dryly, as one signs a pardon. "Gopal. Fetch the clerk."

The ring let out its breath. Old Bhim was laughing and crying, and a woman near the back began a hill song and was shushed and began it again. Kunal stood with his hand on the table and his head bowed, and Ila put her palm between his shoulders and left it there, in front of everyone, for as long as she liked.

* * *

The queen sent for her at dusk.

It was a small room off the salt house yard, with a brazier and a stool and a lamp. The stick lay across Sarala's knees. She was alone, and she had dropped the plural. A courtier's voice had gone out of her and left an old woman's.

"You are the first surveyor in forty years who has made me regret a commission," she said. "And the first I would not trade."

"I know what it cost you to send the letter."

"You know very little." There was no heat in it. "I read his name on a list when I was nineteen and did not dare to ask the archive what came after. Sixty winters, I let the law say what I could not. You went and looked." She lifted her eyes. "You have a rubbing."

Ila's hand went to the hem of her coat before she knew it had moved.

"I would like it," said the queen. "Alone, if you please. Before anyone else reads it."
