# Chapter 19: Knucklebones

"No news until you've eaten," said Gauri, and shut Ila's fingers over a fistful of dried apricots as if she were closing a gate.

Dhanu stood behind his elder sister in the door of the lower hut with his mouth open on whatever he had climbed here to say. He had blown the goat-horn whistle three times along the shore, two long and one short, *come on, it's clear*, and he had not stopped limping toward them since. Right foot planted, left swung out and round. He looked like a man who had run a long way with a full bucket and been told to put it down.

"Gauri—"

"Eat. Both of you." She had been cutting him off since he was small, and she did it now without looking round. She took Kunal's wrapped hands in her broad brown ones, turned them over and sniffed the goat fat. "Who tied these?"

"I did," said Ila.

"Flat knots." Gauri gave the hands back as though they had been inspected and passed. "Good."

Ila had been checked by clerks and engineers for twelve years. She had never once felt it go all the way down.

The hut was one room, warm and low, smelling of dung smoke and wet wool. On the sleeping shelf under a pony blanket lay a small shape with one sock off. Mini. Her fist was against her mouth and her breath whistled faintly.

Ila ate an apricot. It was leathery and sour and sweet, and her stomach turned over with gratitude. She ate a second. By the sixth she had started counting, so she ate a seventh to spite herself.

"Now," said Dhanu, "the bones."

"News," said Gauri.

"After the apricots, you said. The bones keep the hands busy while the mouth works." He was already digging in his coat. He came out with a leather twist, and from it spilled five knucklebones, sheep's, polished by years of thumbs. One was stained red. "A woman who can't throw knucklebones can't be trusted with a pass. Sit."

Ila sat on the hearth edge. Kunal folded himself against the wall with his bandages in his lap and the rope slack across the floor. He had the look of a man who meant to enjoy being useless.

The rules were simple, which made them cruel. You scattered four bones on the flat stone by the chulha. You tossed the fifth into the air, swept one bone off the stone with the same hand, and caught the fifth before it fell. Then two at a time. Then three and one. Then all four.

She lost the first game badly. The bones skittered where they liked. Her hand, which could hold a chain steady in a gale, made a fist of itself and dropped the red one in the ash.

"Ha," said Dhanu, with deep satisfaction.

"She's learning," said Kunal, from the wall.

"She's *losing*."

"She's gathering data."

Ila looked at him. He kept his face grave, but one corner of it had gone.

She gathered data. The hearthstone was a river slab laid by someone in a hurry. The side by the door sat a finger low. The third corner rocked, very slightly, under the heel of her hand. She tapped it twice and listened. When she threw the bones again, she did not throw them anywhere. She let them fall from a hand's height on the one spot where the slope walked them together, two and two, with the red one nosing neatly out on its own.

Then she swept, and caught, and did it again with three.

By the fourth round Dhanu had stopped saying *ha*.

"Do that once more," he said.

She did it once more. The bones went where she had meant them to go. They always did. He stared at the stone, then at her, then at the stone.

"Those aren't throws," said Dhanu. "Those are *sums*." His voice cracked upward. "You've surveyed my hearth!"

"Only the one slab."

"She's surveyed my hearth, Gauri!"

"It's been crooked since the day you laid it," said Gauri, without turning from the pot.

"A finger low on the door side," said Ila, "and the third corner rocks. You'd have a draught under that slab in winter."

"I do have a draught!" He slapped his knee and roared. The bundle on the shelf stirred and muttered, and he clapped a hand over his own mouth and shook with it, the tears running down his face. When he could speak he said, "Ten winters I've lost to my own hearth. Never a soul told me why."

Kunal was laughing against the wall, without sound, with his shoulders. It was the rough laugh from the lakeshore, and she did not look at it for long.

* * *

The soup came after. Gauri said "Now," in the tone of a woman shutting a book, and Dhanu put the bones away.

He told it plainly, with his hands on his knees. His boy had run in two nights ago from the upper folds, and what he had seen was this.

Raghu had eight pairs in the Hollow Moon country.

"Eight," said Kunal.

"Sixteen men, each pair bound at a cairn. They came by the Kuhl cut in twos and threes, so the watchers wouldn't count them." Dhanu rubbed his bad leg. "Seven pairs are in the bowl. They've dug it like a field. Nine holes round the door cairn, and more along the east wall. The boy says they sit round the fire at night and don't talk."

"They took Hari's goats," said Gauri.

"Nine of them. Drove them up the Hollow Moon road in a string." His voice dropped. "Hari wasn't with them. Nobody's seen him since the hut burned."

Ila remembered the hut. She remembered the black ring of it, and the chalk on the marker stone with its second line smeared out. She put her spoon down.

"They haven't found the lakes," said Kunal. It was not quite a question.

"Not the basin. Not yet." Dhanu looked at him. "The eighth pair went off along the rim shelf, and the boy followed their tracks as far as he dared. They've sat on the Kuhl shoulder, the one over the head of the lakes, two nights now. He saw their fire from the upper fold."

Nobody spoke. The fire settled in the chulha.

Ila did the sum. She could not stop it, and it came out the same each way she ran it. A steady orange fire on a shoulder of rock above the last lake, seen from the foot of the Ladder, where Kunal had said nobody kept a fire. She had tied it to Raghu's pairs then, and still she had walked in under it. Two bound men could see the blue light low in the cleft. A shoulder that looked straight down on the Eye. And that morning she had stood on that ice in full sun, drawing the mouth, with the rope slack and a book in her hand.

She looked up. Kunal was already looking at her across the hearth. His face had gone still, and she saw him reach the same figure, in the same order, and arrive where she had. They might already be looking down on the cleft. They might have watched the two of them kneel to chip a hole in the ice.

Neither of them said it. Dhanu had spent himself climbing here, and Mini slept ten feet away with her fist at her mouth. A number said aloud in this room would sit on the child's blanket until morning. Kunal gave the smallest shake of his head, and Ila picked up her spoon again. She could taste nothing in it.

"Mini," said Dhanu, after a while, in a different voice. He was looking at the shelf. "She's asked after you. All the way up. 'Is the lady with the string still counting?'"

It went into Ila before she had thought. It slid under all the sums.

"The star I named for her," she said. "I wrote it down."

Dhanu's head came round.

"The small yellow one over Ewe's Rest, the night we came in. It had no name in any book I own, and it was bright enough to shout." She kept her eyes on the pot, because it was easier. "Kunal said it wouldn't go on the map. It won't. It's in the margin of my field book, in pencil, which isn't the map. But it's hers. It has her name against it, with the bearing."

Dhanu said nothing at all.

When she looked up, his eyes were full. The tears stood in them, bright, and did not fall. He was a big man, and he had laughed himself weak over a crooked hearth an hour ago, and he sat with his hands open on his knees and could not say a word.

Gauri put her palm flat between his shoulders and left it there, as she must have done when he was a boy with a scraped knee.

"She'll want it on the map," he said at last, thickly. "She'll make you swear it."

"I'd rather swear to that than most things," said Ila.

He scrubbed his face with the heel of his hand. Then he did what she had come to expect of him, and reached for a laugh to stand on.

"Look at him." He jerked a thumb at Kunal. "Sitting there like a lamb in a blanket. Do you know how long it took me to teach *him* the bones? Seven winters of evenings, and he sulked every time a goatherd's child beat him. Stubborn as a stone, our Kunal, and you'd never believe the half of what's behind it. Ask him what his uncle did on the ridge when he was—"

"Dhanu."

It was said flat, and it was not loud. It went through the room like a snuffer over a wick.

Dhanu stopped with his mouth still shaped for the next word. His hand came down off his knee. In the stillness Ila heard the lid rattle on the pot, and Mini's whistling breath, and the wind going over the roof.

She looked at Kunal.

He had not moved from the wall. His face was composed. His hands lay in his lap in their wrappings, and he was looking at them with great attention, as if he meant to count the knots. But the colour had gone out from under his skin. His mouth had the set of a door pulled to from the inside.

*And I came home,* he had said, in the snow cave on the Hollow Moon, and put the rest where she could not reach.

A question was ready. It had come up whole, the way they did, with its bearing already taken. *What did your uncle do?* It was fair. She had a right to ask it, with the rope round her wrist and the hill's half-spoken story lying open in the room.

She thought of the letter flat against her ribs. Then the question he had put to her on the shelf, the narrowest one, and how he had taken her refusal and set it down without building on it. *I'll find out in daylight, from you.*

She did not ask.

"My throw?" said Ila.

Dhanu let out a breath that went on a good while. "Your throw," he said, small and shamefaced, and he took the bones out again, and put them in her hand.

She threw without looking at Kunal. The bones fell exactly as she meant them to, and for once she took no pleasure in it.

* * *

Dhanu had put them in the second hut, the smaller one, where the chulha had been lit that morning. It was a plank shelf and a stack of dung cakes and one blanket. They had shared quarters nine times now, and neither of them mentioned it.

Kunal had said almost nothing since the bones. He had eaten, and thanked Gauri, and let Dhanu clasp his shoulder at the door and say, "I'm a fool, brother," and answered, "Yes," quite gently. Ila had taken her boots off and lain on the shelf with her face to the wall and the rope across the blanket. She listened to him not sleeping.

The Drum boomed, far off through the cliff. It was a slow single note, and then nothing.

"You didn't ask," said Kunal.

She was not asleep, and said so. "No."

"Why?"

She thought about it, because he deserved a true answer and because it cost nothing to give one. "You said *and I came home*, in the Moon, and then you put the rest somewhere I couldn't reach. I thought it was yours to bring out. Or not."

"Dhanu would have told you. If you'd asked him tomorrow, when I was out of the room." His voice was very even. "Half the hill knows a piece of it. Dhanu has the best piece. It's the one with the joke in it."

"I wasn't going to ask Dhanu."

"I know." The blanket shifted. "That's why I'm not going to let him."

She turned over. He was sitting up against the wall with the bandaged hands on his knees. In the red light of the coals his face had no courtesy on it at all.

"I'll tell you myself," said Kunal. "Not from Dhanu. From me." He stopped. She watched him make himself go on. "I don't know what it's worth. I don't know what you'll do with it. I've been trying to work it out since the bones, and I can't, and I think that's the point of it."

Ila sat up. Her heart was going hard, and she was careful to keep her voice level.

"All right."

"Not here." He looked at the door, and past it, to the dark and the lake and the long ringing ice. "There's a place on the shore where you can hear the Drum. Where it booms. I'd like to be standing when I say it."

"Where the Drum answers," said Ila, "you lie still till morning."

"I know what it says." One side of his mouth moved, not quite a smile. "I've never in my life done what it says."

"Then we do it with the figures in front of us." She said it now because the room was empty of anyone it could frighten. "The open shore is in plain view of the shoulder. But the Drum fold, the cut under the cliff at the far end, is behind the overhang. I drew it from the mouth of the Eye this morning. From that shoulder the cliff hides it. The fold itself is safe."

"And the way to it?"

"Three hundred paces of open shore, there and back. That they can see, if they look." She counted it off on her fingers, because it steadied her. "It's two men. One sleeps. The one awake is staring into his own fire, and a man who stares at a fire can't see across snow at night. We carry no lamp. We keep to the shadow of the bank. Against that, a glass, a cold clear sky, and two shapes on white ground. I put it at one in ten that anyone looks at that stretch tonight, and I can't make it smaller. If they see us, they'll know the lakes are lived in, and that is what we lose."

"One in ten."

"I've walked worse for a smaller reason." She put her feet over the edge of the shelf and found her boots. "It's your choice and I'm weighing it with you. I'm not following it."

The rope went with her, and with it the whole length of him, so there was no question of going alone. She tied the laces by feel and did not hurry them.

When she stood, he held out one wrapped hand, palm up, the way she had once left hers on the boards. She laid her own in it. The bandage was rough, and the hand beneath it was warm.

He pulled the door open on the cold and the stars. Far down the shore the Drum spoke again, one slow note under the ice, and the whole dark answered it.
