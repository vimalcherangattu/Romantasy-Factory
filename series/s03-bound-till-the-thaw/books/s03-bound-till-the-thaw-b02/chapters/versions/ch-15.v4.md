# Chapter 15: Taking the Cold

Kunal took her hands.

He did it before she could close them. He had set the pot down so he would have both hands free, and he spent no breath on whether he should. He folded her fists open and wrapped them in his, palm to palm.

"Don't," said Ila. "Kunal, don't. You'll know."

"I know where to find the answer. Hold still."

He had learned the loophole at six, the way every hill child did, from a grandmother who told it as a warning and a lullaby together. *The one who was lied to can take the cold. Skin to skin. And then they know.* Children played at it in the lanes with snow and a ring of stones. Nobody had told him what it would be to do it with a woman who had just said *no* in a level voice and gone white to the knuckle.

For the last minute he had been holding a small, shabby doubt. She had said *the storm*. She had said *I've been wet since the scree*. A frostnipped hand looks much like a lie-cold hand, if a man wants it to. He had wanted it to. He had sat there with her hands on display and let the fire's failure to warm them mean nothing at all.

Touching her would end that. He knew it, and he held on anyway.

The cold came up his arms like the Mirror in spring, when you go through to the elbow.

It was not hers any more. It was his. It went into his fingertips first and hit the bone, and then it climbed. With it came the knowing, clear as a name read off a stone. A lie. Not small. Middling, the weight that costs a night. She had said several untrue things about the cold tonight, but a lie this size could only have come with the one answer that mattered.

*Do your orders name these lakes?*

*No.*

The fire cracked in the dung cakes. Kunal sat very still with her hands going warm between his, pink coming back under the nails, and felt his own go dead.

He learned no truth. That was the cruelty of it, and the mercy. He knew only the shape of what she had hidden, like the hollow in snow where something has lain. He could not tell what her orders said, or why she had put a lie over them. He knew he had been lied to, and how much it had mattered, and that was all.

"You've gone grey," said Ila. Her voice cracked on it. She stared at their hands. Hers were pink and shaking and her own. His were the colour of tallow. "Let go. You can't take it all. It's too much, you'll—"

"It's a night's shaking. Nimmi would call it a nuisance."

"That's not what Meera said about—"

"Meera told you about a great lie." He held on. "This isn't one."

"It's still mine."

"It was yours until I asked." He heard how hoarse he'd gone. "I could have kept calling it the storm. Your hands would have let me. I could have gone to sleep tonight on a doubt I'd never had to look at." He found half a smile. "I seem to like my ignorance less than I thought."

"Kunal."

The first shudder took him at the shoulders and ran down his spine. His teeth went. He tried to say something dry about it, and his tongue would not shape the words.

Ila said something. He lost the words. Then there was wool around him, the grey pattu, and her arms, and he was pulled in against her side with his head on her shoulder. She was so warm. It was absurd. It was the ordinary warmth of a body, the heat that had left her an hour before and come back, and it was lent to him now with both arms and no argument.

"Don't talk," said Ila into his hair.

"I want to say—"

"Not now."

"I'd rather say it—"

"Morning." Her arms tightened. "If I tell you now it'll be to stop you shaking. I won't do that to the truth. Tell me you can wait till it's light."

He lay in the circle of her arms, and the cold went through him in long waves. In the gaps between them he understood what he had done. He had taken the lie into his own flesh and, with it, the knowledge of it. He now knew that his bound partner had lied about her orders, and he had chosen that. Nobody had made him. A smaller man would have stood at the edge of the doubt and warmed his hands at it. His uncle would have.

*Whoever knows the way owns the people.* And what did knowing her cost? He could not say. He had never bought anything so dear on purpose.

"I can wait," said Kunal, and the shaking took him.

* * *

He barely slept. The shudders came in rounds, each worse than the last, and between them the world went small and bright: the red mouth of the chulha, the smoke finding its hole, a seam of cold air under the door. Ila fed the fire without letting go of him. She had found a way to stretch and reach the dung cakes with one arm while the other kept him. Twice she wedged more of Dhanu's blanket round his feet. Once he woke with his face in her neck and her bare hand flat against his chest.

"Your heart's going like a goat's," she said. She sounded wrung dry.

"Yours?"

"Steady. I counted it."

"Of course you did."

She laughed, once, a cracked sound with no joy in it. Her breath shook. After that she said nothing for a long time, and he lay in her arms with his teeth going like dice and knew, quite clearly, that she was crying, and that she did not want him to say so.

He did not.

By the time the hut's seams went grey, the shaking had thinned to a tremor. His hands were stiff and prickling, flesh again to the last knuckle, though the tips stayed pale. The wind had gone. In its place was a vast listening quiet, and under it, once, far off, the long boom of the third lake letting go of the night.

Ila sat up slowly. She wrapped the pattu round them both and sat cross-legged in front of him, hands on her knees, palms up, in the dim.

"Check the work," she said. It was what she said to clerks who doubted her sums. "Hands first."

He looked. They lay open and pink in the first light, the nails clean, the knuckles easy.

"The letter names the Glass Lakes," said Ila.

There were no stones here to sing. Her hands did not change.

"My commission says only 'every way.' That's all of it. But there is a letter, in the queen's own hand, sealed. It's sewn in my coat, here." Her hand went flat to her ribs. "The fold says to open it when I stand at the Glass Lakes. Not before. I took the letter for part of my orders, so when you asked, I said no, and it wasn't true. And it wasn't the storm, either. I let you think it was. I don't know why she wants the lakes. I've carried it a week and haven't opened it."

Kunal looked at her hands. He looked a long while.

They were warm.

"Why," he said. It came out as a breath. "Why did you say no?"

"Because I was afraid you'd turn back." She did not look away. "You said yourself you would. If my orders named your lakes, you'd have walked me to Ewe's Rest at dawn and shut the Glass Lakes like a door. And I wanted to see where your way goes." Her mouth shook. "I wanted to walk it. Before the queen's hand came between us and told me what it was for. That's all. It's no better a reason than it sounds."

*I wanted to see where your way goes.* It was a surveyor's reason. It was also, he thought, the plainest thing anyone had said to him in thirty years.

"You could have refused," said Kunal. "It's free."

"You'd have read it. A refusal on that question was an answer."

"Yes." He thought about it. "I would have."

"I know." She let out a breath. "I did the sum, and I did it wrong. I thought it was the lesser cost."

"It was." He turned his own hands over in his lap. The tips were still waxy, and he was glad to see them. "It cost you a night of this, and me one. And I've the truth now, which I'd choose over the quiet."

"Do you want the letter?" She said it very low. "I'll hand it to you sealed. I don't know what's in it. It may be a warrant. It may be a map. It may say that my commission is to hold your people's ways, and then I'll have to choose in front of you."

He might have said *give it to me*. Pritam would have said it, softly, with his hand already out.

"It's yours," said Kunal. "The queen wrote it to you."

"And if you're in it?"

"Then I'd like to be told in daylight." A crooked look crossed his face and stayed. "With my teeth in."

* * *

The storm had spent itself in the night. When they came out, the sky over the lakes was scoured and high, the cold so clean it rang in the nose. Snow lay in long ribbed drifts along the shore, and the ice beyond was blown bare, a dark floor with the wind-curl still frozen into it.

They stood on the scree with their breath smoking. Ila had her field book out and was not writing in it.

"The second lake," she said. "What do people call it?"

He knew what she was doing. She was handing him the night's name early, on credit, because she could not yet stand to open anything else. He took it.

"The Drum."

"Because it booms."

"Because it's the loudest. My father said it was the heartbeat of the valley." He heard how it sounded. "My father said many foolish things at the Fair. That was one of the better."

She wrote it down in her small hard hand. *The Drum.* Then she shut the book and slung her pack.

"The winter line crosses it," she said. "I'd like to know what it crosses before we trust it. That's not a delay. It's what I'd do on any day of my life."

"Then do it."

The Drum lay a long half hour beyond the Mirror's far shore, over a lip of rock and down a shelf of drift. It was smaller than the first lake, a dark oval bounded by black cliff on one side, and its ice was not smooth. Seams cut across it in great pale lines, shore to shore, where the plate had heaved in the night.

"Boom lines," said Ila, kneeling at the edge. "Every one is where the lake let go. It lets go where it's weakest." She slid an iron spike out of her pack, as long as her forearm, with a ring welded in the head, the kind a surveyor drives to hold a chain. "Give me your knife."

He gave it. She laid the spike against the ice and set the point of his knife in the ring like a lever, and began to chip. It was slow work, the spike turning and the blade biting, and chips of white flew up and stung his cheek. In a quarter hour she had a hole as wide as her fist, and black water welled to the lip.

She dropped a length of cord with a knot in it down the hole. It sank, and caught under the ice, and she drew it up until the knot snagged. Then she held the spike beside it and counted along its length, her lips moving.

"Eleven fingers."

"Is that good?"

"It's a number." She wrote it. "A pair with packs wants eight. Three wants twelve. That's what I've always used on rivers, and it's never lied to me yet." She sat back on her heels. "Eleven holds the two of us with packs. It won't hold a third."

"We're only two."

"Raghu's men are two pairs. If they cross close, they're four, and whatever is behind them." She had her pencil out already. "I'd like to know where they'd go through."

He wanted to say that nobody was following them. He swallowed it. There had been a spyglass on the Hollow Moon. He knelt beside her and took the spike when she held out her hand for it, and he drove it where she pointed, at the next seam, forty paces along, while she worked the knife.

It went like that all morning. She never hurried. At each boom line they cut a hole, and she dropped the cord, and she counted and wrote. *Nine. Ten. Fourteen on the thick side, under the cliff. Seven, here, where the black water showed through, and a cross marked over it.* He watched the numbers go down the page in her handwriting, small and square and sure. After the first hour her shoulders came down from her ears. After the second, she was humming under her breath, off key, a counting tune.

Whatever was in her coat, she had left it there. She had a lake to read, and the lake was honest.

*A pair but not three.* He found himself marking the places with her, a little behind, in the margin of his own head. A pair could cross where nine fingers lay. A pair could not cross where seven did. Where fourteen lay, you could march a column. His uncle's men would have given a year of their lives to buy such knowledge.

And she was writing it down in front of him, with her hand open on the page, and he had not once asked her to stop.

Perhaps that was what the night had bought him. He had taken her cold and paid it, and he had not turned into a man who guarded what he'd paid for. He turned the thought over carefully, as a man handles a bone he thinks has healed. It held.

At noon the sun came over the cliff and lit the Drum from end to end, a great dark oval stitched with white seams and Ila's small black holes. She stood at its centre with the spike in her hand and the last number in her book, steady. He could see it in the line of her back.

Then she turned, and the steadiness went. Her hand rose, as if of itself, to her coat.

"I've sounded what I can sound," said Ila. "I haven't another reason."

She said it to the ice. He walked across the plate to her, on her tracks, and stopped with the length of the rope between them slack.

"Open it," said Kunal.

She drew a long breath. She unpicked the stitches at her ribs with the point of his knife and drew out the folded square, thin and cream and stiff with wax. The seal was red, and when the light struck it a thread of darker red ran through it like a vein.

She looked at him once. Then she put her thumb under the wax and broke the seal.
