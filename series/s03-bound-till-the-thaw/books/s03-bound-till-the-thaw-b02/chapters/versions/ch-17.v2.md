# Chapter 17: The Shape of a Silence

Ila had not looked him in the eye since the shelf above the Drum.

She looked at everything else. She looked at the roof poles of Dhanu's upper hut, the smoke hole, the juniper stack, the grain of the split-deodar door with the wind leaning on it. When Kunal fed the chulha, she looked at his hands. When he passed her the tea, she took the cup by its rim, so their fingers never met, and watched his hands go away again.

Her own stayed where he could see them. They lay open on her knees, or flat on the blanket, or spread on the sleeping shelf like a woman showing a clerk she carried nothing.

The storm had caught them an hour past the lip, a white wall coming over the spurs faster than her pocket watch allowed. They had made the hut with the rope hauled taut between them and the third lake still unseen. That was a day and a half ago. The wind had not stopped since, and the stones outside sang in it, a low note with no meaning in it.

Kunal had learned her silence the way he learned a new road. It had edges. It began exactly where the kiss ended. She talked well enough about the weather, the ice, the thickness of the hut walls, which she had paced twice and found a hand narrower than the north side. She answered whatever he asked her. But when he asked anything that came near the shelf, her eyes dropped to his hands and stayed there.

By the second morning he had a theory, and he disliked it.

She was ashamed of the kiss. She had been cold and grateful and wrung out after a night in which he had taken her lie onto his skin, and she had paid him the only coin she had. Now the coin sat between them, and she was too honest to take it back and too kind to say so.

He thought about it while he did the one useful thing the storm allowed, which was the petition.

It was four sheets, folded in his coat, for the thaw council. He wrote it on his knee with one of her pencil leads, warmed against his chest and held in his fist like a coal. The hill council wanted a seat at Taravan, and the keeping of the high ways under the new law, and no crown garrison in any hill village. Every clause was a thing he had said aloud at a long table. None had cost him anything to write until this week.

He wrote *the high ways* and stopped. He knew what the clause meant. He had been looking at three days of Ila's field book, and for the first time in his life he understood that "ways" was a word you could hand someone, and that they could draw it.

He finished the line. Then he put the lead down and said, to the roof poles, "If it was the cold talking—"

"It wasn't."

She said it before he reached the verb. She was crouched at the door, chipping ice into the water pot with the back of her axe, and her head did not turn. But her hands had stopped.

He looked, because he could not help it. They were pink. The nails were clean, and the knuckles were easy, and they were hers.

It was true, then. And it left him with less than he had come in with, because the theory had been tidy and the truth was not. If she did not regret it, and was not cold, and was not ashamed, then there was a wall, and he could see it plainly. He could not name a single stone.

"Good," said Kunal. He found a smile and put it on. "I'd hate to be the sort of man who was kissed by weather."

She laughed. It was the short, loud one, cut off at once, and it startled the water pot. Then her gaze went to his hands again, and the quiet came back down.

* * *

The wind dropped a little after dark, enough that they could hear the lakes through it. Far off the Drum boomed, one slow note, and then another answered it from somewhere under the cliff.

They had the sleeping shelf to share and one blanket's worth of room on it. The first night, they had lain like two planks. Tonight Kunal banked the fire and came back along the rope, and Ila was sitting up against the wall with her knees drawn in.

"Your grandfather," she said. "Prince Udai." She said it carefully, like a woman setting a foot on rotten ice. "You said he walked the way in winter. What was he like?"

It was the first thing she had asked him in a day that was not about a measurement. He sat down at the shelf's end, a hand's breadth from her boots.

"I don't know. He died when I was four." He thought about it. "I have a beard that smelled of apricot smoke, and being lifted onto a wall to watch the Fair. That's all of him I own. The rest is my uncle's."

"What did your uncle say?"

"He said it at supper, the autumn I was nine. My father was a week in the ground." Kunal turned his hands over. "Pritam likes a lesson to arrive with a meal. He said my grandfather had shown the way once, to one guest, in winter, and was not the same man after. Then he put down his knife. *The way kills those who share it,* he said. 'Remember that, boy.'" He heard how light his voice had gone. "That was the whole story. The lesson's on the end like a rope on a post."

"And the rule?"

"My grandfather's rule was only for winter. My uncle made this one that night, over the fish. No prince shows the way to wife or son or friend. It stays in the head of one man at a time." He tried the smile again. "It's a very good rule. Between them, the two have kept the crown out of Neshar's hills for sixty winters."

Ila had gone still. It was a particular stillness, the kind that came over her at a bearing that would not close. Her eyes were on his hands.

"You've gone quiet," said Kunal. "It's a tale for frightening boys. I've shown you three ways in twenty days, and you're alive."

"Yes," said Ila. "I am."

It was not an answer. He put it with the others.

"The night's name," he said. "You measured the long seam at the cliff foot, where the plate rings. Hill people call it Where the Drum Answers."

She had her field book out already. "Because it echoes?"

"Because when it answers, you lie still till morning. You never cross on a night the Drum answers." He watched her write it, small and hard. "My nurse used to say it to me. *Where the Drum answers, lie still till morning.* It was how she got me to sleep."

Her pencil stopped on the last word. Then it finished, neatly, and she shut the book.

"Your question," said Kunal. "I've a name given, so I've a question owed. Or you may refuse."

"I know the terms."

"I've been choosing it all day."

She did not move. He thought of the other questions he could have asked. *What does the letter say. What did you mean by yet. Why can't you look at me.* Any of them was fair. Any might have been the one that mattered. But each, he had found, could be answered with a shrug and a word like *warrant*, and he would never know whether he had been told the truth or only not been lied to.

So he asked the narrowest thing he could find.

"Is my family in your letter?"

The fire ticked. In the stones outside the wind rose, and sank.

Ila's eyes stayed on his hands. Her own lay open on her knees, and he watched them as he had watched them for a week, from the angle where he could see all ten nails. They were pink. They stayed pink.

"I'm not going to answer that," said Ila.

It was free. He knew the exact weight of it. He had read her refusal on the Drum, and had told her himself that a refusal on a close question was an answer. He had said it with his own mouth and thought himself clever.

It was an answer. It said that there was something in the letter that touched his family and that she would not say. It could mean a hundred things. It could mean Pritam, and a warrant, and a garrison on the road to Garh. It could mean his father's debts, or his mother's name, or a line in a ledger from before his birth.

He sat with it. The word *family* had Pritam's face on it, because everything did.

"Then I'll take it as it stands," said Kunal. "Not as a yes."

Her head came up, a little. Not to his eyes. To his collar.

"I can't help what I infer," he said. "I'm not a stone. But I'll put it down and not build on it. If I find out otherwise, I'll find out in daylight, from you." He heard the dryness in it and let it stay. "I'd like it noted that this is a considerable feat for a man raised by my uncle."

"Noted," said Ila. Her voice had thinned. "Kunal, it's more than I've earned."

"Probably." He looked at her open hands. "But it's cheaper than the alternative, and I've lately found I'm thrifty."

She made a sound that was nearly a laugh, and nearly something else. She slid down along the wall and lay on her side with her back to him, and the length of the rope lay between them across the blanket. After a while, without turning, she put one hand out behind her, palm up, on the boards.

It asked for nothing. She was only leaving it where he could see it.

He lay down beside it and did not touch it. He looked at it in the red light until the fire went to coals. It was warm and steady, and it was hers, and it did not tell him one thing he wanted to know.

* * *

The third day brought nothing but the count.

He took the petition out again at first light and ruled the margin with the edge of the pencil case, a line for each day. He had started it the night of the binding, thirty-five days to the Meadow Cairn's meltwater in a soft year, with Shilak's thaw council somewhere after, when the crown's men and the hill's would sit down at one table and read what had been written. He had thought of it as a distance. Now he laid it out in strokes and counted them.

Twenty. Twenty strokes, and fifteen empty places.

He had gone into the winter wanting two things, and each had a number on it. The first was that thaw council. The second was the unmapped hills, still his, when it came. He was beginning to understand that the two might not live in the same house.

When he looked up, Ila was at the door with her eye to the crack, one hand flat on the wood. She had been there some time.

"It's easing," she said. "The pitch has changed."

She opened it a hand's width. Light came in white and hard, and the cold with it, and the singing in the stones thinned to a thread and stopped. Beyond the scree the Mirror lay blown bare and shining. The sky had been wiped.

They stood in the door together with their breath going up. Neither spoke, and he found that he did not want to, because he knew what the next hour was for.

In an hour they would walk east along the shore, past the Drum, to the head of the third lake. She would unfold her field sheet and ask him for a bearing, and he would have to say what he had never said aloud to anyone.

He had decided it in the night, somewhere between the hand on the boards and the grey light. It was not a trade, and she had not asked. That was why.

"The way goes in at the head of the third lake," said Kunal.

She turned her head. Her eyes came to his face for the first time in two days and stopped at his chin.

"There's a cleft in the cliff where the ice meets the rock. Hill people call the lake the Eye. It's the last, and it's the deepest, and the cleft is the pupil." He kept his voice level. "That's where the princes' way enters the mountain. I've never been inside it past the first turn."

"Never?"

"My uncle took me to the first turn when I was twelve. He stood me against the rock and turned me round himself, and walked me out." He made himself say the rest. "He said the remainder was for a prince who had earned it. I took that to mean him. I've never been told what it meant."

The cold came in at the door. She said nothing. The pencil was in her fist, and she had not opened her field book.

"I'm telling you because we are going there, and I'd rather you walked in knowing how little I know than how much I'd like you to think I do." He found he had put his hands out, palms up, in the light. He had not meant to. "Past the first turn I've no more than you. It's all true. You can look."

She looked.

He saw her do it. Her eyes went to his hands and then to the sky behind him, and then, at last, to his eyes, full on, and it was like a door opening onto a room with no floor. There was grief in it. He could see that much and no more. He could not tell whose it was, or what for.

"Thank you," said Ila. She said it very low.

She did not write the Eye down. He noticed, and did not know what to make of it. She stood in the white doorway with her pencil shut in her hand, and her eyes went east along the shore to where the third lake waited behind its spur, and then, quickly, back to his hands.
