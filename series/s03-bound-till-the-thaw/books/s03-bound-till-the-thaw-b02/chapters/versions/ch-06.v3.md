# Chapter 6: Whiteout on the Stair

The cloud dropped while Ila was still reading the last cairn.

One moment it was a grey heap of stones on the white, three hundred paces off. The next it was a smudge. Then it was gone, and the plateau had no edges, and the only straight line left in the world was the rope on her wrist.

"Stop," she said.

Kunal stopped. He stood two spans ahead, a dark shape with his collar up. Past him the white went on in every direction, the same white, with no slope in it and no sky.

"How far to the next cairn?"

"Four hundred paces. Perhaps five."

"Which way?"

He turned his head left, then right. Her mouth went dry.

"Straight on," he said. "I think."

"You think."

"I've walked this a hundred times, in summer, behind ewes." He wiped snow from his lashes. "The cairns are old, Ila. The jogini light them for the bound, and only a little, and they're tired. They'll show at dusk if they show at all."

It was close to dusk. The white had started to thicken to grey at its edges.

She took the compass from inside her coat. Brass, palm-wide, the lid dented where she had dropped it on a Taravan stair at nineteen. The needle swung and settled and swung again. There was iron in the rock under the snow. She held the case flat against her ribs until it quit.

"North-east by north," she said. "I've paced nine hundred and twelve since the head of the Stair. Your straight on bends a hand to the left of my needle."

"It's a song, not an instrument."

"There's a lip on the west side of this plateau. I saw it from the Stair." She put the compass away. "If we drift toward it, how far off are we?"

"Far."

"How far is far?"

He didn't answer, and she let it lie. Free, she thought. Not kind.

"Odds," said Kunal. It was her own trick handed back in her own level voice. "Yours first."

"One in three we miss the shelter and spend the night in the open. One in ten we find the lip." She flexed her cold fingers. "I don't like either number."

"Nor I," said Kunal. "Walk behind me. Count loudly."

* * *

She counted. He sang.

It was barely a song. It was a thread of sound under his breath, four lines that went round and round, and it clashed with her numbers the way two clocks clash in a small room.

*Keep the Crow's Wing on your cheek,*
*keep the cold hollow underfoot.*
*Past the stone that split in two,*
*three hundred to where the ewe lay down.*

The fourth time round, she stopped counting.

"Three hundred what?"

His voice broke off. "Sorry?"

"The last line. Three hundred paces, I assume. Yours or mine? Yours are longer by two fingers, I've measured."

He turned. In the grey she could not read his face, but his shoulders went very still. "You weren't meant to catch that."

"You sing it like a lullaby. I count." She pushed on, because standing felt like falling. "The Crow's Wing is a ridge. It's on the left, if it's on your cheek, and I'd wager it's the one we saw from the Stair before the cloud. The cold hollow is a dip the wind has scoured. The split stone is a boulder. And where the ewe lay down, that isn't a place, is it? It's an instruction."

"It's a place," said Kunal. "A half-ring of wall, waist high, where ewes go to lamb out of the wind. It's three hundred paces past the boulder. You'd find it in the dark if you had the song."

"So every name in it is a direction."

He was quiet for a moment. Snow ticked on his shoulders.

"In Neshar," he said, "all of them are."

The size of it settled on her. Two winters of crown surveyors had ridden these valleys asking shepherds what each hill was called. They had been given good, honest answers, and had written them down as names. No one had told them the names were the road. A hundred years of children had learned the whole high country as nonsense, and there was not a sheet of paper in it to steal.

And she had walked in with a pencil.

"That's why you won't say them," she said.

"That's some of why."

It was not much of an answer, but she wrote it down in her head as a true one. The rope had stayed quiet.

* * *

The cloud tore open like a slit sack.

Black rock stood out of the white, a long crag on the left, no more than half a mile off, with four rounded humps along its top like a fist laid on a table. The light lay on it for a count of maybe ten. Ila had the compass out before she knew she had moved. Ninety-four degrees. She said the number aloud and the distance after it, and Kunal repeated them back to her without being asked.

"Knuckle," she said. "It's got a face. A blunt, bossy, entirely unimpressed face." She shut the compass. "Nimmi's Knuckle."

Kunal made a sound that was nearly a laugh. "It has a name."

"Then tell me it."

"No."

She waited for the rest. It did not come. "That's a refusal," she said.

"It's a refusal." He kept his eyes on the crag as the white closed over it. "A name is a way. If I say it and you write it, anyone who can read your sheet can find that crag. And anyone who knows the song can find where you were standing when you looked at it."

"You think I'd hand it to Raghu?"

"I think paper goes where paper goes." He said it without heat. "I'd rather you called it something rude and kept the true one out of ink."

"It isn't rude. It's a compliment. Nimmi would take it as one."

"Nimmi would pretend she hadn't heard it, and then be insufferable for a week."

She laughed then, loud and sudden, and the cloud swallowed the sound. He flinched and turned, and his mouth twitched before he put it right.

"I named a ridge Tamar's Shoulder once," said Ila, still grinning. "After a cook who fed me when I was seven. It's on my summer sheets."

"Of course it is," said Kunal, and walked on with his shoulders a little looser. She filed that where she kept the stair counts and the wall.

* * *

They found the place where the split boulder should have been, and there was no boulder.

Kunal stopped at the edge of a long smooth hump in the snow and stood looking at it for a count of twenty. She studied the back of his neck. She had learned to read it as she read ground: tight, then tighter.

"It stood here," he said. "I've sat on it. The whole of the cut, both halves." He put his boot on the hump and it held, solid as a bank. "The drift's filled it. A winter's snow, off the Crow's Wing, in one night."

"So you've nothing to count from."

"Nothing." He said it quite steadily. "Three hundred past a thing I can't see. If we walk the wrong line from the wrong place, we come out on the lip."

She was not looking at the hump any more. She was looking at the snow beside it, where the wind had scooped a shallow lee and left a seam of old dimples in a line, each filled to a soft grey cup. She went down on one knee and put her bare palm over the nearest.

A heel bar. Nine round heads, blurred nearly smooth.

"Raghu's pairs," she said. "Two files, the same issue nails. They came through here, on our bearing, before the drift closed over the boulder." She pointed along the dimples. "Packed hard, then buried. A day old, or a little more. They didn't wander. They walked this line like men who'd been told it."

"Told by whom?" His voice had lost its steadiness. "Hari kept this stair thirty years and never gave it to a soul."

"I don't know." She stood. "Either they had the song, or they were guided by someone who did, or they guessed very well. I'd not like to bet on the last." She wiped her palm. "And the snow's taken their line as clean as it took your stone. We're the only ones who can walk it now, and they know where it ends."

"Paper goes where paper goes," said Kunal, quietly, to nobody, and she did not answer, because it was not a joke now.

The grey was deepening. She had perhaps half an hour of light, and he knew it too.

"Give me your numbers," said Kunal.

She blinked. "Mine?"

"Everything. The Knuckle's bearing, your paces since, the pitch of the ground. I've walked this by song all my life. You've walked it by sum for three hours, and you've been right every time the cloud tore." He turned to face her. "Take the bearing. I'll hold the rope."

She stood there with the cold biting her face. It was no gift. It was a man handing the lead to the one person who could be wrong in front of him and not lose a village by it. It must cost him a great deal. She put the thought away until she had somewhere to set it down.

"Ninety-four degrees to the Knuckle at five hundred and sixty paces," said Ila. "The wall lies north-east by north, three hundred of your paces past the stone. Let the stone be somewhere in this hump. Two hundred and seventy of mine." She drew a breath. "I make it nine in ten."

"Then walk."

She walked. He came behind, paying out rope, a hand's width at a time.

Snow squeaked underfoot. She counted aloud, and the numbers went out into the white and did not come back. At two hundred and forty, the surface began to boom.

The sound came up through her boots before her ears caught it, a flat hollow note, like a knock on a cupboard. She had half a breath to know what it was.

The slab went.

It dropped out from under her with a crack she felt in her teeth, and she went down into the white to her ribs, then to her armpits, her boots kicking at nothing. Her field book slid from her coat. Her hands clawed the crust and the crust came away in plates.

The rope hit its end.

It hit hard, a wrenching jerk under her arms that squeezed the breath out of her. Then it stopped. She hung in the hole with her elbows on a ledge of broken snow and a black throat of rock under her boots. Above her came a grunt, a boot skidding on ice, and a long, low swear in the hill dialect.

"Don't move," said Kunal.

"I wasn't going to."

"I have you." He was sitting. She could see him now, six spans off, heels dug in, with the rope round his hip and his back against the slope. He had taken her whole weight on his body with no anchor. "I have you, I'm not letting go. Kick the wall. Find a hold."

She kicked. Her toe found a knob of rock. She pushed, and he hauled, and the two things happened together, and then she was out on her stomach on the crust, dragging in air in great ragged pulls.

For a moment neither spoke.

"Where," said Ila, from the snow, "did you learn that?"

"Goats."

"*Goats.*"

"A kid goes down a crevice every spring." He was flexing his hands. They were bleeding a little at the palms. "You sit, you brace on your hip and you swear. It works on surveyors."

She laughed. It came up out of her loud and cracked and stupid, flat on her face in the snow. It rang off the cloud. He looked at her as if she had rung the Garh Neshar bell at a funeral, and then he began to laugh too, helplessly, with his hands held out from his sides.

"I've never been on that end of it," she said at last, when she could. "Of a rope."

"I know. You hold like a woman who's never expected to be held."

"I expected to be careful."

"Yes," said Kunal. "It shows."

She sat up. The book was gone, down the hole. She looked at the black throat of rock, and a cold went through her that had nothing to do with weather. Then she crawled to the lip, put her arm in to the shoulder, and found the book wedged on a ledge. She came up with it in her fist, and it was dry.

"That makes you five," said Kunal, looking at her. "Held four, been held once."

"Once is plenty." She tucked the book away. "My sums saved you. Your hip saved me. I'd call that level."

"I'd call that a debt," said Kunal, "but I'll take level."

* * *

They reached the cairn at full dark, and she would have missed it.

It was knee-high and grey, a few flat stones heaped by someone who had not expected to be thanked. Beyond it stood a half-ring of dry wall, waist high, where the snow had not drifted. The ewes' bed. Forty paces past her line.

"Forty," said Ila. "Yours or mine?"

"Mine."

"Then I was thirty-odd out. I'll take that."

Kunal put his hand on the top stone, and the cairn began to hum.

It was low at first, a note she felt in the roots of her teeth. Then it rose and held, and a faint blue thread uncoiled from the stones and ran off over the plateau, thin as hair, picking out a line across the dark. The cold went on, and the wind went on, but the world had a path in it again.

She had not known how badly she wanted a line to follow. She stood looking at it for longer than she meant to.

"The last of the high cairns," said Kunal. "Ewe's Rest is a short way below. I won't take that descent in the dark." He nodded at the wall. "We'll sleep in the lee."

They ate sattu, standing, then sitting, with their backs against cold stone and the rope lying slack between them. The blue thread glowed on. Ila took out her field book and found her pencil unbroken.

"I'd like to name my price," said Kunal.

She looked up. He sat with his bleeding hands turned up on his knees, as if he had not noticed them, and he was not smiling.

"You said I'd earn them," she said. "I said price first. Name it."

"Each night I give you one true Neshar name. For a feature you've measured that day, and only that, and in our own word. You write it as I say it." He paused. "In return you answer one question about your orders. True. Or you refuse. Refusal is free."

"And my questions to you?"

"Ask what you like. You always have." He shook his head. "This is the other half. You told me the true road is the only kind you draw. I'm asking you for a true answer, in exchange for the names I've kept out of ink all my life."

She turned it over. It was a bargain, and a clean one, and she distrusted how clean it was. He gave a word. She gave a fact about the queen. She would write down his name for the world, and he would learn her orders a line at a time.

"It's uneven," said Ila.

"Yes."

"Your word is a key. My answer is a sentence."

"A sentence from the queen's own surveyor." He met her eyes. "I think we may be even."

"Names go on the sheet exactly as you say them."

"Exactly."

"I may refuse. Free. Every night."

"Every night." A corner of his mouth moved. "I'd be a poor student of the rope if I tried to take that from you."

She considered the blue thread running off into the dark. The wind had dropped. The stones hummed under her palm, low and quiet.

"There was a spire," she said. "When the cloud tore at dusk, north of the wall. A black pinnacle with a notch in it. I took it at three-fifty, about a mile and a quarter."

He didn't look. He knew where it stood.

"Old Woman's Tooth," said Kunal. "There's water under it in spring."

She wrote it. She wrote the name in his own hill script, because he told her how, and beside it she put the bearing and the distance. Then she added, in her small square hand, *water under, spring*, and stopped with the pencil lifted.

A name, and a place a column might camp, and the season it would be safe to. He had given her more than he needed to, and he had watched her write all of it.

"You didn't have to give me the spring," she said.

"It's part of the name. You'd not understand the name without it." He rubbed his hands together and winced. "My turn."

The wind came back, a little, over the wall.

"Does your commission say garrisons?"

It was a plain sentence. He said it quietly, without the courtly lift, and his eyes were on her face and not her hands. He had been carrying that question since Garh Neshar, she understood. He had carried it up the Stair and across the white and had put it down here at last.

She knew what the answer was. She had read the paper a dozen times since the summer post, and once more by lamp in the guest-house, and she knew every word. She could have said a kind thing, and the rope would have shown it. She could have said nothing, and it would have cost her nothing at all.

"It says the crown may hold every way," said Ila. "That's the phrase. I don't know what holding means to them."

Her fingers lay on her knee in the blue light. She kept her eyes on his face.

Kunal looked at her hands instead. They were pink and ordinary, and he looked at them a long while, and the stones went on humming, low and level and true.

"Every way," he said.

"Yes."

"That's not no."

"It's not yes either. It's what it says." She closed the field book on the Old Woman's Tooth. "I'll answer anything else I know. I won't make up what I don't."

He said nothing. He sat against the wall with the rope across his knees and the light on his bloodied palms, and she saw him take the words and try to weigh them, and find that they did not weigh on either side.

Her hands stayed warm. She was not sure that made it better.
