# Chapter 5: The Burned Hut

The smell reached Kunal before the hut did.

It was not fresh smoke. It was the wet, sour smell of a fire that had burned itself out days ago and been rained on by snow, and he knew it from a long way down the salt road. He knew it the way a man knows his own name said wrong.

"Stop," he said.

Ila stopped. The rope between their wrists went slack, eight spans of Lata's wool lying across the scree like a dropped thread. She had been counting under her breath since the second spur. Her lips were still moving.

"Four hundred and ten," she said. "You've interrupted me."

"Forgive me. There's a hut ahead that shouldn't smell like that."

She looked where he looked. The road had carried them over two bare spurs and down into a hollow under a wall of grey rock. Above the wall, a white staircase of ice and snow climbed out of sight into low cloud. The Shepherd's Stair. He had walked it as a boy with his hand in a goat's wool. Beneath it, where the road gave out, there should have been a low stone hut with a turf roof and a thread of smoke from Hari's chimney.

There was a black square in the snow.

He went down faster than the rope liked. She followed without comment, which he noted, and was grateful for, and had no room to be grateful about.

The roof had fallen in. The walls stood shoulder high, blackened, and the doorframe was a charred hoop around nothing. Snow had drifted into the room, melted on warm ash, and frozen into a grey crust. Hari's bench was there. Hari's iron pot lay on its side. Hari's goat-hair blanket was a rag stuck to the floor.

No body. He made himself look twice and saw none, and his knees nearly let go with relief.

"He's not here," he said, and heard how thin it sounded. "Hari has kept this stair thirty winters. His father kept it. He would not leave it for anything short of—" He stopped. "He's not here."

"Then he got out," said Ila, "or was taken."

"Yes."

She said it as a fact, neither kind nor unkind, and it steadied him more than comfort would have.

There were prints in the ash. Many, overlapping, pressed deep through the crust into the soft black beneath. Hobnailed boots, the sort that cost a month's grain. And half trodden into the char by the doorframe lay a small brass bell, the size of a walnut, with a loop for stitching.

He picked it up. The tongue was gone. It was a patrol bell, off a regent's grey wool shoulder. His uncle's men wore them so the whole valley would hear them coming and know whose they were.

*Pritam's still,* he thought. *He took the habit with him, and the bells.*

He closed his fist round the brass until it bit.

Hari had been burned out for a stair. Kunal knew how it would go from here. Raghu would climb the Shepherd's Stair and learn what lay at the top. After that the high hamlets would have a man at their gates, asking for what they could not give, and then asking again with a torch. The Stair was not a road. It was a door, and the people beyond it had kept it shut for three hundred years because the hill had no other wall. If a captain's pairs went through and the crown learned the way after them, Neshar would stop being a valley. It would be a road with a toll.

And he had a surveyor on his wrist. He looked at her.

Ila was not looking at the bell. She crouched at the edge of the ash with her mitten off, her bare hand flat above the prints without touching them, her lips moving again.

"Two roped pairs," she said.

He let it pass. She did not seem to need him to answer.

"The prints come in two files. Each file has a gap of seven or eight spans between the same two men. That's a rope, tied and kept slack." She pointed. "Two bound pairs. Four men. And look at the soles. The same ring of nine round nail-heads and a bar across the heel, in all four. Issue boots, cut in one store. Four men from one company, not four strangers who happened to climb together."

"Ila—"

"They're loaded." She tapped the print nearest the door. "That heel went in a thumb past the crust, and the toe is nearly flat. A man with a pack that heavy walks flat-footed. Salt weight, or iron, or rope. They stood here a while. They ate, there's grease on the stone. Then they went up." She looked at the white staircase. "The toes dig in, the stride shortens."

"How long ago?"

"The ash has crusted but not drifted. Snow's lain on the roof beams a day, not two. A day ahead, perhaps a little over."

He stood holding the bell.

She was right. He could see she was right, the way he could see a rockfall after someone had pointed to it. He had come down here braced for the shape of a disaster. He had read the ground as a wound. She had read it as a column of figures, and the figures were better.

In thirty-three winters of knowing every stone of this hill, it had not occurred to him to count the men who had burned it.

"Four," he said slowly. "Two pairs."

"Heavily loaded, a day ahead, going up." She wiped her hand on her coat. "Did you not see it?"

"I saw Hari's pot."

It was the most honest thing he had said to her all morning, and he watched her take it in. She did not use it against him. She nodded once, like a woman logging a bench mark. "Then it's as well one of us was looking at the ground."

* * *

They ate standing, because the hut had nothing to sit on. Sattu and cold salt tea from her flask. The wind came down the Stair and rattled the burned beams.

"May I ask you something?" said Ila.

"You may ask me anything. It's the answering that costs you."

"I'm aware." She wiped her mouth. "Is Captain Raghu going up to get Pritam out?"

It was the question he would have asked. His uncle sat under guard in his own tower. A man who loved him, or owed him, might well want a road to carry him over the mountain before spring.

"I don't know," said Kunal. "Freeing him may be part of it. I can't tell you it's the whole."

Her eyes dropped to his hands. They were pink. He saw her see it.

"That's true," she said. "You don't know."

"I know a good deal less than I'd like." He turned the cup in his fingers. "Raghu wants a way over the mountain for something. That much I'd stake the walnut on. What the something is..." He stopped.

He had a sentence ready, and he could feel its weight in his mouth, and the sentence had the word *fear* in it. It was not a lie. It was a thing he was afraid of, particular and specific, and it had lived in him since the fourth light went out. Said aloud to a crown surveyor, it would be the first line of a map he had spent his life hiding. A true thing, once spoken, could not be unsaid. He had watched his uncle build a coup out of true things said at the wrong table.

"I won't say," said Kunal.

"What won't you say?"

"What I think he wants it for. Or what I'm afraid of, if you'd rather." He made himself meet her eyes. "I'm not telling you it's nothing. I'm telling you I won't answer."

The rope stayed quiet. Silence cost nothing. He had known it would, and he had still half expected the cold to come for him.

Ila took out her field book and wrote. He could see the page upside down, her small square hand, and made out two words before she turned it.

*Refused. Free.*

"You're writing it down," he said.

"I'm writing everything down. Every true answer becomes a line on my sheet. I don't see why a refusal shouldn't be a line too. A blank is data, Highness. A good surveyor learns more from the fog than from the clear."

"And what does the fog tell you?"

"That you're afraid." She closed the book. "Not of me, I think. For whoever lives above this stair. Not for a chair in the Garh."

He had braced for a guess at his ambition. He had not braced for this. It went in under the ribs, and he felt his face give it away before he could stop it. She saw that too. She did not write it down.

"I'd like to say I was sorry I saw it," said Ila.

"Are you?"

"I'm trying to find out," she said, which was his own sentence, and he nearly laughed, and nearly didn't.

* * *

The Stair began in a gully so steep that the first rung was a ledge of rock at his chin.

Before they started she untied his wrist and retied the rope round his chest in a loop under the arms, snug, so a fall would not wrench a hand. Then she stood beside him with the pencil behind her ear and her eyes on the ice, working out the same thing he was: how long a thing could stay frozen when a regent's men had trodden it for a day.

Shepherds had cut the lower steps over a hundred years into broad shallow treads, each the length of a boot. In summer they were wet rock. In winter they were glass with a skin of snow, and the skin had cracked.

He put his weight on the first one and felt it give like rotten bread.

"No," he said, and took it back. "Rotten. Heavy men, heavy packs, and the meltwater's gone through."

"Odds?" said Ila.

He glanced at her. She had her chin up and her rope-hand steady. It was the first time anyone had asked him for odds in the voice of someone who meant to use them.

"The first dozen are poor. Perhaps one in three that a tread shears under me. After that the ice is thicker, in the shade of the gully." He tried a second tread. It held, with a noise like a knuckle. "I'd rather it was me than you."

"Then you lead and I belay." She was already uncoiling slack. She put her back to the rock and took a turn of rope round her hip with a speed that told him she had done it on worse. "Don't pull unless you mean it."

"Yes, Surveyor."

"Don't say it like that. As if it's a joke." She said it very quietly. "I've held three men on a rope in my life and I didn't let go of any of them."

He had no reply. He turned to the ice.

The first tread shed a plate the size of a tray, which went skating down the gully behind him and out into the thin afternoon light. The second held. The third gave and held, and he took the next on his knees, spreading his weight. The rope ran easy behind him. He did not look back. He did not need to.

At the sixth tread his glove found iron. A shepherd's peg, black with rust, driven into a crack in the rock a hundred years ago by someone who had also not trusted this ice. He tapped it. It rang. He passed the rope behind it and went on, and the rope ran back through the old iron toward her hands.

At the ninth tread the ice simply let go.

There was no crack, only a lurch, and the gully wall went past his face. He fell the length of two treads. Then the rope snapped taut round the peg and came up hard under his arms and stopped him with a jerk that emptied his lungs. He hung with his boots scrabbling over nothing.

Below him, no sound. Then a scrape, a boot skidding on ice, and a grunt through her teeth.

He looked down. Ila had gone to one knee, her back against the rock and the rope bitten into her hip. The peg had caught most of him, and she had taken the rest. Her face was white with the effort. Her boots had slid a hand's width and stopped.

One breath. Two. The rope did not give an inch.

"Up," said Ila, low and level. "Find a hold. I can't do this all day."

He found one. His fingers closed on a ledge of rock, and the weight came off her slowly, and the rope went slack. When he glanced down she was flexing her hip with a look of outrage.

"That's four," she said.

"Four?"

"Men I've held." She wiped her face with the back of her mitten. "Don't make a habit of it."

He climbed on. At each good ledge he called "Holding," though he was holding perfectly well, and each time her voice came back up the gully, short and steady. "Holding." He told himself he was checking the rope. He called it again at the next ledge anyway.

He counted as he went, by the old family habit, because the Stair's lower treads had always been forty to the men of his house. "Thirty-eight. Thirty-nine." He hauled himself over the lip of the next ledge. Above it a short wall of dark rock stood between them and the long upper slope. "Forty. That's the foot of the Stair done. Bring the rope up."

"Forty-one," said Ila below him.

"I'm sorry?"

"There's a half-step under the first overhang, a low shelf, about a hand high. You stepped over it without weighting it. I counted it because my foot was on it." Her voice rose to him, calm as a ruled line. "Forty-one, not forty."

He looked down. She was a small figure against the grey ice with the rope bright between them, her head tilted back, wearing the expression of a woman with no intention of being wrong.

He thought of the tower stair at Garh Neshar, and the half-step on its north landing that no one counted, so that his family said thirty-nine and taught the children it was the ghosts' count. *A hand high. Stepped over.*

"It's an old shelf," he said. "Nobody counts it. My grandfather said a man who counts the half-step is a man who plans to fall."

"Your grandfather wasn't belaying," said Ila. "Forty-one."

"Forty-one," said Kunal, and it came out of him like a surrender. He had spent a life letting the hill's numbers stay a little wrong, because wrong numbers were a kind of lock. He looked up at the wall, thirty feet of rock with a crack up the middle and a line of holds he had always taken on his left.

"The next pitch is yours."

Her head came up. "Mine?"

"You've the better count. And I've just shown you what I do to ice." He braced his boots and took up her rope in both hands. "Go on, Surveyor. I'll hold."

He watched her think about it, and decide it was not a trap, and that if it was the rope would tell her. Then she crossed the ledge, put her fingers into the crack, and climbed his stair.

He fed her rope as if it were thread, a hand's width at a time, never a coil too much. Halfway up she looked down. "You can give me slack. I won't fall."

"I know," said Kunal, and paid out an inch.

* * *

The head of the Stair was a wide shelf of rock under the lip of the cloud, bare in the wind, with the ice below blue as a vein. The upper snowfield ran away white into the murk.

At the edge of the shelf stood a flat grey stone, waist-high, set upright in a socket of smaller ones. It was a route-keeper's marker, the kind that stood at the head of every hill stair. He had walked past it a hundred times.

It had writing on it.

The chalk was fresh. The wind had not yet scoured the strokes. Someone had drawn them carefully in the old hill script, slow letters with hooked tails, the hand of a man taught to write by his father with a stick in the dirt. Kunal knew the hand. He knew the pot that had held that chalk.

Ila came up beside him, breathing hard and pleased with herself. She saw his face, and stopped being pleased.

"It's in hill script," she said. "I can read the numerals. Not the rest." She held out her bare hand, and for a moment he thought she meant to take his. She pointed at the stone instead. "Read it to me."

He opened his mouth to say it was nothing. A route-keeper's warning, weather, a note to the next shepherd. He had a dozen smooth answers. His eyes went to the letters first, and his mouth followed them before he had chosen anything.

"*They ask for the princes' way,*" said Kunal.

The words went out over the shelf and across the ice, and he could not take one of them back.

The wind dropped. In the stillness the stones under their boots gave a low hum that sank half a note, like a bowl's song when a wet finger leaves the rim, and Ila's hand had gone quite still in the air beside his sleeve.

"Hari wrote that," he said. "Before they burned him out. Or while they did."

"Who are *the princes*?" said Ila.

Kunal said nothing. For a moment he could not have said whether it was refusal or whether he simply had no voice. There was a chalk line under the sentence, a short flat stroke, and beneath it a second line begun and abandoned, a single hooked letter, and a smear where a hand had dragged through it.
