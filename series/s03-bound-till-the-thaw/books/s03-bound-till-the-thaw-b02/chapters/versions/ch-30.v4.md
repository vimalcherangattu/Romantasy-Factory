# Chapter 30: Two Hands

The light came up the crawl on its knees.

Kunal was on his feet before he had decided anything. He put himself between the passage mouth and Ila, and he got his knife out with the flat of his palm, because his fingertips could no longer be trusted to close on a hilt.

Raghu came out first, stooped under the rock, a lantern low in his left hand and a long knife in his right. His beard was stiff with ice. Behind him came Pasang, the brass bell on his shoulder dull with wet, and between their wrists the grey rope ran taut as a plumb line.

"Highness." Raghu straightened and let his eyes go round the vault: the door, the shelf of rotted gloves, the fourteen marks. They went greedy, then flat. "So that was what the nine holes were for."

"You followed the lamp," said Ila, from behind Kunal's shoulder.

"I followed a prince with a bad hand and a woman on a stick. A child could have done it." The knife swung until it pointed at her coat. "Two things. The sheets you offered me at dusk, and a way out that isn't the way I came. I've no wish to crawl through the Drum water again."

"You never wanted the sheets," Kunal said. "You wanted me on the ice, standing where I was put. Dhanu was dead before you wrote the note."

"Dhanu is at home with his goats."

Kunal looked past the blade to the boy. Pasang was twenty, with a soldier's cropped hair and a child's mouth, and he was staring at the carved door rather than at anyone's face.

"Pasang. You're roped to him by the jogini's own rule. He can lie to me for nothing. He can lie to my keeper's widow, to the whole of Neshar, for nothing. He can't lie to you." Kunal heard how it sounded, a blade picked up and turned. He had used the voice on men he meant to bleed. This time the blade was only true. "So ask him yourself. Not me. Ask whether the limping man went home. If he's nothing to hide, he'll say yes. If he says nothing, that answers you as well."

"A cornered prince's trick," said Raghu.

Pasang's mouth worked. He turned his head slowly, the way a man turns toward a sound he does not want to place. "Captain," he said. "Did he go home?"

Raghu knew the rule. Kunal watched him know it. Silence was free, and silence would tell the boy everything.

"He went home," said Raghu. "Walking. Before the moon was down."

The knife hand stayed steady, and the fingertips around the hilt went the colour of tallow. Not deep. The boy already half knew, so the lie bought little and cost little. It was white enough.

Pasang looked at the hand. Then, slowly, at his own.

"He did that on the shore," said Ila. Her voice was dry and exact, the voice she used for distances. "When he said it loud, with his hand on his sword. I saw the tips whiten from the prince's arms and told him, and he couldn't read it. You were two paces away. You weren't looking at the hand. You were looking at what lay by the peg." She did not raise her voice. "Dhanu died the first night. His wife laid stones over him at the fold. You held a rope for a dead man."

"I thought he was sleeping," said Pasang. It came out of him small. Then, louder: "You said the keepers go free. Every one, when we had the sheets. On the ice, to my face."

Raghu said nothing. His mouth stayed shut. The knife stayed in his fist, and the tips around the hilt stayed tallow through the whole long silence, though no new lie had been told. He did not look at the boy.

"Captain." Pasang's voice cracked. "They go free. Say it."

Raghu looked at the door. He did not speak. Kunal watched the boy understand what the silence told him.

Pasang sat down. He did it slowly, on the cold floor, the rope across his knees and the bell ringing once against stone.

"Get up," said Raghu.

"No."

"Get up, or by the mountain—" He hauled. Pasang did not move. Raghu's boots slid a step, two, to the end of the grey rope, and Kunal saw the cold take him: his breath smoked, his shoulders drew in. He could not go on toward the door, or back to the crawl, without the boy. He could not cross the length of a rope alone. "Cut it."

"Cutting ends nothing," said Kunal. "You know that. Every child in the Kuhl knows that."

Raghu looked once at the door, and once at the pale seam of Ila's coat, where the folded rubbing showed through the cloth. Then he threw himself at her, because it was the only direction left to him.

It was not far. Kunal had spent all winter watching men lunge on ice. He stepped in under the knife arm, caught the wrist with the heel of his dead hand, and drove it against the cairn-work with his forearm across the elbow. The blade rang on the floor. Ila's crutch hooked it away into the dark. Raghu sagged, gasping, his white fingers clutching at nothing, and Kunal held him there against the stone until the fight left him.

"Pasang," he said, without turning. "I can't free a keeper from under a mountain. I won't promise what I can't do. I can walk you into daylight, with your life. I can tell the council you put the rope down in this hole. And I'll ask you where they're held, and write your name under the answer."

The boy was quiet a long moment.

"That's more than he gave me," he said, and stood.

* * *

Beyond the door there was a stair.

It was no more than a slope of laid stones, climbing, with blue seams along the left wall and a crack in the roof where meltwater ticked down in the lamplight. Kunal went first with Ila's hand on his shoulder, the rubbing safe in her coat. Raghu came behind with his own belt around his wrists, Pasang at the end of the rope, and nobody spoke for a long time.

The stair ended at a gap between two slabs. Light came through it, grey and thin. Kunal put his shoulder to the slab and it moved. He came up on his knees into cold air, and a ring of flat stones stood round him, chest high, a hum rising from every one.

It was dawn at the Shilak Cairn. Below it the roofs of Kothi Shilak lay under a skin of snow, and goat bells were going somewhere in the village.

Four wardens stood inside the ring, in grey, and in front of them, one hand flat on the cairn, stood a tall man Kunal knew from a drafting table and a guest-house in midwinter. Rohan had a beard of three days. He had plainly stood this watch more than once.

For three nights the Shilak Cairn had hummed from under the ground with no one inside the ring. Gopal had sent for Rohan, and Rohan had stood the dawn watch ever since, waiting to see who the mountain would give back.

"Captain," said Kunal, with his hands where they could be seen.

"Prince." Rohan's eyes went over him, over Raghu, over Pasang on the rope's end, and stopped on his sister coming up through the earth on a juniper crutch. "Gopal. Take that man. Hold the boy at the rope's length, and no closer. He walks with us as a witness."

They did it quickly. Raghu said nothing. When he went by, Kunal saw that his fingertips were still white.

Ila crossed the snow without a word. She stood in front of her brother, and she did not tell him what she had found. Kunal saw her choose it. Her free hand went to the back of his collar, where the queen's seal had once been sewn into the coat, and Rohan reached up and covered it with his own. He looked down at her foot. He looked up at her face. Neither of them spoke. She laid her forehead against his shoulder for the count of three, and then she stepped back and was a surveyor again.

Rohan nodded once at Kunal. It was a clerk's nod over a line that balanced. Kunal gave it back.

* * *

The salt house was warm.

It was the first warmth that was not the lamp and a stranger's embers. Bindu's people had lit the chulha in the top room, and the heat came off the clay and went into the beams, and the whole room smelled of salt dust and wool and apricot smoke. Someone had left a basin of hot water by the hearth. Ila's feet were out of her boots and wrapped in clean linen, and she sat on the sleeping shelf with her back to the wall and the grey rope lying eight spans slack across the boards.

Kunal sat on the floor facing her, because he did not trust himself higher.

Her coat lay folded beside her. Her hand went to it before she spoke, and rested on the stiff fold inside, the rubbing with its torn edge and its worn last word that neither of them could read. She did not take it out. She let her hand lie there a moment, checking that it had come up the mountain with her. Then she took the hand away and turned both palms up.

"Look at my hands," said Ila.

He did. They lay open on her knees in the lamplight, flushed to the nail, with the knuckles scraped from the crawl.

"I like you," she said.

"Is that all?"

"It's what I can measure to the hair." She did not smile. "You carry my pencil leads against your chest so they won't snap. You said *forty-one* out loud on the Stair, and you didn't argue the half-step. You make me laugh when I'm furious, which no one has managed since I was nine. I like you, Kunal." She turned her hands over. "On the Neshar road, the first night, I stood eight spans and a bit from you, and I let the rope chill my wrists to the elbow before I'd follow you east. Look at them now."

They were warm. He could see it. The flush of blood in them was easy and slow, as if they had never been anything else.

"I love you," she said.

The rope did not climb. It lay along the boards and gave a low, even note, the sound of a string drawn to its length, and he felt it come up through his wrist and settle in his chest.

"You said it in the dark," said Ila. "I wanted to say it where you could see my hands."

"I didn't need to see them."

"I know. That's why I wanted you to." She drew breath, and he saw the figures go behind her eyes, the way they did when she committed something to a sheet. "There's a third thing, and it's a promise. I'll give Neshar the same map I give the queen. The same lines. The same names. The same hour."

The note in the rope held. It did not rise.

"The Meadow Cairn runs in a few days," said Kunal. His voice was not as steady as he wanted. "After that nothing makes you keep it. Nothing makes either of us keep anything."

"I know the date. I've kept the thaw dates eleven winters." She looked at him over the rope. "I'm not saying it for the mountain."

He had nothing clever to say. He said the plain thing. "Then you'll have the names. All of them, in the hill words. Even the ones that make a fool of me."

The rope hummed once more, even and soft.

* * *

He crossed the boards on his knees and she met him halfway, and neither of them let the other go for some time.

She was thirty-one and he was thirty-three, and for a month of days they had been tied together in every way but this. Her mouth tasted of salt tea and apricot. He had kissed women in courts, with calculation. Now he could not calculate anything. When she put her hands into his hair and her bandaged feet came up off the floor, he stopped, because he had seen how she walked.

"Your feet."

"Are on a folded blanket. Put it there." She was laughing, breathless. "Kunal. I'll tell you if anything hurts. I'll tell you the size of the hurt, in spans."

He built her a nest out of the pattu and the blanket, so that her heels lay clear of the boards, and he undid her shirt with his palms because his fingers could not feel the buttons. She saw it. She took his right hand in hers, with its dead grey tips, and laid it against her breast.

"Can you feel that?"

"The heat. Not the shape."

"Then use your mouth," said Ila, "and I'll tell you the shape."

She did. It was a precise, unhurried instruction, given in the measured voice she used on the Stair, and it undid him faster than any caress. She told him where to press and where to wait. When he drew her breast into his mouth she said *there* and then said nothing at all. His palm went down her ribs, over the white scar, over her hip. She gasped when he found her with the flat of his thumb, and she took his wrist and held it where she wanted it.

She turned them herself, with her feet off the floor. She came down over him slowly, her knees on the blanket and her hands braced on his chest, and when she took him in they both went still. He felt the heat of her through every inch of his body that could feel anything. His hands held her hips, heel and palm, steadying her.

"Is it—"

"Don't ask me." Her forehead came down to his. "Look at my hands."

They were braced on his shoulders, warm as bread. He watched them while she moved. He watched them while she shook, and cried out against his neck, and went on moving until he lost the thread of his own name. Then he held her so tight she laughed, and afterwards she lay along him with her ruined foot hooked over his shin and her cheek on his heart, and the chulha ticked.

"After the thaw," she said, after a long time. "Where do you sleep?"

"Garh Neshar. The tower, thirty-nine steps. Forty, you'll say."

"Forty. And in spring?"

"At Ewe's Rest, for the lambing. The north wall needs checking." He laughed, quietly, into her hair. "You could check it."

"I'd want a new pace." She was half asleep. "Mine's gone wrong. I'll have to measure myself again."

He had never in his life heard anyone talk about the thaw as a place a person might live. The rope lay across the blanket, humming almost too low to hear, and he did not look at it.

* * *

The knock was two knuckles, patient.

Kunal woke with the sun on the boards and Ila's hair across his mouth. The door opened a hand's breadth. Meera stood in it, in her guide's brown with the new licence cord at her belt, the post's brown seal and the council's blue hanging on the cord, and a pardon wax beside them with a thread of red in it. She had the count under her right arm. Her left hand, in its felted glove cut for a thumb and two fingers, rested on the latch.

She looked at the two of them under the one blanket, at the grey rope across the floor, at Ila's bandaged feet, and she did not remark on any of it.

"Day thirty-two," said Meera. "Your meadow's running soon."
