# Chapter 1: The North Wall

Ila had never been wrong about a distance she had walked herself, so when the tower came up short she walked it again.

Two and a half spans to a pace. She had checked that stride against the brass rule at Taravan when she was nineteen, and against a chalk line her father laid every spring after. The brass had drifted twice. Her legs had not.

The yard of Garh Neshar lay grey and trampled under a sky the colour of old pewter. It was the eve of the Snow Fair, the thirty-first winter of Queen Sarala's reign. Down on the river meadow someone was testing a drum, one slow boom at a time, and the smell of roasting barley had begun to climb the hill. At the yard's far end stood the tower where Regent Pritam sat under guard, square and ugly, and lying to her.

She stopped at the north-west corner and turned to face the wall. Outside, the north face ran twenty-two paces. Inside, that afternoon, with the hill-council captain tapping his boot in the doorway, she had paced the long room behind it and taken off the wall's thickness, which the window cut showed plainly. The room should have run a full pace longer than it did.

A pace was two and a half spans. She allowed half a span for a drunk mason and sloppy plaster. That left two.

"Kiran," she said. "Chain."

He was sitting on the mounting block with the brass chain across his knees, chewing a strip of dried apricot. He was twenty-five now and had stopped looking like a man who expected to be arrested. "I did chain it, Ila-ji. Twice. It said what you said."

"Then do it a third time. I like the answer better when I've made someone else do the work."

He got up, grumbling, and walked the end of the chain along the foot of the wall. The links rang one against another, cold and bright. At the corner he held his end to the stone and she read the last link against her mark.

Twenty-two paces, outside. She wrote it down. Then she opened her field book to the page and wrote under it, in the cramped hand she used for things that mattered: *North wall, two spans out of true, void?*

She looked at the question mark for a moment, because she did not like question marks. Then she left it. A question mark was honest. A guess dressed as a measurement was not.

"Void," said Kiran, reading over her arm. "That's where he keeps it."

"Keeps what?"

"His conscience. Two spans is about right. Tight little room, nobody visits." He grinned. The grin was quick and slightly too bright, the way it always was when he had decided in advance that nothing was serious.

Ila snorted. She tucked the pencil behind her ear and looked up at the tower, and for no good reason said the thing she had been carrying around for a month.

"Rohan wrote that the stones at the Shilak Cairn spoke at the thaw. Everyone there felt it in their hands. The Old Bargain is unpaid, and the pass closes for good after the fourth winter unless somebody pays. Now the crown's commission wants every way across mapped, and I'd like to know whether a map of a pass that is going to close is a map or an obituary."

Kiran's grin did not fall. It simply stopped being used. He said nothing at all, and his hand rose to his left ear, to the torn rim, and rubbed it once with two fingers.

He did that when the weather turned, and when the talk did.

"Kiran?"

"Cold," he said. "It aches when it's about to snow." He bent and began to wind the chain onto its frame, link over link, very carefully. "Come on. The light's going. You'll want to eat before you start measuring the kitchen."

She let it be. Silence was not an answer, but it was a measurement of a kind, and she wrote it down in her head with a mark beside it. His sister had tied a rope on Ila's brother in the spring. By summer Kiran would be some sort of brother to her too, and she had no intention of prying him open in a yard.

* * *

They went out by the main gate, where the notice was.

It was nailed to the left post, the new law in eleven lines on good rag paper, with two seals pressed under it in two colours of wax: the Shilak post's brown, the hill council's blue. Ila knew the eleven lines by heart. She had stood beside her brother while he cut the last of them down from twenty-six. *Licences carry two seals. Medicine always passes. No person is hanged or held to blame for a death on a bound crossing.* Somebody had already scratched a small, careful goat into the margin beside the third line. The clerks had not scraped it off.

The road below the gate was busier than a winter road should have been. Two pairs of crossers were coming down off the Neshar road, each pair tied wrist to wrist with a short grey rope, each carrying a roll of oilcloth strapped flat to the back. A clerk in a blue sleeve stopped each pair, read the paper they held out, and compared the seals with an expression of deep suspicion. Redbark. She could smell it, a dry bitter smell like the inside of a medicine chest. A year ago it had come over the pass in the dark with a rope round someone's neck.

"They're allowed to smile," she told the second pair, who were looking at the clerk as if he were a hanging judge.

The man did, uncertainly. The woman tugged the rope, and they went on.

"Hill-council men on the grain," Kiran said, nodding along the lane. Four of them stood in the lee of the long barn, in undyed wool, leaning on spears they clearly did not enjoy. The barn doors had a new bar and a new lock. The old lock hung on a nail beside it, so everyone could see it had been replaced. "Pritam's grain. They count it every morning and put the number on a slate. Chetu's boy told me yesterday it's gone up by a sack since the last count."

"Up?"

"Somebody gave it back." He shrugged. "Or somebody's lying about the slate."

"Slates don't lie. People who write on them do." She meant it as a joke and it came out as a rule. She frowned.

Beyond the barn the lane dropped toward the guest-house, and the drum on the meadow boomed again, closer now. Kiran kicked at a frozen rut.

"They say Varun went down to Taravan in chains in the summer," he said, not looking at her. "Is that true? Rohan's letters. Did he say?"

"He said Varun cried at the gate," said Ila. "Rohan's never said what he felt about it, but he mentioned the crying twice." She considered. "Once in each letter. He's not subtle."

Kiran laughed, and the laugh was real this time, and they walked the rest of the way in a better temper.

* * *

The guest-house kitchen had been given to her in the first week of snow, with the cook's blessing and the cook's own stool. Ila lit two lamps instead of one and spread the sheets across the table.

She did not need to unroll them. She knew what was on them. But she liked to see them under light, the way some people like to count coins.

Twelve sheets, fixed at the corners with river stones. The summer passes lay across them in her finest ink: every col, every scree-slope, every saddle that a laden goat could manage, with contours so close that the paper looked grey in places. The commission had come up with the summer post, sealed in the queen's wax, and it was short. *Map every winter way.* Nothing else. Two words more than any chief surveyor had written in forty years.

She had drawn what she could reach without being killed. Of the winter ways she had nothing. A pair could cross a closed pass, and the stones would light the line for them, and nobody had ever drawn it, because a map was a thing you made in daylight and the winter line was seen only by the bound.

She ran her thumb down the edge of the nearest sheet. There on the third ridge was the little hand-lettered name she should not have lettered: *Tamar's Shoulder*, for the cook who had fed her when she was seven and sulking. Farther east, a long ugly knob of rock she'd have called Point 4410 a year ago was *Kiran's Elbow*. She had other names too. A ridge for her brother. A tarn for a ferryman who'd once shared his lunch. Nobody had asked her to name them. The crown's own ledger called them Spur Nine and Spur Fourteen, and the queen's clerks would, she supposed, cross out every one of hers.

A map was true or it was false. She believed that the way she believed her own legs. And she knew, in the part of her that was honest about small things, that naming a peak after a person she liked was not a measurement at all.

She turned the lamp up.

Without the winter ways the map would sit in the Taravan archive as a very good summer map. They would nod at it. They would thank her. And the chief surveyor's chair, which had been promised by a dozen men to a dozen other people, would go to someone who had never been cold in his life.

Her winter licence went before the hill council tomorrow, at the Fair. With it, she could ask a bound pair to carry her across the Saddle. Without it she was a woman with a lovely sheet of paper and no way to finish it.

She stared at the sheets for a long time. Then she picked up her chain, her field book and a lantern, because she had remembered something, and the remembering was like a stone in a boot.

The stair. She had counted it from the outside, twice. She had never counted it from the north landing, where the room began.

* * *

The tower stair was a spiral of grey stone steps with an iron rope for a rail, cold enough to burn through her glove. The guard at the foot, a hill-council man with a fresh blue cord on his shoulder, looked at her pass, then at her chain, then at the sky, and waved her up with the air of a man who would rather not be asked later what he'd seen.

She climbed with the lantern low and counted aloud under her breath. The count was not a habit. It was a way of making the stone keep faith.

On the north landing she stopped. The stair here widened to a flat of old boards, and the north wall, rough-dressed and sweating a little, ran a few paces away to the dark of the long room's door. She put her palm on the wall. It was colder than the rest. Not very much. She might not have noticed it if she had not been looking.

"The crown sent you to measure a prison."

The voice came from below her, pleasant and level. She did not jump. She put the lantern down on the step and turned.

Prince Kunal of Neshar stood three steps down with one hand loose on the iron rail. He was thirty-three, tall in the way mountain men were tall, with a plain dark coat and a long face that looked as if it had been made for laughing and had not been used for it lately. He had no circlet. He had a tired look and a small ink stain on his right thumb. She had seen him four times across a drafting table in the guest-house, arguing over a rope.

"Highness," she said.

"Kunal, on a stair." He came up one step. "Though I'm told the crown would like to know why it wants a prison measured. Is that wrong?"

"You'd have to ask the crown."

"Would the crown tell me?"

"It might." Ila lifted her chin. "It told me. The commission says every way. A tower with a prince's uncle in it is, in the narrow sense, a way."

"Is it?" said Kunal. "A way to where?"

She had stood at the drafting table beside her brother and a man who answered every question with another one, and she had found it funny. It was less funny at close quarters, on a cold stair, with her lantern at his feet. She looked at him.

"How many steps from the yard door to here, Highness?"

"Does it matter?"

"I'd like to know if you know."

"Thirty-nine," he said at once. He said it easily, a child's number, one that had been counted a thousand times with a hand on the rail. "Thirty-nine. Is that not what you got?"

"Forty."

"Is it?"

"There's a half-height step under the north landing, just there." She pointed with her chin. It was set into the boards so low that her boot would have missed it if she had not been counting. "Everyone steps over it. Nobody counts it. It's still a step."

Kunal looked at it. For a moment, only a moment, he looked at the north wall beside it instead, the cold wet length of it, and then back at her face.

"Children's count," he said. "My cousins and I learned thirty-nine. We were small and the step was for ghosts."

"You can't leave out a step because it's embarrassing."

"Can't I?" He smiled slightly. "I'm told it's done in every court in the world."

She stopped herself laughing. It was a close thing. She held up the field book instead.

"The north wall," she said. "Inside, the long room is two spans shorter than the outside allows. I've walked it three times and had my chain-man do it twice. There's a void."

She said it plainly. She was a surveyor, and plainness was most of what she had. And she watched him, because she was also a person who watched.

Kunal didn't move. After a second he stepped past her onto the landing and put the flat of his hand on the cold stone, the same place she had, as if it might say something.

"Do you know," he said, "how often my family has been told our house is crooked?"

"Is it?"

"Garh Neshar was built by a mason named Dev, who was drunk for most of the work. He's in the family's songs, mostly for that. The kitchen leans, the hall has a floor like a stormy lake and if you put a ball at the door of the east wing it will roll out the window." He turned and leaned against the wall with his arms folded. "A drunk man built a lopsided tower. Add it to your demands at council tomorrow. I'm sure you have a list."

"I have no list."

"Do you not?"

"I have a licence application, which asks to be carried across the Saddle in winter by a bound pair of the council's choosing, to draw the passes. It's four lines. I wrote them myself." She heard her own voice go brisk. "You read it. You were at the table when we cut the Law down to eleven lines. You argued I should say ten spans and not eight, and when I said eight, exactly, you asked whose arm."

"I did ask whose arm."

"It was the guide's own."

"I remember." His face did not change, but something moved behind it. "It was a good answer."

The drum on the meadow boomed once, very faint through the stone.

Kunal pushed himself upright. He was not smiling any more. He looked, for a moment, almost kind, which she distrusted more than she had distrusted the smile.

"I'll tell you plainly, Surveyor, since you've been plain with me. Tomorrow I will speak against your licence."

She waited. He didn't add anything.

"Against," said Ila.

"Against."

"All of it? Or the crossing?"

"Does it matter?"

"Highness," she said, "I measured your uncle's tower for six weeks. I know when I'm being given a number."

He opened his mouth to answer, and she saw the question coming, ready and polished, and something in her came loose.

She laughed. It burst out of her, loud and sudden, off the stone and down the stair, a bray that had been getting her sent out of dinner tables since she was eleven. It rang in the stairwell. Below, the guard must have flinched. She put the back of her hand to her mouth and could not stop.

"Against," she managed. "You came up a spiral stair in the dark to tell me, to my face, that you'll vote against me, and then you tried to give me a stair count to take with me."

Kunal stared at her. It was the first time she had seen him with nothing to say. He looked, as far as she could tell, startled, as a man looks when the door he has put his shoulder to turns out to have been open all along.

"I did say it plainly," he said at last, and for once it was not a question.

"You did." She wiped her eye with a knuckle and picked up her lantern. "Thank you. It's the most honest thing anyone in this house has said to me since the snow." She stepped carefully over the half-height step, into the light. "Good night, Highness. It's forty."

She went down the stair counting, and did not look back, and on the thirty-first step she began to wonder what he had been standing so very close to the north wall to hide.
