# Chapter 8: A Cold Man's Whistle

"Take her back over the Saddle," said Dhanu.

He said it to the chulha, not to Kunal, and he said it low. The long room had gone dark but for the coals. At its far end the sleeping shelf held a long shape under a goat blanket. The pale rope ran from that shape across the flags to the stool where Kunal sat with his knees nearly in the ash.

"Tomorrow night, when the cloud's gone. Down the Shilak side, to her brother's table. Let the rope run out at the Meadow Cairn's thaw and let her go home with her pencil. We keep the ways. She keeps her paper. A bright line, Kunal. You can draw it with a stick."

"I like bright lines," said Kunal.

"You like them when somebody else is standing behind them."

That was fair. Kunal poked a coal with a juniper twig and watched it flare. Hari had kept the Shepherd's Stair thirty winters on a line as bright as Dhanu's. His hut was a black square on the snow now.

"The crown's cover sheet to the council names a way beneath the Shilak Cairn," he said. "I read it twice in the hall while Chetu argued about grain. Her own note doesn't say it. She hasn't seen it."

"Beneath."

"Beneath. Have you ever heard of one?"

Dhanu thought. The coals ticked. "A cairn is a heap of stones. Under a heap of stones there's ground."

"Then why does the queen want the ground?" Kunal turned the twig over. "If I send Ila home, I send away the only door I have into what the crown knows. I would sooner learn about a way I've never heard of from her surveyor in the snow than from a garrison captain in spring."

"And what does she learn from you?"

"As little as she can draw."

"She drew my wall in a breath." Dhanu's voice was flat. "A hand short, the whole north side, and my father laid it with his own eyes. Don't tell me she's slow."

"I don't think she's slow."

"Then you're a fool who's fond of her, and the rope will tell you so." Dhanu grunted and put two fingers into his shirt. "Look here."

He drew out a thong with a horn on it. It was a goat's horn, a finger long, yellow as old tooth, with a black ring worn into the mouth where a lip had rested for twenty years. He held it in the coal light as if showing a child a coin.

"Two long, one short," said Dhanu. "That's the high way. *Come on, it's clear.* Three short is *turn back.* That's the whole of the way, to a stranger. A horn and a noise." He closed his fist round it. "You know why I wear it against my skin?"

"Tell me."

"If they ever take me, I'll swallow the route. The bridge, the mule, every stone of it. And they'll get my whistle off a cold man."

He grinned. Kunal did not.

"That isn't funny."

"It's a joke we both know is a joke," said Dhanu. "Mostly."

*Off a cold man.* Kunal looked at the horn until Dhanu tucked it away. He had told himself that a song was safer than ink, because a song went only where its singers went. Then Hari had chalked *They ask for the princes' way* on a marker stone, and somebody had sung Raghu the Stair. Whoever it was had not needed to take a man cold. He had only needed to take a man.

"Say it," said Dhanu, quieter. "What's all this for? I like to hear it."

Kunal set the twig down.

"No garrisons in the hill villages. Not one barrack at Sarn, not one crown lamp in a tower. And a seat for Neshar's council at Taravan, won at the thaw with the queen's hand on the paper." He listened to how it sounded. "Won. Not given."

"And the ways?"

"Stay where they are. In our heads."

"Then take her over the Saddle."

"I can't, and I've told you why." He looked at the shelf. The blanket hadn't moved, and the rope between them lay slack on the flags. Slack was not the same as asleep. "I'll have to be careful what I give her. And she'll have to be careful what she asks."

Dhanu sat a while with his bad foot out. Then he said, "Gauri and I go ahead by the short way tomorrow noon. We'll stock the lake huts and sit on the high ground and watch for Raghu's pairs. You come after, when you've done what you've got to do."

"By the Hollow Moon."

"Where his men went."

"Which is why I want them seen."

"Then see them," said Dhanu, and cuffed him round the ear, softly, for the fourth time that day.

* * *

In the morning Kunal sat in the doorway mending a pack strap and listened to Ila teach his friend's daughter to count.

"No, don't copy me," Ila was saying. She stood in the trodden yard with the knotted cord in one hand. "My stride's no use to you. Walk to the wall. Say each step. Now again, and see if you get the same number. If you do, that's your pace, and it's yours for good."

"Twenty-two," said Mini, from the wall.

"Again."

"Twenty-two!"

"Then you know how big the pen is. Not in my spans. In Mini." Ila crouched and held out the cord. "Everything in the world can be told in your own steps, until someone bigger turns up with a rule."

"Is a goat bigger?"

"Not in the ways that count."

Mini considered, then walked the wall again with her tongue between her teeth. Kunal's needle had stopped in the strap. A small, cold, unreasonable alarm rose in him.

He had taught that child the Stair song as nonsense, a counting rhyme about goats and ghosts, so it would go on being carried without anyone knowing what it carried. And here was the crown's surveyor teaching her a different kind of carrying. A number was a way, as a name was a way. A child who could pace could walk a line off a sheet in the dark. A child who sang could walk a line off a song.

*Paper goes where paper goes,* he had told her. He had not said, because he had not known it until now, that people went there too.

Ila looked up and caught him watching. She did not look away, so he did not either.

"Two hundred and four," Mini announced, round the whole pen.

"Two hundred and four of Mini," said Ila gravely. "That's a long way for short legs. Write it down. In your head. Not on my sheet."

At noon Dhanu and Gauri left with the pony and a pack of lamp oil and barley, and Mini riding on top of both. The short way kept under the snow-line, where the jogini asked no rope. Gauri shook Ila's hand once, hard, like a woman closing a bargain. Dhanu looked at the rope, then at Kunal, and said nothing at all. It was the most dangerous thing he had said in a week.

The yard emptied, and the smoke of the one lit chimney stood straight up.

* * *

They had the house to themselves that night. The chulha burned low. The wind, which had been loud all afternoon, dropped to a mutter in the roof.

Ila sat on the flags with her field book shut on her knee. She had measured the cliff behind the houses that afternoon, and he had given her the name for its notch, *the clerk's stoop*, with its season and its use. She had written it exactly as he said it and asked no more. She had not used her question for the night, and he had been waiting for it, waiting for a slate to slide.

"A question," said Ila. "Not from the bargain. Mine to ask. Yours to refuse."

"Then ask."

"At supper Dhanu said that at nine, the first time you walked these ways, you went alone. *All alone the first time.* You steered us off it as you'd steer a cart round a hole." She said it without edge. "What did he mean?"

He had known it was coming, and still the answer rose in him ready-made. It had a use already. It could be said so that she'd think kindly of him, or so that she'd think nothing, or so that she'd stop asking. He caught himself weighing it, as he weighed everything, and was tired.

"My uncle taught me the ways," said Kunal.

His hands were spread to the coals, bare, the fingertips pink in the glow. Her eyes went to them, quick and brief, the look of a warden's sister who had learned what to read on a bound man's skin.

"That's true," said Ila.

"It is."

"It isn't the answer."

"No. It's half."

"Which half?"

"The half I have ready." He kept his eyes on the coals. "I'm sorry. I haven't a better way to say it."

She was quiet. He waited for the follow-up, the plain, exact question that would cost him the rest, and he did not know what he would do.

"I'll take the half," said Ila, and opened her book, and wrote nothing in it.

She had not asked what use the other half might be to her. She had only declined to take it.

* * *

He woke her in the dark before dawn, with a hand on her shoulder and no excuse, and took her up the goat path behind the houses. She came without argument, her compass in her fist. He had decided he would show her the Hollow Moon from above before tonight. He had not decided what else he would say.

The cliff top was a bare shelf of rock and old snow. The sky in the east had gone the colour of pewter, and below them Ewe's Rest lay folded in its own shadow, the pen wall like a chalk line round the sheep. To the north the land fell away into a long wide bowl, white and rumpled, with the first light lying on its far rim.

"The Hollow Moon," said Kunal. "The summer way to Kuhl, for the flocks. A night's walk across the lip, and no cairns worth the name."

"Bearing to the far rim, ten degrees west of north." She had the compass out. "Eight hundred paces down, the fan of loose stone. That's where the snow's thin. A man would keep to the left of it."

"A man would," he said, "if he had the song."

She nodded, and went on looking.

He should have stopped there. He knew every reason to stop, and he had weighed them all while he climbed. A true answer was a line on the crown's map. A true answer was a way, and a way was a lock, and he had spent his life holding the key. But the light was coming, and she had been kind about the half. She had stood in his friend's yard with a child's hand in hers and taught her to count in her own steps. Something in him had come loose and was running before he could catch it.

"When I was nine," said Kunal, "my uncle—"

"Don't turn your head."

He stopped, with his mouth still open on the next word.

Ila had not moved. Her voice had gone flat and low and perfectly exact, the voice she used for a bearing. She was looking past his shoulder, across the bowl, and her compass hand was quite still on her coat.

"The west lip of the bowl," she said. "Three hundred and fifteen degrees, above the stone fan. A black outcrop, with a pale scar of scree beneath it. Look at it with your eyes only."

He let his eyes slide. The sun had cleared the ridge behind them and laid a long gold bar across the far slope. On the rim, in the dark of the outcrop, a point of light flashed once and went out.

Then it flashed again.

"That isn't water," said Ila. "It isn't ice. Ice doesn't wink twice and stop. I've been reading these slopes since we climbed, because I read slopes. That's glass, Kunal. A lens, turned to catch the sun. It was pointed at the hamlet before we came up, and it's pointed at us now."

The wind moved. Far below them, the one chimney of Ewe's Rest went on breathing its thin straight line of smoke.

"Someone," said Ila, "is watching your house."
