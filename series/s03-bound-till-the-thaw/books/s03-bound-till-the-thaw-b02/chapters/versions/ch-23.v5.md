# Chapter 23: Seven Breaths

The cleft breathed on her before she had the lamp lit.

A slow, even draught, cold enough to ache in the teeth, came out of the dark at the head of the Eye as if the mountain had turned over in its sleep. Ila shuttered the lamp to a slit. Behind her the lake lay black and still. On the far shore, four men with spyglasses were watching a cliff.

"Blue," said Kunal.

She had been looking too high. She looked low. Along the foot of the left wall, where the ice met the stone, small stacked stones ran into the cleft, one every few paces, each with a pale blue glow. The glow was no brighter than breath on glass.

"I see it."

"I didn't. Last time." He stood with the rope slack between them and his bandaged hands held a little away from his coat. "I stood here at twelve and there was nothing. Only a man behind me saying it was dark and I'd been brave."

She did not say anything kind. He would have heard the effort in it.

"Walk," said Ila. "I'll count."

They went in.

The walls were ice over stone, and the lamp lit them from inside, so that they walked down a throat of blue glass. Water ticked somewhere ahead. Each drop came alone, like a clerk tapping a pen. She counted her paces under her breath, because counting was what her feet did when her head wanted somewhere to stand. Eighty. Eighty-one.

The wall on the left came in. The passage doubled back on itself like a hairpin, the stone slick and black at the bend, and Kunal stopped dead with the rope drawn out behind him.

"Here," he said.

He was not asking. She waited. His breath smoked in the lamp slit.

"He turned me by the shoulders. Like this." His hands stayed in his coat. "He said, 'The rest is for a prince who's earned it.' Then he walked me out. I counted his steps all the way to the shore, so I'd know them."

"Kunal."

"There's a song." He said it to the hairpin, not to her. "He taught it to me that day, so I'd have it when I'd earned it. I have sung it to myself for twenty-one winters and never once walked it." He drew a breath, let it go, and sang very low, in a child's flat tune.

*First the turn that turns you home,*
*then seven breaths and none to keep,*
*then the stone that weeps and weeps,*
*step on the wet where the water sleeps.*

"It ends there," he said. "Past that, nothing. He never gave me the rest."

Ila looked at the hairpin. She had walked into it already with her eyes. "That's not an order," she said.

"What?"

"*The turn that turns you home.* You've heard it all your life as 'go home.'" She put her gloved hand on the bend. "It's a bearing. The passage turns you round, and you face the way you came. It doubles back, so you're facing home and walking away from it."

He stared at the stone. Then he laughed, very short and not happy. "All my life."

"Twenty-one years. You were twelve." She held out the lamp. "Go on. You're past your furthest point by a step, and I'd like you to be the one who takes the first."

He took it. The rope paid out behind him around the corner. She followed, and the blue stones went with them.

* * *

The slot began forty paces on, a crack in the wall the width of a man's shoulders sideways and no more. It ran away into a dark the lamp could not enter. Cold air came out of it. Kunal put his face to it and took it in like a man smelling bread.

"Seven breaths," he said. "That's what the song means. Long enough that you'd want seven, and you can't have them. The rock's too tight. Breathe in and your chest swells and you stick." He turned to her. "Let it all the way out first. Then don't take another until you're through."

"How long is it?"

"I don't know." He said it plainly, and she respected him for it.

She did the sum anyway. A man's slow walking breath, seven times. The slot was long. "Take the lamp through with you," said Ila. "Set it down on the far side, shuttered. I'd rather cross toward a light than back into one more dark." She tied her pencil case flat inside her coat, where her ribs would shield it. "If you stick, don't fight. Breathe out more, and I'll pull." She went on, because he was not saying it. "If I stick, you do the same."

"I'd have done it anyway."

"I know. I wanted it on the record."

He went in first, the lamp held out before him in one bandaged hand. The rope slid after him in short, hard jerks. She counted them. At seven spans it went slack and then taut again, and one long pull came down it. *Come.*

She breathed out until her ribs met each other. Then she turned her shoulder to the stone and went in.

It was worse than the Ladder. The rock took her front and back, cold through her pattu, and the white scar along her rib dragged over a knob of stone and woke. *One.* Far ahead, a thread of lamplight, no wider than a hair. *Two. Three.* Her lungs began to talk. *Four.* The rope at her wrist pulled steadily, and she let it. *Five.* A black pressure came behind her eyes. *Six.* The stone ground at her breastbone. *Seven.*

Then the stone let go of her chest, and she fell out of the crack onto her hands and knees and breathed in. The air tasted of iron and snow. She had never tasted better.

Kunal had her by the collar, one hand in her pattu and the heel of the other under her arm. She saw that the dead fingers of the right one would not close.

"Seven," she said, with a gasp that turned into a laugh. "I counted seven. I'd like to write to your uncle. His song's exact."

"Don't." He was laughing as well, helplessly, with his forehead against the wall. "Don't, he'd be insufferable."

He lifted the lamp from the ledge where he had set it, and opened the shutter a hand's width. They stood a moment in a space like the inside of a bell. Somewhere ahead the water ticked faster.

The weeping stone was twenty paces on.

A ledge of black rock lay glazed with ice, and a skin of meltwater slid over it in a thin bright sheet and fell into a runnel below. The runnel went down into a dark with no bottom. A bound pair's winter line crossed the stone, one blue cairn at each end, and the light on the far one was as dim as a candle through a sleeve.

"Step on the wet," said Kunal, "where the water sleeps." He looked at it. "The dry ice is the glaze. The wet is the stone under it."

"One in five," said Ila.

"What is?"

"That the glaze takes a boot." She dealt in odds that way. She meant it as a number, and he took it as one. "I'd put it lower on the wet. I'd put it higher if you kept looking at your feet."

"I won't."

He sang as he went, to keep his eyes up. The tune was the same flat one, a little steadier. His boot came down in the bright skin of water, and it held, and the next. On the third step the dead hand slid off the wall. He did not drop. He put the palm flat to the stone and leaned his weight into it, and she saw his mouth go flat with the effort.

He reached the far cairn. The blue light climbed on his face.

She crossed on the wet, with his song in her ears. Her boot held. She came off the ledge into a long dry passage of loose stone, and the blue stones ran away ahead in a thin line, down into the mountain.

* * *

"Stop," said Kunal. "I want you to write this."

They had gone on perhaps three hundred paces. She had counted every one, and the passage was wide enough now to walk upright, with a floor of dry gravel that ticked under their boots. Far ahead the meltwater ran in the dark, and the cairn-light made a path of small blue seeds.

"Write what?"

"All of it." He sat down on the gravel with his back to the wall and held his hands open on his knees. "Get the sheet out."

She did, and the pencil lead warmed in her fist. Tonight his dead fingertips could not pinch one from his coat, so she had reached in for them herself, without a word, all the way down.

"First, a rule," said Kunal. "My uncle made it the week my father was buried. No prince shows the way to wife, son or friend. He said the way kills those who share it." He kept his eyes on the gravel, so she would not have to watch him say it. "I told you of the Eye already, and that was a crack in it. Today I'm breaking it with my feet. Here, in front of the jogini, on purpose. Write that I did."

"I'm not your wife."

"No." Something came and went at the corner of his mouth. "Friend will do for the rule. I haven't settled the rest."

She wrote it. Her line shook once, and she let it.

"From the cleft mouth, by your count, and you'll mend me where I'm out," said Kunal. "Eighty-one paces to the turn. It doubles back on the left. Forty to the slot, and the slot is seven breaths, out first. Twenty to the weeping stone, and you step on the wet, the third step is the bad one. Then three hundred and four to here." He looked up. "Ila, he gave me four lines of a song. I've had it all my life. Everything past the stone, I'm learning as we walk it, with you. If I'm out, correct me."

"You're not out." Her pencil went on. "You're not."

"Then it's yours. All of it. Whatever the hills say to me after."

He had not asked what she meant to do with it. She waited for the question, with her hand over the page. A man asks that to buy a thing, and no question came.

"You haven't said what you want for it," said Ila.

"No."

"Why?"

He turned his face up to the lamp. That was not the courtier's face. She had stopped being afraid of that one. This was the other, which looked younger than he was and a good deal more tired.

"Because I want to know what you do with it more than I want to own it," said Kunal.

The cairn hummed. The small stone beside her knee, no higher than her boot, held the note low and level, and her own fingertips were warm when she looked at them.

"That's a bad trade," she said, and her voice came out too light.

"It isn't a trade."

That was the worst of it. She wrote the next figure down and the line shook. She had traded her whole life, a name for a measure, a clause for a clause, and she knew where she stood on every ledger. This was a gift with nothing on the other side of it. She had no column for it.

"I'll draw it," she said.

"I know."

They went on.

* * *

The way ended in water.

No lake lay there. The stone fell away into a hollow wide as a hall, with a floor that sloped to a black edge. Beyond the edge the dark drank the lamp. The blue stones went down the slope in a neat line and into the water, and she could see them under it, small and steady, a long way down.

The sound came up cold. The ticking was gone. A slow, great pulse had replaced it, as if the water were breathing.

"It goes under," said Kunal.

"It goes down." She walked to the edge and put the lamp low. The cairns went on beneath the surface, a lit stair into black. "I can't say how far. If the water's still, it could be a pool. If it's moving, it's a river, and we'd need rope longer than twelve spans, and the spirits won't take that."

"Dive it?"

"With nothing but a lamp and a bound partner?" She tried to say it lightly, and managed it. "One in two we'd drown, and I'd rather have worse odds on something I could see."

He nodded slowly. "Then we need stores."

"Bread for a night, and oil for less." She shook the lamp and heard it slosh, half gone. "And a line of days, which we don't have. If we turn now, we're out by dawn. If we push on with half a lamp, we're not out at all."

That was the sum of it. She did it twice, and the sum had a worse line under it. Going back meant the slot again, and the slot had cost her more than the Ladder. She would do it with her scar dragging and a count of seven she did not trust herself to hold twice. The Meadow Cairn faced south, and the thaw was out there somewhere, soft or hard, and a day turned back was a day gone from the weeks she owed him. *If it runs before we're through.* The words sat in her head and would not move.

"We come back," said Kunal.

"We come back."

He sat on the slope with his back to a boulder. His shoulders had stayed up since the cleft, and now they came down. She sat opposite, took out the long sheet, and laid it on her knee under the lamp.

She drew.

The line went down thin and black on pale paper, with every count in its small square figure. The hairpin that turned you home. The slot, with *7 br.* beside it and a mark where the stone dragged her rib. The weeping stone. The gravel passage. The blue cairns marked as a dotted line, and the black hollow at its end, where the dots ran under the water and off the edge of the paper.

She lifted the pencil. Every figure closed. It was the best sheet she had ever made, and she knew by now what that was worth.

Kunal watched her hand, as he always did, with that slightly stunned look.

"Dhanu said to ask," he said. "After. Not before." His eyes stayed on the sheet. "I'm asking."

"What will I do with it."

"Yes."

She looked at the line. She thought of the survey office, and a key she had never been given. She thought of a small yellow star in a margin, and a man's gloves laid palms together on a stone.

"I drew it," said Ila. "I don't know yet whose it is."

The cairn beside her gave its note, steady and unbroken. Her fingertips stayed pink. The answer was true all the way down, which was the only comfort in it.

He breathed out slowly. It was not relief, and it was not disappointment. It was the sound of a man who had braced for a bargain and been handed none.

"That's the first true answer anyone's given me about a map," said Kunal.

"It's the only one I have."

She should tell him. The thought came in whole, with the lamp on his face. *There's a letter in my hem, and it says your grandfather walked this way with a warden, and the warden did not come home.* Hedged, even in her own head: the letter did not say he had walked this way, only that he had been guided. She had the first word of it in her mouth. Her hand lay flat over the stitches of the pattu.

Her oath stopped it first. She had sworn in a hall in Taravan to keep what the crown's papers held, and the queen had written it plainer still: *Tell no one Neshar's part.* To speak was to break both, here, on her knee, with the sheet still wet. She had broken one oath for him already, in her way. She did not know yet if she could break the second.

And there was the other reason, which was hers alone.

*Through the cleft, and the Eye, and whatever's under it.*

That was what she had said, with the rope humming, and she had meant every word. And they were not through. There was water under that dark and a stair going down into it. If she told him now, she would be paying a gift with a confession, a thing to square the account. He had said it wasn't a trade. She would not make it one.

She shut her mouth. Silence was free, and she hated what it cost.

"What?" said Kunal.

"Nothing yet," said Ila. "I'll tell you when I can pay it properly."

He looked at her a long moment. That morning he had paid for his own question with his fingers, and he had never asked her twice.

"All right," he said.

* * *

They came out at dawn.

The cleft let them go as quietly as it had taken them, into a grey light that turned the lake to a sheet of pewter. Ila stood on the ice lip with the sheet inside her coat and her legs shaking. She breathed the cold air and tasted nothing in it but morning. The rope hung slack and warm between her wrist and his.

Nobody had come for them. The far shore was bare. The cliff stood where it stood. Raghu's four had gone, or hidden, or were watching something else.

"Gauri will have bread," said Kunal. He sounded very tired. "She'll say nothing about it. She'll hand it over and say nothing."

"I'd marry her for that alone."

He laughed. Then he turned toward the lower end of the lake, where the shore folded in below the Drum and the lodging huts sat in their shelf of ground, and the laugh stopped.

Ila looked where he looked.

Every morning of her time on this lake she had seen a thread of smoke over the huts, grey and straight in the cold, where Gauri banked the fire. The huts stood in their fold of shore, small and dark and square.

There was no smoke.
