# Chapter 3: Names to the Stones

The council clerk found Ila in the guest-house kitchen at dusk and would not sit down.

"Surveyor." He held a flat packet in both hands, the way a man holds something that might spill. "The crown's commission came in two parts. The cover sheet was for the council. This part is yours. We were told not to open it, and we haven't."

She took it. Oilcloth outside, then a fold of good paper, then the wax. She turned it to the lamp.

It was the royal archive's seal, pressed deep. A thread of red ran through the wax, thin as a vein, curling once at the edge. She had seen that same thread in the wafer Rohan pressed on the Fair meadow a year ago, blood the queen had mixed in Taravan with her own hand. Her brother had stood white-faced beside it. It was a bad thing to remember at a kitchen table.

Across the fold, in a hand she had seen on her own commission, was a single line.

*Open when you stand at the Glass Lakes. Not before. S.*

"Where are the Glass Lakes?" said Ila.

The clerk looked relieved to have a question he could answer. "I don't know, Surveyor."

"Neither do I."

That was the part she did not like. She knew every named water in Hemkot. She had drawn tarns the size of a threshing floor, and given three of them numbers. She went through them now, valley by valley, and none was called Glass.

She turned the packet over. A man who wanted to know what was inside could slide a hot knife under that wax and nobody would see. She knew exactly how it was done. She laid the packet on the table and looked at it the way she looked at a bench mark someone had told her not to move.

A sealed paper was a measurement she was not allowed to take. The queen had put a clause in the council's sheet that Ila had never seen. Now she had put a place in Ila's hand that Ila had never heard of. Someone was drawing a map of Ila, and she was the last to see it.

She wrapped the packet in its oilcloth and sewed it into the lining of her coat, over the ribs. It was a poor hiding place. It was also hers.

* * *

Kiran came to the kitchen door after the lamps were lit. He had his pack on, and his cap in his hands.

"I've a place," he said. "A licensed pair, going over to Shilak at first light. The post's own, with seed barley. Bindu's cousins." He turned the cap. "I've not been home since before last winter."

"Then go home." She said it before she heard how it sounded. "I mean it kindly. I can't take you. It's pairs only. You know the rule. A third on the rope and the mountain takes all three."

"I know." He tried the grin. It came a little crooked. "I only wanted to ask."

"You asked, and it's no. Ask the other thing."

He looked at her. She had meant it for a joke. He didn't take it as one.

"If you find it," he said. "Where the Bargain is paid. Wherever it is. Send word to me first. Not to Didi. Not to your brother." His thumb came up and found the torn rim of his ear. "To me."

"Why you first?"

"Because I'd like to hear it before the rest of them." He gave a short breath of a laugh. "I'm the nervous sort. Ask anyone."

Ila knew nervous men. Her whole profession was full of them, sure of every distance but their own. She had a sudden picture of Kiran walking the Saddle line alone in his head, all winter, wondering whether the mountain would close behind him.

She went and put her hand on his arm. It was a stiff gesture, the kind she gave a chain-man who had dropped a pole and was sorry.

"I'll send word," she said. "Eat before you go. They'll feed you on sattu and talk."

"Thank you, Ila-ji."

He left. She stood a while with her hand still half out, and told herself he was worried for her, and that it was kind of him, and that the kindness was the whole of it.

She wrote *Kiran: ear, again* in her field book, and then drew a line through it, because it was not a measurement.

* * *

The Meadow Cairn stood on the shoulder above the Fair meadow, and they climbed to it in the dark.

It was the nearest jogini cairn, and the only one a licensed pair could reach without crossing a pass alone. Kunal went ahead, with a lantern he kept low. Ila came behind with her pack and her field book and a coil of rope over one shoulder. Frost cracked under her boots like a thin plate.

She had bought the rope in Taravan in the autumn, undyed wool from Lata's house, spun to a hand's thickness. She had measured it against her own arm, span by span, on the guest-house floor, because the law said the guide's own arm and she was the guide. Eight spans. The wool smelled of lanolin and cold.

"There's a clock on this, you know," said Kunal over his shoulder.

"Is there?"

"A bond ends when meltwater first runs at the cairn where it was tied. Not at the cairn you name. This one." He stopped to let her draw level. "The Meadow Cairn faces south and holds the sun. Its water runs about a month before Shilak's does. And when it runs, the rope is just rope, wherever we're standing."

"Wherever?"

"If we're at the top of a ridge, we're at the top of a ridge. If we're on the wrong side of it, well." He lifted a shoulder. "The mountain is very exact. It's one of the things I like about it."

She had her book open before he'd finished. Eleven winters of thaw dates, each in her own hand, from the post at Taravan to the posts on the passes. She ran the Meadow column against Shilak's by the light of his lantern. In a hard year the gap closed to three weeks. In a soft year it opened.

"Thirty-five days," she said. "In a soft year. That's what I lose."

"Lose?"

"The crown thinks I have the whole winter. I have the winter minus thirty-five days." She shut the book. "Did you plan that?"

"Did I plan a thaw?" said Kunal. But he was almost smiling. "You'd have found it in your own tables by morning. I only saved you a night's sleep."

It was true, and she disliked that it was true. She walked on.

The cairn rose out of the dark as a ring of flat stones, chest-high, pale with rime. Ila put her mitten on the top course and felt the stones hum under it, a low note, steady as a held breath. The east was going grey. On the inner face, where the rime had not reached, someone had cut two hands into the rock, joined by a line.

"Wrists," said Kunal.

They tied it the way the law said, the same knot on each other. She used a warden's double hitch, the one her brother tied on everything, and was irritated to find her fingers shaking. He had cold hands. He tied hers a little loose, then drew it snug and met her eye as if to ask whether that was right.

"Snug," she said.

"Good."

The rope lay between them in a long slack curve, eight spans, and it was heavier than it looked.

They stood either side of the stones. Ila spoke first, because she had decided to.

"Ila, of the Shilak post's family, surveyor. Bound for the Shilak Cairn."

The hum did not change. She had expected something and had got nothing, and it made her feel foolish, then relieved.

"Kunal of Neshar," said Kunal. "Bound for the Shilak Cairn."

He said the last words a breath late. It was nothing. A man collecting himself. The hum held steady, low and level, as if the stones had never heard anything else. But she had spent her life noticing a hair of difference in a line, and she noticed it, and put it away where she put the stair count and the two spans of wall.

A warmth went round her wrist and stopped there, like a hand laid on it. Across the stones, Kunal looked down at his own wrist, and then at hers.

"Well," he said. "That's done."

"Is that all you do? Say well?"

"I'm told I say it at weddings."

She nearly laughed. Instead she did what she had come up the hill meaning to do, because she was a person who tested a chain before she hung weight on it.

"Highness. Watch my hands."

His eyes went to them. She held them out, palms down, ten ordinary fingers in the grey light.

"I like you," said Ila.

She said it flat, and she meant him to believe it, because the rule would only count it if she did. She heard the stones lift half a note, thin and cold, before she felt anything. Then the cold came, a tight pinch in every fingertip, as if she had pressed them to a frozen bucket. She watched them go white, nail beds first, to the second joint.

"Ah," said Kunal.

He had believed her. She saw it, for the length of one breath, a small start of surprise at the plain words. Then his eyes found her fingers and the surprise went out of him, and what replaced it was something so quick and open and uncalculated that she did not at first know what it was.

He laughed.

It was not the laugh she knew, the polished one for councils. It came out of him in a rush and bent him half over the stones, one hand on the cold rim to hold himself up. He laughed until the rope shivered between them. The first light caught the long face and found in it, for a moment, the man it had been made for.

"An hour," he said, when he could. "Is that an hour? For something so small?"

"An hour. Small lie." She flexed the white fingers and winced. "Don't gloat. You're wondering whether I meant it. Go on. Say you don't care what I meant."

"I don't," said Kunal, and then stopped, and looked at his own hands as if they had done something without leave. They stayed pink. He seemed more startled by that than by anything the stones had done. "That was true. It was a great deal easier than I expected."

"It's useful," said Ila. "Now we both know the rope works and that neither of us can say a thing for free."

"Except to anyone else."

"Except to anyone else."

"Or in silence."

"Or in silence." She tucked her chilled hands into her armpits. "You'll be very good at silence."

"I've had practice," he said. He was still half smiling, and it did not reach the part of his face that had gone careful again.

* * *

They walked west all day, up the Neshar road, because it was the only road a bound pair could take toward the Saddle. The rope paid out between them, eight spans, and she learned the length of it with her body. Seven and a half was the edge of comfort. At eight a thin chill began under the ribs and told her to come back.

They spoke little. He answered two of her questions with questions, and she let him. She wrote down the road: a culvert, a cattle-shed, the bridge where the road crossed a stream that made no noise because it was frozen to the bed. The white left her fingertips at noon. The pink came back with a sting.

By dusk the road had climbed into thin air and open slope. The wind had a bite. Behind them the Fair meadow was a smear of smoke far below, and ahead lay the long white rise of the pass, going violet.

"Stop," said Kunal.

It was the first thing he had said in an hour. She stopped. He was looking not at her but past her, at a cairn on the bank beside the road, a waist-high stack of grey stones.

A light was coming up in it.

It came as pale as breath on a cold glass, blue where the stones touched, then brighter, a thread and then a rope of blue. It lifted from the cairn and ran along the road ahead of them, stone to stone, cairn to cairn, up the slope and away toward the Long Saddle. A path, laid out in the dusk by someone who knew the mountain. She had been told about it all her life. She had never before seen it, and she was surprised to find her throat had closed.

"There it is," she said, and her voice came out small. "The winter line."

She got out her book with clumsy fingers, because the only way to look at a thing like that was to measure it. Bearing. Distance between lights, a hundred and forty paces, then a hundred and sixty. She had her pencil moving before she thought.

Then she looked east.

There was another thread.

It was fainter. She thought at first it was the afterimage of the first, a bruise in the eye. But it did not move when she blinked. It ran off to the east, over a shoulder of ground she had walked in summer, and it was broken, a light and a gap and a light, as though something kept going out in it. It dropped into a valley that lay in shadow. She knew the shoulder. She had named a ridge near it. She did not know the valley. It was not on her sheets. No stream ran from it, no path led to it, no shepherd had ever told her of it.

She had been through every fold of Neshar within two days' walk of the Fair, in boots and in ink. A valley was not a thing a person missed.

"Kunal," she said. "What's that?"

He said nothing.

She turned. He stood with the rope slack in his hand and his face toward the faint line, and the whole of him had gone still. It was not the stillness of a man thinking. She had seen men think; they fidgeted. This was the stillness of a goat on a ledge when a shadow crosses, when every muscle has decided together that the safest thing is to be a stone.

His hands hung at his sides. They were pink, quite pink, in the blue light. He wasn't lying. He wasn't speaking at all.

The wind picked up and made the cairn hum.

Two lines lay in front of her, one strong and one failing. She was a woman with a commission to draw every winter way, a sealed letter against her ribs, and a rope of Lata's wool binding her to the one man in Neshar who plainly knew the answer. She did not know which line was the one she was meant to take. She did not know whether there was any such thing as a right one.

"Which way?" said Ila.

Kunal turned his head slowly. His eyes, dark in the blue, held hers. When he spoke it was gentle, and she heard in it the old courtly sharpness, very faint, like a knife wrapped in cloth.

"Which does your queen want?"

She opened her mouth. Nothing came, because there was nothing she could say that was free.

He didn't wait. He looked back once at the broken light, then down at the rope on his wrist, then at the open book in her hand. Whatever he weighed, he weighed it fast. A surveyor who found this way alone was one thing. A surveyor who saw it tied to him was another, and he seemed to know the difference to the span. Then he stepped off the road and onto the frozen slope, east, toward the broken light, and the rope drew taut between them. Ila stood a heartbeat longer with her book open and her pencil dry.

Then the cold began under her ribs, just where the rope ended, and she followed him down into the dark.
