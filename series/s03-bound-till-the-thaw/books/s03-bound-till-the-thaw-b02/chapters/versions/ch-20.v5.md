# Chapter 20: The Ridge

The shore was a shelf of black stone with the Drum under it, and the ice there was alive.

Kunal stood where it boomed. Out on the lake a long seam let go, and the note came up through his boots and into his teeth. The cold was clean and the sky was thick with stars. Behind him Ila had stopped at the end of the rope's slack, a pace and a half away, with her hands in her sleeves.

She had not said anything since the hut. In twelve days he had learned this about her: she waited like a woman holding a level, to see which way the bubble went.

He had rehearsed it on the walk down, in the courtier's order. *First the fact, then the frame, then what it means.* He had a version that began *You should understand that my uncle* and ended with Ila nodding. He knew exactly what that version bought. It bought a woman who thought she had been told everything.

"I was nine," said Kunal.

His voice came out thin. He started again.

"My father died at the end of summer, of a lung fever. It was quick. A month after the funeral, in autumn, my uncle came to the nursery and said he would take me to see what was mine." The ice boomed. He waited it out. "We went up the Kuhl ridge. It was a long day. He bought me honey cake at the last farm, and he carried my coat when I got hot."

He stopped, because the next part had a shape he had never said aloud, and his mouth would not make it.

"At the top he gave me a knife and a heel of bread. He said, 'Find your way down.' I said, 'Which way?' and he said, 'That is the whole lesson.' Then he walked off along the ridge, and I watched him get small."

"Kunal," said Ila.

"Let me." He held up a bandaged hand. "I'll lose it if I stop."

She said nothing more.

"He said one more thing before he went. He didn't shout it. He said it like a man reading out a price. *Whoever knows the way owns the people.*" Kunal looked at the dark lake. "I had no route. I'd never walked that side. It came on to snow at dusk, and I sat under a rock and held the knife, and I was not brave about it."

"How did you get down?"

It was a surveyor's question, practical and low. It steadied him as nothing else could have.

"Goats." He half laughed. "In the morning I heard a bell. A nanny and eleven others, going down to the winter fold, and she knew the way in her feet. I followed her for two days. I ate snow and the bread, and on the first night one of the herd boys gave me a cheese rind and didn't ask my name. I walked into Garh Neshar at dusk on the second day. My uncle was at the table. He said, 'Well?' and I said, 'I came home.'"

The wind moved over the ice, and the seam boomed again, farther out.

"I kept every way after that. Whatever's in your head can't be taken. A map can be burned. A guide can be bought. A route-keeper can be hung. But I carry the Stair and the Moon and the Ladder, and the Eye, where he took me at twelve and turned me round. I carry them and I give them to nobody. It's why I'm alive." He breathed out. "It's why the hill council has followed me for eight winters, and why none of them has the whole of it. I tell them to be open, Ila. I stand up and tell them. And I've never once opened my own hand."

She said, very quietly, "You think you're him."

"I think the one thing I took from that day was his lesson, and I kept it too well." His voice cracked on it. He let it. "I'm afraid I'm his best pupil. I'm afraid that if I say a way aloud to you, I'll start counting what it buys. I counted at the Eye. I counted at the Stair. I'm counting now, and I can't find the sum."

He had never said any of it. It lay on the stone between them, and nothing he could think of would pick it up again.

"I don't know what telling you gets me," said Kunal. "That's why I'm telling you."

* * *

For a long moment she did not move.

Then she crossed the pace and a half. She stood at his shoulder, not touching him, and looked out where he looked, at the pale lake and the cliff beyond.

"You found the way," said Ila.

He turned his head.

"A boy of nine. No map, no guide, a knife and a heel of bread. You found it by listening for a bell, and you were home in two days." She said it in the flat, exact voice she used for distances, and he understood that she was giving him a measurement. "He walked off along a ridge and left you the whole mountain to cross. He owned nothing, Kunal. He owned a table and a chair, and a nine-year-old who got home without him."

He had no answer. His throat had closed.

"The goats owned more of that road than he did," she went on. "That's what I'd put on a sheet, if I drew it. And the thing you're afraid of." She paused, choosing the words one at a time. "He never gave a way to anyone. He kept the grain and the bell and the roads. You paid nineteen silvers with the corners clipped, so that the medicine in Ranu could not be traced to your hand. A man who does that in secret for two winters, with no one to thank him, is not Pritam. Pritam would have put his name on the sack."

The Drum boomed. Kunal was shaking, finely, in the shoulders, as a horse does after a fall.

"I didn't think anyone would work out why they were clipped," he said.

"I'm a surveyor. I check my closures." She turned to face him, and her face in the starlight was grave and open. "Nobody has told you that, have they?"

"No."

"Then I'm the first, and I've said it. It's measured." She took her hands out of her sleeves.

She held them up between them, bare, palms toward him, the fingers slightly curled. She was not holding them out to be taken. She was showing him.

They had been in the cold air for half an hour. In the starlight the tips were pink, the colour of blood close under skin.

"I want you," said Ila.

He heard it, and then he looked at her hands, as a bound man does. Warm. Not a shade paler. They stayed warm while he looked.

"I'd like you to know I said it with these showing," she said. Her voice was not entirely steady. "I'm not very good at this. I'd rather be checked."

"Ila."

"I'm thirty-one and I've checked my closures twice," she said. "I've known it since the lake. Possibly before, on the Stair, but I'd have had to work out the bearing, and I hadn't the numbers." One corner of her mouth went up. "Say something, please, because my hands are going to freeze."

He laughed. It cracked in the middle and became something else. He took her hands in his bandaged ones and held them, and they were warm, and he had never held a better piece of evidence.

"Kunal," she said.

Not *Highness*. Not *the prince*, with the small, dry edge she kept on it. His own name, in her brisk voice. He had to look out at the lake a moment before he could look back.

"Say it again," said Kunal.

"Kunal." She stepped closer. "I'd like to go back to the hut, if you want that too. If you don't, I'll sit here with you till the Drum stops, and that's true as well."

"I want it," he said. "In twenty-four years of counting, I've never wanted anything so plainly."

The rope slid between their boots as they turned. Neither of them reached for it. It was eight spans of undyed wool on a stone shore, and it had never been the thing that held them.

* * *

The herders' hut stood on the scree above the Drum, a steep climb up from the shore. Dhanu's people used it in summer for the high grazing, and it was cold. Kunal knelt at the chulha and coaxed a coal while Ila stood in the middle of the floor. Her coat was off, and it would hang on the peg by the door all night, where any hand might find what was in it. She unpicked three stitches of the lining, drew out a folded square of cream paper, and slipped it into the hem of her pattu. Her needle went fast and hard. He read in it that she would not leave the paper anywhere it could be found unasked, even by him, and she did not look at him while she sewed it shut. Then she untied the rope from both their wrists and laid it on the floor, because the knots were for the stone, and the stone was not what she was thinking of.

The fire caught. He sat back on his heels and found her watching him.

"I should say the practical thing," she said.

"Please."

"I want it, and I'm saying so, and you can say no at any point and I'll stop. I'd like the same." She blew out her breath. "I find it easier to put it as a rule."

"Then it's a rule." He stood. "No at any point. A word, and we stop, and I'll warm tea."

"You'll warm tea," said Ila. She was smiling. "You'd do it, too."

She came the last step. He kissed her, and the cold went out of his hands. He noticed everything: the dry warmth of her mouth, the cold edge of her nose, the way her fingers closed in the front of his coat and pulled. She kissed as she measured, with her whole attention. When he lifted his head she followed him a little way, and said, "Again," and he did.

His bandaged hands were clumsy on the toggles of her coat. She laughed against his jaw and undid them herself, and then undid his, one by one, with the small exact movements he had watched on a chain a hundred times. She took the strips off his fingers, kissed each pad where the skin had mended pink, and laid the wrappings on the shelf in a neat row.

"Oh," said Kunal, to the roof.

"You're hurt."

"I'm not. That's the trouble."

Her pattu came off her shoulders. Beneath it she wore the thick grey wool of a woman who had crossed three lakes, and she smelled of snow and goat fat and chulha smoke. He found the laces at her collar. He looked up for leave, and she nodded once, and he undid them. She did the same for him, with her mouth set seriously, and put her palm flat over his heart and held it there.

"That's quick," said Ila.

"Yes."

"Good." She said it fiercely. "Mine is too."

They went down onto the shelf and the single blanket together. The coals lit her from the side: the long line of her shoulder, the freckles below her collarbone, the white scar along one rib where a chain had once whipped back. He kissed it. She said "Ow," and "Don't stop," in the same breath, and he didn't.

In a hundred cold rooms he had thought desire was a thing you managed. A debt, a trade. Not this. Her hands knew where to go as though she had measured him. She slid them down his back and he arched, and she said, shaky and delighted, "There?" and he said "There."

When she drew him over her she held his face between her hands and stopped.

"Say it with me. Yes?"

"Yes," said Kunal. "You?"

"Yes." Her mouth shook. "Come here."

It was slow, because she wanted it slow, and he found that he did, too. The coals cracked. She breathed his name into his neck and again against his mouth, and her hand found his and laced their fingers beside her head.

Somewhere in it she stopped counting.

He knew the moment. She had been breathing in sets, a habit he had seen on every climb, in threes and fours. Then the sets broke, and she gasped, and laughed in surprise at herself, and held on. She went over first with his name in her throat. He followed, with his face in her hair and her heart going under his.

* * *

The coals had burned low when she shivered.

She had gone to the end of the shelf for water and come back with her feet bare. She tucked them under the blanket, and they met his shin like two stones taken out of a river.

"Ila. You're frozen."

"They're always cold. It's nothing."

"Is that a small lie?" He looked at her hands. Pink. "No. It's true. Come here."

She let him pull her feet into his lap. He wrapped his hands round the left, then the right, and rubbed the arches slowly with his thumbs. They were long, narrow, slightly callused at the heel, and so cold they made his palms ache. She went rigid for a breath. Then she let her head fall back against the wall and made a sound he would not forget.

"No one has done that," said Ila, in a different voice.

"Never?"

"My mother, when I was small." She laughed, shakily, with her eyes shut. "I keep them under me in the field. I put them in a bag on the Stair. I don't show them. It's absurd. They're feet."

"They're feet," he agreed. "And it's you telling me where you're soft. I'll take it as given." He drew his thumbs up under the ball of her foot, and she shivered all through.

"Ila."

"Mm."

"I'll give you a name a night," said Kunal. "From now on. One of ours, one of the hill's, true, as I know it. We've traded so far. A name for a question. I'd like to stop that." He kept his eyes on her foot. "I'll ask nothing back. Not an answer, not a line. You can draw it or burn it, and I'll know I gave it freely."

As he said it his eyes went, unbidden, to the pattu lying by the fire, and the hem where he had watched her sew the paper in. *Not an answer.* It covered that too. He saw it plainly enough, and let it stand.

She sat up. Her feet stayed in his lap, but her face had changed. It was the one she wore over a closure that would not come out.

"Then hear the price first," she said. "Out loud, so neither of us can say we didn't. You say that across the rope and it's a promise. A promise made while bound and broken before the thaw strikes you like a great lie. Your hands first, then the rest." She held up a second finger. "And every name you give me, I have drawn for the crown for eleven years. I haven't decided what I'll do with them. Each one could be a line on the queen's map by spring, with your hill's name on it, and you'd have put it there."

"I know both," said Kunal.

"Say you do."

"I know both. I'd rather lose the fingers than keep counting." He heard how that sounded and did not take it back. "Tie it on, and I'll say it again."

She looked at him a long moment. Then she reached for the rope on the floor. She knotted the end round his left wrist, flat and snug, and he tied hers, and the wool warmed between them.

"I'll ask nothing back," said Kunal.

The rope hummed against his wrist, low and level, with no climbing in it.

Her hand came down and lay over his on her ankle. "Which is tonight's?"

He knew which. It was the one he had held back longest, and it surprised him how little it weighed.

"The black crag you named for the healer." She went still. "We call it the Widow's Knuckle. For the hand a widow laid on the rock while she waited for her husband. It's older than the Stair. Write it down however you like."

"Kunal," said Ila.

"It's yours."

She slid down against him and put her face in his neck, and her cold feet against his calves. He pulled the blanket up over both of them and held her, and for the first time in twenty-four years, he did not count.

* * *

He woke before dawn, with the hut dark and grey at the edges, and the coals a thin red line.

She was awake. She lay on her side with her head on her arm, in the pattu, watching him. How long, he could not tell. Her eyes were very steady.

"You were speaking," said Ila.

"What did I say?"

"Names. In hill speech. I didn't write any of them."

He smiled and reached out. She let him touch her cheek, and it was warm. The pattu had slipped where its hem hung over the shelf's edge, and a corner of cream paper showed through a loosened stitch. Her hand lay over it, flat and still.

She saw him see it. She left her hand where it was.

Whatever it was, she had carried it since the Meadow Cairn. He remembered the shelf, and *I'll find out in daylight, from you.* He had set that down on purpose.

"Ila," said Kunal.

"Yes."

The question was already in his mouth. *What does it say?* He drew breath for it, and the cold arrived at once. It came into the tips of his fingers like the first bite of a long night on the ice, and the rope at his wrist went tight and still. He had said *nothing back*. He had said it with the knot on, and he had known what it covered.

He closed his hand on the blanket and swallowed the question whole.

"You're awake early," he said instead. The chill drew off his fingers slowly, as a tide goes out.

She had seen his fist close. She looked at it, and then at his face, and something crossed hers that he could not read.

"I was thinking." Her voice was calm. "About a number. It won't come out."

She lay watching him in the grey, and her hand did not leave her hem.
