# Chapter 5: The Burned Hut

The smell reached Kunal before the hut did.

It was not fresh smoke. It was the wet, sour smell of a fire that had burned itself out days ago and been rained on by snow, and he knew it from a long way down the salt road. He knew it the way a man knows the sound of his own name said wrong.

"Stop," he said.

Ila stopped. The rope between their wrists went slack, eight spans of Lata's wool lying across the scree like a dropped thread. She had been counting under her breath since the second spur. Her lips were still moving.

"Four hundred and ten," she said. "You've interrupted me."

"Forgive me. There's a hut ahead that shouldn't smell like that."

She looked where he looked. The road had carried them over two bare spurs and down into a hollow under a wall of grey rock. Above the wall, a white staircase of ice and snow climbed out of sight into low cloud. The Shepherd's Stair. He had walked it as a boy with his hand in a goat's wool. Beneath it, where the road gave out, there should have been a low stone hut with a turf roof and a thread of smoke from Hari's chimney.

There was a black square in the snow.

He went down faster than the rope liked. She came after him without comment, which he noted, and was grateful for, and did not have room to be grateful about.

The roof had fallen in. The walls stood to shoulder height, blackened, and the doorframe was a charred hoop around nothing. Snow had drifted into the room and melted again on warm ash, and frozen into a grey crust. Hari's bench was there. Hari's iron pot, on its side. Hari's goat-hair blanket was a rag stuck to the floor.

No body. He made himself look twice for one and found none, and his knees nearly let go with the relief.

"He's not here," he said, and heard how thin it sounded. "Hari. He keeps this stair. He's kept it thirty winters. His father kept it. He would not leave it for anything short of—" He stopped. "He's not here."

"Then he got out," said Ila, "or was taken."

"Yes."

She did not say it kindly or unkindly. She said it as a fact, and he found he preferred it.

There were prints in the ash. Many, overlapping, pressed deep through the crust into the soft black beneath. Boots, hobnailed, the sort that cost a month's grain. And half trodden into the char by the doorframe, bent and scorched, lay a small brass bell, the size of a walnut, with a loop for stitching.

He picked it up. The tongue was gone. It was a patrol bell, off a regent's grey wool shoulder. His uncle's men wore them so that the whole valley would hear them coming and know whose they were.

*Raghu's,* he thought. *Not Pritam's. Not any more. But he took the habit with him, and the bells with it.*

He closed his fist round the brass until it bit.

Hari had been burned out for a stair. Kunal knew exactly how it would go from here. Raghu would climb the Shepherd's Stair and learn what lay at the top. After that the high hamlets would have a man at their gates, asking for what they could not give, and then asking again with a torch. The Stair was not a road. It was a door, and the people on the far side had kept it shut for three hundred years because the hill had no other wall. If a captain's pairs went through it and the crown learned the way after them, Neshar would stop being a valley. It would be a road with a toll.

And he had a surveyor on his wrist. He looked at her.

Ila was not looking at the bell. She was crouched at the edge of the ash with her mitten off, her bare hand flat above the prints without touching them, and her lips moving again.

"Two roped pairs," she said.

He didn't answer. She did not seem to need him to.

"The prints come in two files. Here, and here. Each file has a gap of seven or eight spans between the same two men. That's a rope, tied and kept slack." She pointed. "Same on the other file. Two bound pairs. Four men. Hobnails, but the left boot of the rear man in each pair is worn on the outer edge, so they're not four strangers. They've walked together a long time."

"Ila—"

"They're loaded." She tapped the print nearest the door. "Look at the depth. That heel went in a thumb past the crust, and the toe is nearly flat. A man with a pack that heavy walks flat-footed. Salt weight, or iron, or rope, or all of it. They stood here a while." Her finger moved to a trampled patch by the wall. "They ate. There's grease on the stone. And then they went up." She looked at the white staircase. "The toes dig in, the stride shortens. Up."

"How long ago?"

"The ash has crusted but not drifted. The snow's lain on the roof beams a day, not two." She frowned at the sky. "I'd say a day. A day ahead, perhaps a little over."

He stood holding the bell.

She was right. He could see she was right, the way he could see a rockfall after someone had pointed to it. He had come down here with his mind on Hari and the smell, and his whole body bracing for the shape of a disaster. He had read the ground as a wound. She had read it as a column of figures, and the figures were better.

He had not counted. In thirty-three winters of knowing every stone of this hill, it had not occurred to him to count the men who had burned it.

"Four," he said slowly. "Two pairs."

"Heavily loaded, a day ahead, going up." She wiped her hand on her coat. "Did you not see it?"

"I saw Hari's pot."

It was the most honest thing he had said to her all morning, and he watched her take it in. She did not use it against him. She only nodded once, like a woman logging a bench mark, and said, "Then it's as well one of us was looking at the ground."

* * *

They ate standing, because the hut had nothing to sit on. Sattu and cold salt tea from her flask. The wind came down the Stair and rattled the burned beams, and Kunal made himself look at the white slope and not at the bell in his pocket.

"May I ask you something?" said Ila.

"You may ask me anything. It's the answering that costs you."

"I'm aware." She wiped her mouth. "Is Captain Raghu going up to get Pritam out?"

It was a good question. It was the question he would have asked. His uncle sat under guard in his own tower, with a bell mark on every sack in the valley and the whole hill council watching the door. A man who loved him, or owed him, might well want a road to carry him out over the mountain before spring.

"I don't know," said Kunal.

The rope hummed low and level, and her eyes dropped to his hands. They were pink. He saw her see it.

"That's true," she said. "You don't know."

"I know a good deal less than I'd like." He turned the cup in his fingers. "Raghu wants a way over the mountain for something. That much I'd stake the walnut on. What the something is..." He stopped.

He had meant to go on. He had a sentence, and he could feel its weight in his mouth, and the sentence had the word *fear* in it.

He did not want to say it. It was not a lie. It was a thing he was afraid of, a particular, specific thing, and it had lived in him since the fourth light went out, and saying it aloud to a crown surveyor would be handing her the first line of a map he had spent his life hiding. A true thing, spoken, could not be unsaid. He had watched his uncle build a coup out of true things said at the wrong table.

"I won't say," said Kunal.

"What won't you say?"

"What I think he wants it for. Or what I'm afraid of, if you'd rather." He made himself meet her eyes. "I'm not telling you it's nothing. I'm telling you I won't answer."

The rope stayed quiet. Silence cost nothing. He had known it would, and he had still half expected the cold to come for him.

Ila took out her field book. She wrote. He could see the page upside down, her small square hand, and he made out two words before she turned it.

*Refused. Free.*

"You're writing it down," he said.

"I'm writing everything down." She did not look up. "That's what you agreed to, at the fork. Every true answer becomes a line on my sheet. I don't see why a refusal shouldn't be a line too. A blank is data, Highness. A good surveyor learns more from the fog than from the clear."

"And what does the fog tell you?"

"That you're afraid." She closed the book. "Not of me, I think. I'd like to say I was sorry for it."

"Are you?"

"I'm trying to find out," said Ila, which was his own sentence, and he nearly laughed, and nearly didn't.

* * *

The Stair began in a gully so steep that the first rung was a ledge of rock at his chin.

He knew the route-song. *One for the goat who knows the gate, two for the stone that will not wait.* It was a child's counting game, and every verse was a pitch, and he sang none of it aloud. Ila climbed beside him with the pencil behind her ear and her eyes on the ice, and she was working out the same thing he was. How long a thing could stay frozen when a regent's men had trodden it for a day.

The lower steps were ice, cut by a hundred years of shepherds into broad shallow treads, each the length of a boot. In summer they were a stair of wet rock. In winter they were glass with a skin of snow, and the skin had cracked.

He put his weight on the first one and felt it give like rotten bread.

"No," he said, and took it back. "Rotten. They've been trampled and the meltwater's gone through. Heavy men, heavy packs. The Stair doesn't love them."

"Odds?" said Ila.

He glanced at her. She had her chin up and her rope-hand steady. It was the first time anyone had asked him for odds in the voice of someone who meant to use them.

"The first dozen are poor. Perhaps one in three that a tread shears under me. After that the ice is thicker, in the shade of the gully." He tried a second tread. It held, with a noise like a knuckle. "I'd rather it was me than you."

"Then you lead and I belay." She was already uncoiling the slack. She put her back against the rock and took a turn of rope round her hip with a speed that told him she had done it on worse. "Eight spans. If a step goes, I hold you. If I go, you hold me. Don't pull on the rope unless you mean it."

"Yes, Surveyor."

"Don't say it like that."

"Like what?"

"As if it's a joke." She said it very quietly. "It isn't. I've held three men on a rope in my life and I didn't let go of any of them."

He had no reply. He turned to the ice.

He climbed. The first tread shed a plate of ice the size of a tray, which went skating down the gully behind him and out into the dusk. The second held. The third gave and held again, and he took the next on his knees, spreading his weight, and felt through the rope that she was there. There was no tug. It was a weight, steady and warm, a hand on the other side of a door. In thirty-three years he had climbed this stair alone or with men who watched him. He had never climbed it with someone holding him up.

He counted as he went, because it was the song, and because it kept his mind away from the drop. "Thirty-eight. Thirty-nine." He reached the lip of the next ledge and hauled himself over it. The last tread had been solid. Above it the gully opened, and a short wall of dark rock stood between them and the long upper slope. "Forty," he said. "That's the foot of the Stair done. Forty steps, the old count, and we're through the worst of the rot. Bring the rope up."

"Forty-one," said Ila below him.

"I'm sorry?"

"Forty-one. There's a half-step under the first overhang, a low shelf, about a hand high. You stepped over it without weighting it. I counted it because my foot was on it." Her voice rose to him, flat and sure. "Forty-one, not forty."

He looked down. She was a small figure against the grey ice with the rope bright between them. Her head was tilted back, and her face wore the expression of a person who had no intention of being wrong.

He thought of his family's count, thirty-nine, recited to children as a rhyme for ghosts. He thought of the tower stair, with its half-step that no one counted. *A hand high. Stepped over.*

"It's an old shelf," he said. "Nobody counts it. My grandfather said a man who counts the half-step is a man who plans to fall."

"Your grandfather wasn't belaying," said Ila. "Forty-one."

"Forty-one," said Kunal, and it came out of him like a surrender, and he found he did not mind. The rope lay level between them, humming quietly through the ice. A true count was a true count. He had spent a life letting the hill's numbers stay a little wrong, because wrong numbers were a kind of lock.

He looked at the wall above him. It was a short pitch, thirty feet, with a crack up the middle and a line of rock-holds that he had always climbed on his left side.

"The next pitch is yours," he said.

Her head came up. "Mine?"

"You've the better count." He braced his boots against the ledge and took up her rope with both hands. "Go on, Surveyor. I'll hold."

He watched her think about it. He watched her decide that it was not a trap, and that if it was, the rope would tell her. Then she crossed the ledge, and put her fingers into the crack, and climbed with her small square competence into his stair.

He held her. All the way up, he held her, and the weight on the rope was a weight he found he wanted.

* * *

The head of the Stair was a wide shelf of rock under the lip of the cloud, bare in the wind, with the ice below them blue as a vein. The upper snowfield ran away white and level into the murk.

At the edge of the shelf stood a flat grey stone, waist-high, set upright in a socket of smaller ones. It was a route-keeper's marker, the kind that stood at the head of every hill stair. He had walked past it a hundred times.

It had writing on it.

The chalk was fresh. It had not been rained on, and the wind had not yet scoured the strokes. Someone had drawn them carefully in the old hill script, slow letters with hooked tails, the hand of a person who had been taught to write by his father with a stick in the dirt. Kunal knew the hand. He knew the pot that had held that chalk.

Ila came up beside him, breathing hard and pleased with herself. She saw his face, and stopped being pleased.

"It's in hill script," she said. "I can read the numerals. Not the rest." She held out her hand, the one without the mitten, and for a moment he thought she meant to take his. She pointed at the stone instead. "Read it to me."

He opened his mouth to say it was nothing. A route-keeper's warning, weather, a note to the next shepherd. He had a dozen smooth answers and each one would have cost him his fingers.

He had also been about to refuse. It would have been free. But he had stood at a burned doorway with a brass bell in his fist, and held a rope that held him, and he did not want to be a man who gave her a blank every time the page got difficult. The decision was half made. He had not finished making it.

His eyes had already gone to the letters, and his mouth did the rest.

"*They ask for the princes' way,*" said Kunal.

The words went out over the shelf and across the ice, and he could not take one of them back.

The wind dropped. In the stillness the stones under their boots gave out a low hum, level and unbroken, and Ila's hand had gone quite still in the air beside his sleeve.

"Hari wrote that," he said. "Before they burned him out. Or while they did."

"Who are *the princes*?" said Ila.

He did not answer. For a moment he could not have told her whether it was refusal or whether he simply had no voice. There was a chalk line under the sentence, a short flat stroke, and beneath it a second line that had been started and abandoned, a single hooked letter, and a smear where a hand had dragged through it.
