# Chapter 12: The Widow's Ladder

The fall had stopped in mid-leap.

Ila stood at the rim with the wind gone out of the morning and looked at it. Frozen water hung off the lip in grey ropes and sheets, thick as a man in places, thin as window glass in others. Light came through it green. Below the last of it, cloud lay in the gap like wool in a bin, and she could not see the bottom.

"Under," said Kunal.

"Under what?"

He crouched and showed her. Where the ice met the rock, a slot ran back, one body wide. Behind the curtain, a stair went down. Steps were cut in the wall, each the depth of a boot, each lip glazed clear. They slanted left and out of sight.

"The Widow's Ladder," he said. "The families of Ewe's Rest use it when soldiers come. So do the other hamlets on this side. No one below the Kuhl cut has walked it. No one in Taravan has heard the name."

"Who cut it?"

"Whoever needed to leave in a hurry. The hamlets re-cut it every thaw." He looked up at her. He had not mentioned the cave since they dug out, and neither had she, and the not-mentioning had a shape like a third person walking on the rope. "I'll anchor. You count. It's what we're each for."

She looked at the steps and felt the old, clean pull of a problem she knew how to solve.

"The rope is eight spans," said Ila. "The ice is a hundred feet, near enough. That's four pitches, and a ledge to stand on between each. Is there a ledge?"

"Three."

"Then say so. I'd like my facts in the order I need them."

"Three ledges," said Kunal, gravely. "Possibly four. I was eleven."

She laughed, once, and was glad of it. The morning eased a little.

He set the rope round a horn of rock above the slot and sat behind it with his boots braced and the line across his hip. His hands were steady. She stepped over the lip and into the green.

* * *

The first pitch was kind. The steps were deep and the ice outside them was a hand thick, and it hummed faintly in the sun like something alive. She went down facing the wall with the axe in her right hand and counted aloud, the way she always did.

"Eleven. Twelve. Wet on the lip. Thirteen."

"Slow," said Kunal's voice, from above and a long way off. The curtain made it hollow.

At the first ledge she stood on a shelf the width of a door and brought him down. At the second, the ice changed.

It leaned out. The wall under the fall bellied away from her, and the steps went down it at a slant she did not like, glazed over and weeping where the sun had touched them. The curtain was thin here. She could see the cloud through it, and she did not look at the cloud.

"This is the hard pitch," said Ila.

"Yes."

"You said three ledges."

"I said there were ledges. I didn't say they were evenly kind."

She set the axe-pick in the ice and tested the rope. It came taut. Above her, Kunal had moved to the rock horn with the line round his back and was sitting low, on the lee side, braced. She could hear him breathe.

"Count it for me," he said. "Aloud. I'll feel the rope."

So she did. She gave him every step as she took it, so he would know where her weight was before it came. "Twenty-two. Twenty-three. Lip's rounded, I'll stand on the heel. Twenty-four."

He began to sing.

It was not a song, quite. It was a low count with a lilt in it, the kind of thing a mother hums with a hand on a back. She understood after a moment that it was meant to keep her feet in time. A step to a line, a line to a breath.

*Count to the ledge and the ledge to the wall,*
*lean to the rock and let the water fall.*
*One for the cold hand, two for the warm.*
*The widow was carried down.*

"Thirty-one," said Ila. "Don't make it rhyme with anything. Thirty-two."

"It's a route-song. It has to rhyme or nobody remembers."

"Thirty-three."

The ice leaned. She leaned with it, and the song went round again, and she counted over it. At forty she put her left boot on a glaze she did not trust, and it held, and she let a long breath go through her teeth. At fifty-one the rope hummed against her wrist, not with a lie but with the strain of him holding. At fifty-seven her boot struck flat stone and the world stopped sloping.

"Fifty-seven on the pitch," she called. "Ledge. Come down."

She had just time to stand with her back to the rock and her heart going before the line slackened and he began to descend. He came down much faster than she had. He had done it before, she thought, as a boy, with soldiers or without them.

The song was still in her head. *The widow was carried down.*

She turned the word over.

"Carried," said Ila, when he reached the ledge.

"Mm?"

"In the song. What does it mean?"

"What it says." He shook the rope loose from his hip. "She had a bad hip and a sister who walked her. She was carried down the way a child is. By the hand, over the bad places. My grandmother sang it that way. In the hamlets, to carry is to see someone across."

"In the chronicles it means something else."

He looked up.

"I've read nineteen chronicles of the passes for the survey office. Every one." She said it flatly, because that was how it went. "*Carried* is a word for the dead. A man is *carried down at the thaw.* It means they found him and brought him in. It's the only way the clerks use it."

"Same word," said Kunal slowly.

"Same word, two valleys."

"One of us has the wrong end of it."

"Perhaps neither." She was not looking at him. She was looking at the ice, at the clean green edge of it where the sun came through. Her grandfather had been carried down. Her father's entry said so, in the post's own copperplate. She had read it at nineteen, over his shoulder, and she had thought it only a word.

She put it away, carefully, with the number sixty. The pile was growing. It was not a thing she could set a bearing on.

"Third pitch," she said. "Keep singing."

* * *

They reached the foot at dusk, with the cloud tearing off the slope in strips and the light going the colour of a bruise. There was a niche behind a boulder, lined with flat stones, where some family had sheltered often enough to build a hearth. Under the lowest slab lay a cache of dung cakes, dry as bread. The mountain's one custom, thought Ila, and the only one anyone ever kept.

They made a small fire. They ate, and said little, and he went out to look at the sky.

She sat with the field sheet on her knee.

It was the fair sheet, the one she would carry home. Not the notebook, which was her own, but the paper she had drawn on since Garh Neshar, and which she had sworn at nineteen would show the crown every way. She drew the day on it with the ruling pen. The lip of the Hollow Moon. The Two Aunts, in his words. The left fork, the cairn where she'd sat, the bearing she had taken of a long grey slope she had been steered away from. The rim above the fall.

She drew the rim. She drew the lip of it, and the shape of the ice, and the line of rock. She stopped with the pen over the slot.

It would be a short line. A body wide. She had the count, fifty-seven on the pitch and forty-odd on the rest, and she knew the slant and the three ledges, or four. She could ink the whole of it in the time it took the fire to settle. It was true. It was a thing she knew.

*If I drew less than I found I'd be lying on paper.*

She had said it herself, in a cave, and meant it.

She lifted the pen. The ink dried on the nib. She drew the rim of the fall as it looked from above, grey, unbroken, with nothing behind it, and went on to the next feature. It was the first line she had ever left out on purpose and then known she had left out. The one on the east, in the dark, she had followed him past and not decided about. This one she decided.

A sheet that was true and incomplete. She knew the phrase. She'd have called it a dodge in anyone else.

Kunal came back in, ducking under the stone. The cold came in with him. He sat across the fire from her and held out his hands to it, and said nothing, and did not look at the sheet.

"I didn't draw it," said Ila.

He went still.

"The Ladder. The slot. The steps. I didn't draw it." She held out her right hand, palm up, over the fire, so the light fell on it. "I wanted you to hear it from me and not find it out by a gap."

He looked at her hand for a long time. It was pink and ordinary. The nails were clean. The fire lay along the lines of the palm.

"Warm," said Kunal.

"Warm."

"You told me in the cave you would."

"I told you in the cave I would draw a way if I found one. I found one." She put the hand down. "I've left it off. I don't know yet if that's a lie, but it isn't one that costs anything. I've checked."

"Why?"

She had thought about that on the third pitch, on the ice, with fifty-seven steps going by. She did not think the reason would sound as good aloud as it had in her head.

"Because Gauri is in the main room at Ewe's Rest," said Ila. "And Mini. Because a girl of six has a place to go when soldiers come, and I would be the one who put it in the crown's hand." She stopped. "It isn't a principle. It's just that I know their names."

"That's all?"

"It's quite a lot."

He smiled, and it was a small, crooked thing, gone as soon as it came. "I didn't say it was small." He was quiet. "You know what it costs you. The oath. The sheet is audited at Taravan every spring. If an auditor finds a way you walked and didn't draw—"

"Then I'm a surveyor who forgot a way."

"You, forgot."

"I'd have to say it was an error," said Ila, "and it wouldn't be. That's the part I haven't solved. I'll have to find the sentence that's true, and I haven't." She heard her own voice go thin. "Chief Surveyor is a name on a sheet. I've worked eleven winters. I could lose it over a staircase."

"Then take it back. Draw it. I'll not ask you to carry my people's ways at the cost of your own."

"I won't."

"Ila."

"I've decided." It came out sharper than she'd meant. She made herself breathe. "I've said I'd tell you before I drew a way, and I'm telling you. I didn't draw it. That's all. Let me have the whole of it before you say anything kind."

Kunal sat with his hands open to the fire. When he spoke it was low.

"Promise me you never will."

The rope lay between them in the dust. She looked at it.

It was a fair request. It was the only request he could have made, and she saw how much he had wanted not to make it. She saw his hands, bare, steady, ready.

"I can't," said Ila.

His face did not change, but she saw the fire in his eyes stop moving.

"A promise made on this rope, if I broke it, strikes me as a great lie," she said. "You know that. I'd lose the hand, or worse. And I'd be promising against my own oath. The crown commissioned me to draw every way I find, and I swore it. If the crown asks for the sheet, the oath says draw. I won't promise to break that before I know what I'd do."

"You've just not drawn it."

"That's one staircase, on one evening. A promise is for all of them." She heard how reasonable it sounded. She hated it. "I won't make you a promise I might not keep. It's the one thing I can give you that isn't free to lie about."

He took that in.

"And besides," said Ila, "it isn't properly measured yet."

The words lay in the firelight. The rope did not hum. It stayed quiet and level as it always did for a true thing. She had spoken a fact. She had taken the bearing of the head of the Ladder only by eye, and the whole slant wanted a second reading, and she did not yet know the length of it to within a span.

And she had said it so that he would hear a way out. A *not yet.* An *it may be.*

He heard both. She watched him do it. His eyes went to her hands, and they were warm, and his mouth moved a little at the corner, the way it did when a sum came out and he did not like the answer.

"That's true," said Kunal.

"Yes."

"And it's a dodge."

"Yes."

"You might have said so."

"You'd have heard it anyway."

"I did." He let out a breath that fogged and thinned. "A map can be true and still leave a thing out. You'll tell me that."

"I'll tell you a blank isn't false."

"No," said Kunal. "A blank isn't false. It's only a decision."

She opened her mouth to answer and found there was nothing in it. She closed it. It was the sentence she'd have used on any man who tried it on her, and he had used it gently, as one hands back a dropped glove.

She did not concede it. She did not argue it. She sat with the dried ink going stiff on the pen and let it stand.

* * *

He rose a little later and went out to the edge of the shelf. After a time she put the sheet aside and followed, with the rope paying out between them, and stopped at his shoulder.

The cloud had gone. Stars lay thick and hard in a black sky, and the cold came up off the ground as it does after a storm, with a clean smell like iron and snow.

"The fall has a name," said Kunal, without turning. "The families don't give it to people from below. They call it the Long Braid."

She stood very still.

He had not asked her anything. He had not set a question beside it, to be paid for. It was the third night he might have, by their terms, and the first he hadn't. It was a name, held out, with nothing on the other side of the scale.

"That's your name for tonight," said Ila.

"It's just a name." He was still looking out. "I'd have given it you anyway."

"It'll go on a line."

"I know what happens to a true answer on this rope." His voice was almost gentle. "I know what you do with them. I've watched you. You write what's true, even when it's me." He looked at her then. "You may write it."

She took out the notebook, the one that was her own. She wrote it by starlight, exactly. *Long Braid.* The pencil scratched. Her hand was steady, and she saw that it was warm.

When she looked up, he had turned back to the edge.

"There," said Kunal.

Below the shelf the ground fell away into a vast pale floor, ringed by black mountains. Three long shapes lay on it, one after another, lighter than the snow round them, as if someone had set down three mirrors and walked off. Starlight lay in each, flat and perfect. Far out, from somewhere in the middle one, came a sound like a great drum struck once, deep and slow. It rolled along the basin and came back off the cliffs.

"The Glass Lakes," said Kunal.

He didn't say anything more. He didn't give her a bearing. He stood with his hands bare in the cold and let her look.

Her left hand went to her coat, to the lining over her ribs, and lay flat on the paper sewn there.

*Open when you stand at the Glass Lakes.*

She was not standing at them yet. They were a night's walk off and a long way down. But she could see them, and the ice rang again in the dark, and she did not take her hand away.
