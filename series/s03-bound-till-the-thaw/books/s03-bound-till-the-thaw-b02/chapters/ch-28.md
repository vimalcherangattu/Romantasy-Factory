# Chapter 28: Off a Cold Man

"Raghu's clerk wrote it," said Gauri. "A thin man with ink on his cuff. By lantern, at first light, on a scrap off Dhanu's own tally-hide." She did not look up from the pot. "He read it back twice, to see if it sounded like a man in a hurry."

The whistle lay against Kunal's breastbone on its frayed thong. He could not feel it with his dead fingertips, only its weight. It was small and warm from his skin, and it was not his.

"Tell it," he said.

"There isn't much." Gauri lifted the lid a finger's width and juniper smoke rolled out, sharp and clean. She set Ila's bare foot on a fold of her own shawl, close to the heat, and began to chafe the ankle with her broad thumb without being asked. "They came to the huts at moonrise, while you were up in the Eye. Raghu, the boy Pasang, the four from the cornice. They wanted names. Every keeper from the Stair to the Kuhl cut. Every runner. Every house that feeds a pair."

"And Dhanu."

"Dhanu gave them his ewes." Her mouth moved. It was not a smile. "By name. Oldest first, with their faults. The clerk had written eleven before he understood."

Ila made a small sound. Kunal did not.

"They took him out to the seam," said Gauri. "The black water, where the ice won't hold. They put a rope under his arms and let him down into it, and hauled him up, and asked again. He was not a young man. His heart stopped before the moon cleared the Kuhl shoulder." The thumb went on working the ankle. "That was the first night. Before there was a note to write."

Nobody spoke. The embers ticked.

"They took the whistle off him after," she said. "I watched them do it."

Kunal's hand had come up on its own and closed on the horn. He felt nothing through the grey tips.

"It was looped over the nail," he said. "By the thong. I took it down and I thought, *he's sent it so I'll know it's him.*" He heard himself breathe. "There was no walnut."

Gauri looked at him then.

"He gave me two. A boy with a red-thread walnut is Dhanu's word, and Dhanu stands behind whatever comes with it. This came with a whistle and no walnut, and I told myself a man with a blade at his back doesn't stop to find his tokens." His voice sounded far off, like someone else's in the next room. "There was no walnut because there was no Dhanu."

"No," said Gauri.

* * *

She left them soon after. She did it without ceremony, as she did everything. She set a skin of water by Ila's knee, pushed two flat loaves out of her shawl onto the stone, and went to sit at the cleft mouth with her back to them and her face to the lake.

Kunal stayed where he was. The sum he had refused all night had finally come and sat down in front of him, and he made himself read it.

"A ransom is for a man who is alive," he said. "Raghu never wanted a trade. He wanted a prince on that ice at dusk, standing where he was put. I moved the hour myself. I said, *dusk, so Dhanu doesn't wait another night.*" He turned his dead hand over. "He'd already stopped waiting."

"Kunal—"

"Let me finish, or I won't." He did not look at her. "The paper was never the point. A man who thinks he's bargaining stands still. That's what the sheets were for. I hand them over, I stand where he points, and the glass on the cornice finds my chest while his four finish it." He swallowed. "And you told me to look at his hand."

"I did."

"The fingertips. I couldn't read them. He'd told that boy Dhanu was only hurt, and the boy saw the truth, and Raghu laid a second lie over the first, there on the shore, loud, with his hand on his sword. It was the boy he was lying to. He needed Pasang's hands steady on a rope till it was done, and a boy who knew he was guarding a dead man's body would not have held."

"I knew the shore was lying," said Ila. "I didn't know which part."

"It was all of it." He made himself look up. "If you'd said the sheets were in your coat, I'd have given them over at the water's edge and believed the trade. I'd have stood still. Because I thought they were ash, I had nothing in my hands. So I said *take me,* and when he lifted two fingers I ran. And I ran with you on my arm, calling the ice." He drew a breath that shook. "The two who cut the chord went through the seam. That was the short way, the way a man in a hurry thinks is safe. It would have been mine."

Her hands lay on her knees, in the ember light, with the fingertips pink.

"Your lie cost you two toes," said Kunal, "and it kept me alive. It didn't cost Dhanu his."

"Nothing could have."

"No. I know that now." He looked at the fire. "I couldn't know it then. That's what I'm trying to say. You couldn't have told me."

"I could have said it was a trap."

"You'd have said it with nothing to show, and I'd have told you the sum was mine." He said it plainly, and it hurt less than he had expected, which was its own kind of shame. "You asked me twice about the letter and I made you pay for the asking. You'd have been right and I'd not have heard it. So you went alone, with your hands in your sleeves, and carried the cold, because you couldn't trust me to listen." He stopped. "I wouldn't have listened."

Ila did not say *thank you,* or *it's all right.* Her mouth opened, shut, and opened again.

"No," she said. "I don't think you would."

* * *

She pulled her pattu round and sat up straighter. He watched her do it, a woman about to hand over a ledger.

"I said I'd tell you when I could pay it properly," said Ila. "I'm early. I know."

"You don't owe me—"

"I do. I owed it you before the Eye." She laid both hands flat on her knees, palms down, where he could see every finger. "Watch them."

He watched.

"The letter is the queen's, in her own hand, under the archive seal. It names my grandfather. Warden Harish, of the Shilak post. The chronicle says he went out in a night storm to check the cairns and was carried down at the thaw." Her fingers stayed pink. "The letter says the storm was an excuse. He went to the Under-Cairn, beneath the Shilak Cairn, where the Old Bargain is paid. And he paid it. Then he walked out into the snow and took off his gloves and laid them palm to palm on a flat stone, square, with the fingers pointing the way he'd gone."

"The gloves your father spoke of."

"Yes."

"Why does the queen tell you?"

"Because the next spring the crown wrote the Closing Law. So nobody would cross in winter and ask what had happened to a warden at the thaw." Her voice was level. "She wants what's under the cairn found. She wants it read. The crown has never dared. The letter says what is owed is written down there." She breathed. "It says one more thing. *Tell no one Neshar's part.*"

The embers shifted. He had known, he found. He had known since she laid her left hand flat on her coat at the lakeside cairn. He had only not wished to.

"The sheets," he said.

"Not ash. Sewn into the side seam of my pattu, above the hem. All of them. I didn't burn anything." Her hands had not moved. They were still pink. "I'm sorry for the lie. I'm not sorry I told it."

He heard what she did not say, and what she was about to. He saw her mouth shape it, the soft thing, the *it was long ago, it wasn't you, it was his time.*

"Udai," he said, before she could. "My grandfather. He walked a warden up the princes' way in winter, sixty winters ago, to the place under the mountain. And let him pay." He kept his voice flat, because it was that or none. "My family walked your grandfather to his death, Ila, and buried it for sixty winters."

"Kunal. He was—"

"Don't make it smaller. Not for me." He looked at his burnt palms. "Pritam told me the story over supper the week after my father's funeral. Udai showed the way once, to one guest, and was never the same. *The way kills those who share it.* He made it the family rule that night. No prince shows the way to wife, son or friend. I was nine. I thought it was about my grandfather's grief." He laughed, and it was not a laugh. "It was about a grave. The guest died, and my family built a rule round the hole so nobody would look in."

"You didn't know."

"I didn't. I'd have been the next man to keep the rule." He lifted his eyes. "Your hands."

She held them up. The fingertips were warm and clear.

* * *

"I said you'd let Dhanu die for your queen's map," said Kunal. "In the voice I use on men I mean to bleed. I knew what it would do when I said it. I was wrong, and I said it anyway."

"I know."

"I'm not asking you to forget it."

"I won't." She did not look away. "I lied to you because I didn't want to argue it out. I could have made you hear me, or tried, with every figure I had. I decided you wouldn't, and decided it for you. It happened to be true. It wasn't mine to find out that way." She said it as she would give a distance. "I'm not saying that to be forgiven."

"Good. I told you I don't forgive you." He turned his ruined hands palm up between them. "I'll amend it. I don't excuse you, and I don't excuse me. I see what you did. I see how you did it, and why, and what it cost."

"That's enough."

"It isn't," said Kunal. "But it's true."

Silence was free. For once he did not hate it. He sat in it and found it held weight.

"The strip on your hem," he said. "The one you offered."

She had it off her shirt in a breath. She bound his right palm with it, knotting it flat, her warm fingers quick and sure over the broken skin. The wool of the pattu brushed his wrist. When she tied it off she did not let go at once.

"Do you still want to go under the mountain?" he asked.

"Yes."

"With your foot."

"With my foot." She turned her head toward the dark passage, where the blue stones lay faint along the gravel. "My grandfather paid something, and somebody wrote it down. I would rather read it than be told it. I've spent my life drawing what other people could see. I want to read the one thing nobody drew."

"The Meadow Cairn won't wait for Dhanu to be buried."

"No."

"Then I'll take you." He held out his bare right wrist. "Tie it."

She took up the grey wool where it lay across the gravel. Her fingers did the warden's double hitch, two turns, a tuck, a locking half-turn, clean on his wrist, as she had done it at the Meadow Cairn with more hope than she had now. Her end was already tied. It lay between them like a line drawn on a sheet.

* * *

Gauri came back when the light had gone a plain grey. She had a lamp under her arm, a skin of oil and a cloth sack that smelled of bread. She set them down in a row, the way a woman sets out a trader's goods, and did not look at the rope.

"I'll stay at the huts," she said. "Till the thaw."

"Gauri," said Kunal.

"I've laid stones on him at the fold. The ground won't take him till the thaw, and he'd not like to be carried." Her eyes were dry. "And someone has to keep the hearth lit for the next pair through. He'd have said so. He'd have said it was no use hating a lamp."

"Let me send someone."

"Send nobody." She said it gently. "Go and do what you've gone for."

He undid the whistle. It took him some time, with the thong stiff and the heel of his hand clumsy against the knot. He held it out.

She looked at it. She did not take it.

"Keep it," said Gauri. "Two long and a short. Blow it when it's clear."

He closed his hand round the horn.

"The pen wall at Ewe's Rest," he said. "Ila showed you where the north side was short. Footed, it'll hold this spring." He had no idea why he was saying it, only that it was the nearest solid thing. "The first pair of lambs born there, whoever's they are. I'll send them to you. The first spring pair."

The rope lay across his wrist. As the words left him it moved against his skin, a low, level hum, as even as a drawn string. It ran up through his arm and into his chest, and across the gravel Ila's head came up. She felt it too.

Gauri heard it. He saw her hear it.

"Then I'll expect them," she said. She picked up the shawl, folded it, and laid it across Ila's feet.

Ila's boots were dry. Gauri had brought those too, with the sack. Kunal pulled them on over the ruined toes while Ila gripped the wall and said nothing, and when he looked up her hands were still on the stone, still pink in the new light.

He lit the lamp from the embers. The flame caught, steadied, and threw their two shadows long down the passage, over the blue stones and the dark beyond them.

"Eighty-one paces to the first turn," said Ila. "Don't count aloud. I'll do it."

"You'll have to forgive me if I count anyway."

"I forgave you," said Ila, "for that, a week ago."

He took the lamp. The rope hummed once more, faintly, between them, and they went back into the cleft.
