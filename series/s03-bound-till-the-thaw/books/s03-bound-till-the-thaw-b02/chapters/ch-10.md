# Chapter 10: The Princes' Way

Raghu's men had dug at the foot of the door for most of a night, and they had dug in the wrong place.

Kunal stood in the wreck of it and counted the holes. Nine, in a ring round the cairn's footing, each deep as a man's forearm, the spoil heaped beside them and frozen to a grey crust. They had levered up the great flat stone at the base and turned it face down. Someone had scraped its underside with a knife point, hunting for a cut sign. They had found lichen. A pick-head lay in the scree where its haft had snapped in the frost.

"They wanted something under it," said Ila. She stood on a fallen slab with the rope slack round her boots. "Not a body. You'd dig for a body once. This is a search."

"A mark," said Kunal. "A carved marker, the kind your office sets in rock. *Here the way begins.*" He turned a clod of crust with his boot. "They think a way is a thing somebody carved."

"And it isn't?"

"There's no mark on this cairn. There never was."

He heard the relief in his own voice and was ashamed of how much of it there was. Hari had been taken, or burned out, or worse, and the last thing he had chalked was a warning. But Raghu had come up here with picks and a lantern and gone home with lichen. He had the name. He had a notion that the way began at some cairn of this valley. He lacked the beginning. Whoever had sold him the Stair had not sold him that.

*Yet,* said a small voice in Kunal's head, in his uncle's silken tones.

"Then I'll ask now, while it's quiet," said Ila. She had the field book shut under her arm. "Hari's chalk said *They ask for the princes' way.* You read it aloud and the stone hummed level. I wrote down the words and not the thing. What is the princes' way?"

There it was. He had known since the Stair that she would ask, and he had spent four days building the answer that cost least. He looked at it now, whole, in the grey light, and found he did not want to hand her the cheap one.

"What do you think it is?" he said, and heard himself, and winced. "No. That's a habit. Forgive it."

"I'll forgive it once."

"It's a route," said Kunal. "My family keeps it. Not a road, and not a secret I invented. My father kept it, and his father. And it has one rule. No one of our blood walks it between first snow and thaw."

Ila looked at him for a moment. "And you're taking me toward it in winter."

He had expected the question. It still landed like a stone on a sore place.

"Yes," he said. "I'm breaking the rule. I'd rather break it with my eyes open than let Raghu break it blind. He has the name, and he has men who dig. If someone is going to walk it this winter, I want it to be someone who knows what she's looking at." He made himself add the rest. "And the rope won't let me wait for the thaw. That's a reason too. It isn't the best one."

His fingertips stayed pink. He did not look at them, and she did, and she nodded once.

"Where does it go?"

"I'll keep that."

Her eyes dropped to his hands. They were bare, pink at the fingertips, and still. "That's free," she said, to herself, and waited.

He could have stopped. He knew exactly where the stopping place was. But she had put a sentence on a sheet last night and told him so before he could find it, and a man who takes that and gives back only fog is a poorer thing than he likes to be.

"It begins at the Glass Lakes," said Kunal.

Ila's left hand went to her coat. It lay flat against the ribs, over the lining, for the length of one breath, as a woman checks a purse in a crowd. Then it came away and she looked at the cairn.

He noted it and filed it. A pocket of sheets, he thought. She was a woman who counted her paper.

"Why that rule?" she said. "A family doesn't forbid itself its own road without a reason."

"No." He sat down on the slab opposite hers, because the story was not one he told standing. "There's a reason. It's a story, and it's the only one we have, so you must take it as the family tells it."

"Tell it as they tell it."

"My grandfather was Prince Udai. When he was young, before he was anyone, he walked the way in winter with a guest." The old cadence came up in his mouth, his grandmother's, with its stops where she had put them. "He came down alone. He never went up again. Not in winter, and not in summer either, though he lived a long time after, and kept the way, and trained my father to keep it. After that the rule stood."

"The guest?"

"Is just *the guest.*"

"Who?"

"No one says." He picked a splinter of slate off the slab. "I asked my grandmother when I was eight. She said it as though there were a stone in the word. My father told me, before he died, that she'd never told him either. My grandfather died without saying, and he was not a man who forgot things."

"When was this?"

"Sixty winters ago. The year before the Closing Law was written, as the old women reckon it."

Ila said nothing. It was not a pause for effect. She had simply stopped, pencil case half out of her pocket, eyes on nothing, the way he had seen her stop at the edge of a slope.

"Sixty," she said. It was not a question.

"Near enough. You've gone quiet."

"I'm counting."

Of course she was counting. He nodded.

"It isn't much," he said. "A prince walked up with someone, and came back without. That's the whole of what my family knows about the most important thing it owns."

"It's a good deal."

"It's a hole with a rule round it."

"Most things I draw are," said Ila. "Holes with rules round them."

He laughed, once, short, and was surprised by it.

Then what he'd said came down on him in order. *Hollow Moon,* on the cliff, had slipped out careless, and she had refused to write it until he said it again on purpose. *Glass Lakes* he had given on purpose, with the stopping place in plain view. He had meant it, so the name was hers to write. And her hand had gone to her coat.

"May I write the lakes?" said Ila.

She was asking, and so the choice sat in his hands again, where he had left it on the cliff.

"You may write the name," he said slowly. "Not a bearing. I haven't given you one."

"No. I'll want one. Not today." She opened the book and wrote two words, the pencil scratching in the cold. "I'm telling you now, so you know where I stand. I'll ask for the place of them, and you may refuse, and I'll mark the refusal."

"You're very fair."

"I'm exact. It sometimes looks the same."

He watched her write. The rope between them lay along the slab and did not hum. He had given a true answer, and a line was on the crown's sheet because of it, and he sat in the broken doorway of his own people's country and felt it as he would feel a door left unlatched behind him. He felt it, and left the latch alone.

* * *

Cloud came down from the north rim before the sun was well up. It poured over the lip like milk over a bowl's edge and filled the Hollow Moon to the knee, then to the waist, and the stones went grey and soft in it.

Beyond the ruined door, the route climbed a stony shoulder toward the high pastures of Kuhl. Kunal looked up it and saw nothing. No blue thread, no beads of glow, only wet grey stone going up into wet grey air.

"It's gone," said Ila. "All of it. They've taken the whole road."

"No. The door's stretch forgot itself. Only that." He shouldered his pack. "A keeper's cairn holds the road from itself to the next keeper. Raghu broke one. He didn't break the one after. We have to cross the dark between, and I can do that."

"By what?"

"By my feet, and a song."

He sang it under his breath, the way he had been taught it, a dull sing-song with the tune filed off:

*Door to the shoulder, shoulder to the pair,*
*count the slate steps and don't look where.*

"Don't look where?" said Ila.

"It's a rhyme. It rhymes."

"It's a very bad rhyme."

"It's a very old one."

She counted the slate steps. He heard her do it, a murmur at his back, and at ninety-one she said, "The song says nothing about ninety-one."

"The song allows for the dark."

It was two hundred paces by her count, and it was the longest walk of his life that did not hold a knife. Cloud swallowed the rope a few spans behind him and gave it back as a pull at his wrist. Twice his boot found loose scree where the memory said slate, and twice he stopped, and turned, and found the line again by the lean of the ground. Then the cloud ahead of him went faintly blue.

An old cairn stood on the shoulder, waist-high and whole, and round it hung a skin of light like frost with a fever. Beyond it, in the white, a second blue bead shone, and beyond that a third.

"Cairn-light," said Ila, and let out a long breath. "I thought they'd taken it."

"They took the door's piece. This one was never theirs."

"That's a comforting thing to say."

"It's also what I believe."

The shoulder climbed, and levelled. At its top stood a double cairn, two heaps within a stone's throw of each other, and from between them the thread of light went two ways.

Kunal stopped.

The right-hand fork ran north-east along a shelf of the rim, straight and clean, and on its first hundred paces the snow had been trodden hard. He read it without wanting to. Four men, two pairs, loaded, a day gone, perhaps two. They had followed the broken line to the first place it forked, as men follow a rumour. Beyond the shelf the fork dropped toward the Kuhl cut, and from the shoulder over it a man with a glass could look straight down into the head of the lakes.

The left-hand fork went round. It was a curve along the lip of the bowl, high and cold and slow, and it would cost them half a day.

Half a day was not a small coin. Raghu's two pairs, going straight, would stand on that overlook a full day ahead of them. And Dhanu, Gauri and little Mini, who had gone on by the short way, were expected at the lakes. They would walk down to the shore and meet Raghu's glass before any friend arrived to warn them.

"Which?" said Ila.

She said it neutrally, the compass in her hand, and he understood that she was giving him the choice because it was his to make and, he thought, because she wished to watch him make it.

*She would stand on that shoulder,* he thought, *and take a bearing in one breath. She would turn round with the sun behind her and see the lakes laid out like a plate, and a bearing is not a name. A bearing is the place.* He had given the name freely. He could not give the place the same way, in one sweep of her pencil, on a morning that was not his choosing.

And Dhanu's party would have a day alone against Raghu. He weighed that, and found it heavy, and found it was not enough to turn him. That was the thing to be ashamed of later.

"The left," said Kunal. "It's the longer way. It's the quieter one."

"Raghu's men went right."

"They did."

"Is that why we go left?"

"It's one reason."

She considered that. She did not ask the second reason, and he led.

* * *

The lip of the bowl was a bad road for a prince with something to hide. It was narrow and windy and open on both sides, and in the cloud there was nothing to look at but the next heap of stones and the rope going out ahead of him. He kept to the side that faced the bowl. Whenever the cloud thinned and the north-east began to show, a long slope falling away toward the Kuhl cut, he found a ridge of rock to put between it and her.

He did it three times. He did it kindly, without a word, as a man steers a guest past a door he would rather not open.

Somewhere behind him the pencil began to move.

He kept walking another fifty paces, to the next cairn, and let the wind have a say. The blue thread brightened under his boot. She was coming up on the rope behind him, steady, her stride measured, and in the lee of the cairn he stopped, because she had.

Ila sat down on the stones with the book on her knee. She looked nothing like angry. He had seen her angry once, at a clerk, in a letter she had read aloud at the drafting table, and this was another face altogether. It was the face she wore for a slope she was going to cross.

"You've kept the north-east shut behind a rock since the fork," said Ila. "Three times. Once I thought it was wind."

"A rock keeps what it keeps."

"That's a question with the question taken out."

He had no answer to that. He stood with his hands bare at his sides, in the cloud, and the cairn hummed under his shoulder, low and level, as it had hummed all night.

"You're walking me around something," said Ila.

He could have said a hundred things. He could have asked her what she meant, and what she thought it was, and how a surveyor knew where a prince's loyalties lay when she could not see the slope she was being kept from. He could have said *a prince has his reasons* in his uncle's voice, and been believed by anyone but her.

"Yes," said Kunal.

The stones did not change their note. He looked at his fingertips because he could not help it, and they were pink.

Ila nodded. She turned a page, wrote a line, and held the book out to him across the rope's slack.

*K. avoids NE fork,* it said, in her small square hand, with the date beside it and the bearing of the cairn. Under it she had left the place for a reason, ruled, empty.

"You might have put *hides,*" he said.

"You didn't hide it. You said yes." She took the pencil from behind her ear. "If you'd said no I would have written *hides.* I write what's true, Kunal. Even when it's you."

He read it twice. It was accurate. That was the worst of it, and the strangest thing was how little he wished to take it back.
