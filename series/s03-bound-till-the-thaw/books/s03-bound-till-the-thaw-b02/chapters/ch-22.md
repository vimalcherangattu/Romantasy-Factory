# Chapter 22: Walnuts

Dhanu put a walnut in Kunal's palm and folded the dead fingers over it himself, one at a time, like a man closing a purse.

"Feel that?"

"No," said Kunal.

"Then look at it."

Kunal looked. The shell was dark and ordinary, with a red thread wound twice round the seam and knotted off. Dhanu laid a second one beside it. They sat just inside the herders' door, with the scree spur at their backs hiding it from the far shore. Between their boots a clay pot stood upside down over a single banked coal. It gave off no flame, no smoke, and a small orange line at the rim.

"My word without a walnut isn't from me," said Dhanu. "You know the rule."

"I made half the council swear to it."

"You taught it to them. My people made it, ten generations back. Different thing." He lowered himself onto the upturned stool with the good right foot first, the stiff left swinging after. "A boy comes with a walnut, it's mine. A boy comes without, I never said it, I was asleep, I'm a lame man with a bad leg. Even if it's my own runner. Even if it's you at my door, doing my voice."

"Why two?"

"One for when you're stupid." Dhanu's face did not move. "I brought a second because I've known you thirty years."

Kunal laughed, and it hurt somewhere under the ribs. He shut the dead hand on the walnut. Behind him the hut was dark. Ila slept on the shelf in her boots, because they went into the Eye at moonrise. The rope lay across the boards, untied, a long pale line from his place at the door to her wrist. Eight spans. He had paced it twice without meaning to.

"Send one, and I come," said Dhanu. "I don't ask why. Not till after."

"Thank you."

"Don't. It's a bad bargain. Half the time you'll be wrong." He held his hands over the pot's warmth. "Now say it."

"Say what?"

"What you've decided. You've been decided since noon. You sat on that scree with your jaw set and your mouth shut tight as a fist."

Kunal watched the orange line. He thought of the sheet Ila had drawn that morning, with the shore path on it, thin and certain as a goat track across new snow.

"I'm taking her through the princes' way," he said. "All of it. Every turn I know."

Dhanu was quiet for a time.

"No," he said.

* * *

"I thought you'd say that," said Kunal.

"Then you thought right, and you can stop there." Dhanu never raised his voice, and he didn't now. He leaned in. "Listen, because I'll say it once and then again. She's the crown's surveyor. Her commission has the queen's own words in it. *The crown may hold every way.* She read it to you herself."

"She said she doesn't know what holding means."

"I know what it means. A garrison means it. A garrison is a house with soldiers in it, and the house goes where the way goes." He tapped the stool with one finger. "You give her the princes' way, and she draws it, and she's honest, so it's true. Then it sits in a drawer in Taravan, with a key you'll never be given. And one winter a captain opens that drawer and sends two hundred men through the mountain in a night. Can the villages shut a door against that?"

It was a fair argument. It was better than the one Kunal had made himself on the long walk from the lake, and he took his time answering it.

"The hills have kept that way sixty winters, and more," said Dhanu. "Your grandfather kept it. Your father kept it. They didn't keep it because they were mean, Kunal. It's the one door we have."

"And my uncle kept it for the same reason, he'd say."

"Your uncle kept it for a throne."

"He kept it exactly as you describe. In one man's head, with nobody to check him." Kunal turned the walnut over. "That's the part I can't get round. A door only one person can open isn't ours. It's his."

"That's a speech."

"Then here's a fact." He said it flat, the way Ila would. "The crown's cover sheet to the council names a way beneath the Shilak Cairn. I'd never heard of any such way. Raghu's men have dug nine holes round the door cairn on the Hollow Moon, looking for a carved marker that was never there. They're hunting the princes' way with picks. And the queen put a letter in Ila's hem that she won't show me, and she wouldn't have sewn it in so carefully if it didn't matter." He opened his hand. "It's already out, old friend. Somebody in Taravan knows it exists. Somebody in the hills is paid to dig. All that's left to argue about is whether the one person on this mountain who lied to my face once and owned it by dawn gets the true line, or whether Raghu draws a bad one and sells it."

"Secrecy's failed, you're saying."

"It failed before I was born. I only noticed."

The Drum boomed on the lake, a long note that rolled up the scree and through the planks of the hut. Neither of them looked round.

"It's a good case," said Dhanu at last. "I still say no."

"I know you do. I'm glad. If you didn't, I'd be doing this because it was easy."

* * *

Dhanu lifted the pot with one finger, checked the coal, and set it down again. When he spoke, he had changed the ground, which he did when a quarrel was done and the work was not.

"Tell me the petition."

So Kunal told it. He had rehearsed it on the scree, with the wind taking half of it, and it came out smoother than he liked. "When the thaw council sits at the Shilak Cairn, I stand up and ask two things of the crown. First, no garrisons in the hill villages. Not on the Stair, not on the Moon, not in any fold the council names. If a warden wants a hearing, he asks the council, and the council answers."

"And second?"

"A council seat at Taravan. One voice of ours in the queen's own hall, with a right to speak before any order touches the passes." He heard how it sounded in the open air, and it still sounded good. "Neshar a self-governing valley under the crown. Not outside it. I don't want a war, and she can't afford one. This gives her the passes safely open, and it gives us a door she can't close from the capital without our hearing."

"It's a fine petition."

"But."

"But they'll ask." Dhanu did not look away. "Old Bhim will stand up first, because he always does. And he won't ask whether it's fair. He'll ask what you gave the crown for it. A queen doesn't hand over a seat in her hall for a speech."

Kunal had known that question was waiting, as one knows the next stair in the dark. He had put off stepping onto it.

"I'll tell them the truth," he said. "That I showed a crown surveyor the way. That it was mine to show. That I gave it before I knew what she'd do, and the council can hold me to it."

"They'll hold you to it, all right." Dhanu said it without heat. "Some will say you sold the hills. Some will say you were a fool in love, and that'll be the kinder half. Let me tell you what none of them will say, because none of them have watched you as long as I have."

"Go on."

"You have never given anyone anything you couldn't take back."

It dropped into the quiet like a stone down a well.

"That's not true," said Kunal. "The silver—"

"Clipped at the corner so no one could trace it to you. Nineteen bits, and you never once said your own name to the woman who ate because of them. The names you give the council, you give in pieces, so none of them holds the whole." Dhanu lifted the sheep-knuckles from his belt and let them lie in his palm. "Seven winters I taught you the bones. You played me every game and never once let me win, nor lost on purpose. You played so it couldn't cost you."

"I broke a promise to her this morning. I paid for it."

"You broke it so you could stop counting, and you paid in flesh you'd already priced." Dhanu closed his hand. "This one's different. You put a way in her head, there's no clipping it back. She can't forget it. You can't have it back. If she's the crown's, it's the crown's, and the hills pay."

Kunal sat very still.

"I know it," he said.

"Do you?"

"I've known it since the Eye, when I was twelve and a man turned me round by the shoulders." He looked at his bandaged hands. "That's why I want to. If I can't take it back, then I've finally given it. Not lent it. Not parcelled it, or priced it. I want to find out what I am without the lock on."

Dhanu studied him a long time. The limp was in the way he sat, the right foot planted and the left held out, as though even at rest he were walking round something.

"The council will want your head."

"They can have a hearing."

"And if she draws it and takes it down the mountain and hands it over?"

"Then I was wrong, and the hills will say so, and I'll stand in the hall and hear it." He said it slowly. "I'd rather be wrong that way than right in the way I've been."

Dhanu grunted. It was the sound he made when a thing was settled and he had no intention of praising it. He heaved himself up, and the left foot swung out and round.

"Ask her what she'll do with it. Not before. After. I'll say that without a walnut, so you needn't take it from me."

"I'll take it anyway."

"You're stupid enough already." He went off down the scree with the whistle swinging on its thong, and the dark folded behind him.

* * *

Kunal stayed by the pot until the sound of him was gone.

There was a third walnut in his coat. He had carried it since the day the council fixed the thaw date, in the lining over his ribs, wrapped in a scrap of grey wool. It was plain and dry, with no thread on it. He took it out without asking himself why he had brought it.

Mini had asked for one. That was all. Six years old on the pony in front of Gauri, with her fist in the horse's mane, she had said, *Uncle Kunal, can I have a walnut with a string, like the runners? I want my own.* And he had said yes and, by the stones, had said *When I'm back.* It had been a light thing to say, the way men say it to children. He had not thought of it again until last night, when he lay awake with Ila's cold feet against his shin and did not count.

A hank of red thread lay in his pouch. It was Gauri's, a gift from the winter before. He pulled out a length with his teeth, because the fingers would not pinch it, and laid it round the seam.

The dead tips of his right hand sat on the shell like the grey ends of burned candles. He could see them move. He could not feel what they held. The thread slid, caught, and slid out again, and the first knot came out a lump the size of a seed.

He undid it with his teeth, and began again.

It was a simple knot. A runner's turn, a loop, a tuck. He had tied it ten thousand times, in the dark, in a gale, with half a hand frozen. The second try came out lopsided, the thread riding up off the seam and leaving the walnut naked on one side. He pulled it loose and sat a moment with the thread dangling.

Pinku came to mind, in the walnut grove at Ranu at moonrise, last winter, with a clay lamp too big for his fist. Kunal had tied that one in a hurry, by lantern light, with all ten fingers. It had been the best knot he knew.

The third try he did slowly. He held the walnut against his knee, trapped the thread under the heel of his hand, and made each turn with the whole arm instead of the fingers. The knot came out round and fat and slightly off true, with an ugly tail he could not cut straight. It held. He tugged it, and it did not slip.

It was the worst knot he had tied since he was eight.

He sat with it on his knee, smiling, stupidly, at a nut.

"Mini will like it," said Ila, from the shelf.

He had not heard her wake. She sat up with her hair flat on one side and her boots laced, and the rope slack across the boards. It had stayed within its eight spans the whole night. She looked at the walnut, and then at his hand, and said nothing about the fingers, which was her own kind of manners.

"It's bad," said Kunal.

"It's very bad. It's lopsided." She crossed and sat on the stool where Dhanu had been, close enough that the coal lit one side of her face. "She'll show it to everyone."

"That's the idea."

"Is it from you?"

"It's from me." He turned it so she could see the tail. "No one else could make it so ugly."

She laughed, short and sudden and loud, and it rang off the scree like a dropped pan. Kunal's head went round to the doorway before he could stop it. Beyond the spur the far shore lay dark and still. Out on the lake the ice answered her. Nothing else did.

Ila had already lowered her voice. "Sorry," she said, and held out her palm, and he put the walnut in it. She took the tail between two fingers, drew the pencil knife from her sleeve, and cut it off square at the knot, one stroke, without asking. She tucked the scrap of red in her sleeve and gave the walnut back. The tail was clean now, and the knot sat round and fat and honest under it.

He put it in his coat, over the heart, in its wool.

The coal had sunk to a dull orange. The moon was low behind the cliff, and above the Eye the stars stood thick and cold. Raghu's four were somewhere on the far shore, with their spyglasses and their patience. In an hour the line would show, blue, to the two of them and no one else.

Kunal looked at the woman beside him. She had a pencil lead in her sleeve and a letter in her hem and eleven years of true sheets in her head. He did not know what she meant to do with the way. He had not asked, and her one promise covered a letter, not this. He meant to give it before he heard her answer.

"Ila."

"Yes."

"I'm taking you into the cleft tonight." He set his hands open on his knees, and the words came out whole, with nothing kept back. "The whole way I know, every turn, in order. And then past it. Past the place where my uncle turned me round. I've never been there. We'll find out together what's on the far side of it."

She said nothing. The wind came down off the shoulder and slid round the spur. He watched her face go still, the way it did over a closure that would not come out. The pencil lead in her fingers had stopped mid-roll, and under her breath, so low he almost missed it, she began to count paces.
