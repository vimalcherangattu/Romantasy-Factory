# Chapter 22: Walnuts

Dhanu put a walnut in Kunal's palm and folded the dead fingers over it himself, one at a time, like a man closing a purse.

"Feel that?"

"No," said Kunal.

"Then look at it."

Kunal looked. The shell was dark and ordinary, with a red thread wound twice round the seam and knotted off. Dhanu laid a second one beside it. The juniper fire popped between them, and the smoke went up straight and sweet into a sky full of stars.

"My word without a walnut isn't from me," said Dhanu. "You know the rule."

"I made half the council swear to it."

"You taught it to them. I made it. Different thing." He lowered himself onto the log with the stiff right foot first, the left swinging after. "A boy comes with a walnut, it's mine. A boy comes without, I never said it, I was asleep, I'm a lame man with a bad leg. Even if it's my own runner. Even if it's you at my door, doing my voice."

"Why two?"

"One for when you're stupid." Dhanu's face did not move. "I brought a second because I've known you thirty years."

Kunal laughed, and it hurt somewhere under the ribs. He shut the dead hand on the walnut. Through the open door behind him, the herders' hut was a square of dark. Ila slept on the shelf in her boots, because they went into the Eye at moondown. The rope lay across the threshold in the ash-grey light, untied, a long pale line from his place at the fire to her wrist. Eight spans. He had paced it twice without meaning to.

"Send one, and I come," said Dhanu. "I don't ask why. Not till after."

"Thank you."

"Don't. It's a bad bargain. Half the time you'll be wrong." He held his hands to the fire. "Now say it."

"Say what?"

"What you've decided. You've been decided since noon. You sat on that scree with your jaw set and your mouth shut tight as a fist."

Kunal looked at the flames. Juniper burned with a clean, short heat, and the resin spat. He thought of the sheet Ila had drawn that morning, with the shore path on it, sharp as a hair.

"I'm taking her through the princes' way," he said. "All of it. Every turn I know."

Dhanu said nothing for a time.

"No," he said.

* * *

"I thought you'd say that," said Kunal.

"Then you thought right, and you can stop there." Dhanu did not raise his voice. He never did. He leaned in, and the firelight caught the grey in his beard. "Listen to me, because I'll say it once and then I'll say it again. She's the crown's surveyor. Her commission has the queen's own words in it. *The crown may hold every way.* She read it to you herself."

"She said she doesn't know what holding means."

"I know what it means. A garrison means it. A garrison is a house with soldiers in it, and the house goes where the way goes." He tapped the log with one finger. "You give her the princes' way, and she draws it, and she's honest, so it's true. Then it sits in a drawer in Taravan, in a hand you'll never see, with a key you'll never be given. And one winter a captain opens that drawer and sends two hundred men through the mountain in a night. Do you think the villages can shut a door against that?"

It was a fair argument. It was better than the one Kunal had made himself on the long walk from the lake, and he did not answer it at once.

"The hills have kept that way sixty winters, and more," said Dhanu. "Your grandfather kept it. Your father kept it. They didn't keep it because they were mean, Kunal. They kept it because it's the one door we have."

"And my uncle kept it for the same reason, he'd say."

"Your uncle kept it for a throne."

"He kept it exactly as you describe. In one man's head, with nobody to check him." Kunal turned the walnut over. "That's the part I can't get round, Dhanu. A door only one person can open isn't ours. It's his."

"That's a speech."

"Then here's a fact." He made himself say it plainly, as Ila would. "The crown's cover sheet to the council names a way beneath the Shilak Cairn. I read it. I'd never heard of any such way. Raghu's men have dug nine holes round the door cairn on the Hollow Moon, looking for a carved marker that was never there. They're hunting the princes' way with picks. And the queen put a letter in Ila's hem that she won't show me, and she wouldn't have sewn it in so carefully if it didn't matter." He opened his hand. "It's already out, old friend. Somebody in Taravan knows it exists. Somebody in the hills is paid to dig. All that's left to argue about is whether the one person on this mountain who's never lied to my face gets the true line, or whether Raghu draws a bad one and sells it."

"Secrecy's failed, you're saying."

"It failed before I was born. I only noticed."

The Drum boomed on the lake, a long note that rolled up the scree and through the planks of the hut. Neither of them looked round.

"It's a good case," said Dhanu at last. "I still say no."

"I know you do. I'm glad. If you didn't, I'd be doing this because it was easy."

* * *

Dhanu fed a stick to the fire. When he spoke again, he had changed the ground, which he did when a quarrel was done and the work was not.

"Tell me the petition."

So Kunal told it. He had rehearsed it on the scree, with the wind taking half of it, and it came out smoother than he liked. "When the thaw council sits at Garh Neshar, I stand up and ask two things of the crown. First, no garrisons in the hill villages. Not on the Stair, not on the Moon, not in any fold the council names. The hills answer to the hills. If a warden wants a hearing, he asks the council, and the council answers."

"And second?"

"A council seat at Taravan. One voice of ours in the queen's own hall, with a right to speak before any order touches the passes." He heard how it sounded in the open air, and it still sounded good. "Neshar a self-governing valley under the crown. Not outside it. I don't want a war, and she can't afford one. This gives her the passes safely open, and it gives us a door she can't close from the capital without our hearing."

"It's a fine petition."

"But."

"But they'll ask." Dhanu did not look away. "Old Bhim will stand up first, because he always does. And he won't ask whether it's fair. He'll ask what you gave the crown for it. A queen doesn't hand over a seat in her hall for a speech."

Kunal had known that question was waiting, as one knows the next stair in the dark. He had not wanted to walk onto it.

"I'll tell them the truth," he said.

"Which is?"

"That I showed a crown surveyor the way. That it was mine to show." He took a breath. "That I gave it before I knew what she'd do, and that the council can hold me to it."

"They'll hold you to it, all right." Dhanu said it without heat. "Some will say you sold the hills. Some will say you were a fool in love, and that'll be the kinder half." He stirred the coals with a stick. "Let me tell you what none of them will say, because none of them have watched you as long as I have."

"Go on."

"You have never given anyone anything you couldn't take back."

It dropped into the fire like a split deodar round, and the sparks went up.

"That's not true," said Kunal. "The silver—"

"Clipped at the corner so no one could trace it to you. Nineteen bits, and you never once said your own name to the woman who ate because of them. The names you give the council, you give in pieces, so none of them holds the whole. The bones." Dhanu lifted the sheep-knuckles from his belt and let them lie in his palm. "Seven winters I taught you. You played me every game and never once let me win, nor lost on purpose. You played so it couldn't cost you."

"I broke a promise to her this morning. I paid for it."

"You broke it so you could be safe in the cleft. And you paid in coin, which is fine and clean and over." Dhanu closed his hand. "This one's different. You put a way in her head, there's no clipping it back. She can't forget it. You can't have it back. If she's the crown's, it's the crown's, and the hills pay. That's what I mean by a thing you can't take back."

Kunal sat very still. The fire hissed.

"I know it," he said.

"Do you?"

"I've known it since the Eye, when I was twelve and a man turned me round by the shoulders." He looked at his bandaged hands. "That's why I want to. If I can't take it back, then I've finally given it. Not lent it. Not parcelled it, or priced it. I want to find out what I am without the lock on."

Dhanu looked at him for a long time. The limp was still in the way he sat, the right foot planted and the left held out, as though even at rest he were walking round something.

"The council will want your head," he said.

"They can have a hearing."

"And if she draws it and takes it down the mountain and hands it over?"

"Then I was wrong, and the hills will say so, and I'll stand in the hall and hear it." He said it slowly. "I'd rather be wrong that way than right in the way I've been."

Dhanu grunted. It was the sound he made when a thing was settled and he did not intend to praise it. He heaved himself up, and the left foot swung out and round.

"Ask her what she'll do with it," he said. "Not before. After. That's all I'll say, and I'll say it without a walnut, so you needn't take it from me."

"I'll take it anyway."

"You're stupid enough already." He went off down the scree with the whistle swinging on its thong, and the dark folded behind him.

* * *

Kunal stayed by the fire until the sound of him was gone.

Then he opened his hand and looked at the second walnut.

There was a third in his coat. He had carried it since the day the council fixed the thaw date, in the lining over his ribs, wrapped in a scrap of grey wool. It was a plain walnut, whole and dry, with no thread on it. He took it out and held it in his palm, and he did not ask himself why he had brought it.

Mini had asked for one. That was all. Six years old on the pony in front of her mother, with her fist in the horse's mane, she had said, *Uncle Kunal, can I have a walnut with a string, like the runners? I want my own.* And he had said yes and, by the stones, had said *When I'm back.* It had been a light thing to say. He had said it as men say it to children, and he had not thought of it until the Drum froze under him last night, and he had lain with Ila's cold feet against his shin and not counted.

He took a hank of red thread from his pouch. It was Gauri's, a gift from the winter before. He pulled out a length with his teeth, because the fingers would not pinch it, and laid it round the seam.

The tips of his right hand lay against the shell like pebbles. He could see them move. He could not feel what they held. The thread slid, caught, and slid out again, and the first knot came out as a lump the size of a seed.

He undid it with his teeth, and began again.

It was a simple knot. A runner's turn, a loop, a tuck. He had tied it ten thousand times, in the dark, in a gale, with half a hand frozen. The second try came out lopsided, the thread riding up off the seam and leaving the walnut naked on one side. He pulled it loose and sat a moment with the thread dangling.

*The last joints lag.* He had said it to the hand that morning, flat, as a fact.

He thought of Pinku, in the walnut grove at Ranu at moonrise, last winter, with a clay lamp too big for his fist. He had tied that one himself, by the light of a lantern, in a hurry, with all ten fingers. It had been the best knot he knew. He had sent the boy home with it, and never learned if anyone had asked where it came from.

The third try he did slowly. He held the walnut against his knee, trapped the thread under the heel of his hand, and made each turn with the whole arm instead of the fingers. The knot came out round and fat and slightly off true, with an ugly tail he could not cut straight. It held. He tugged it, and it did not slip.

It was a bad knot. It was the worst he had tied since he was eight.

Kunal sat with it a while on his knee, and found that he was smiling, stupidly, at a nut.

"Mini will like it," said Ila, from the doorway.

He had not heard her wake. She stood on the threshold in her pattu, with her hair flat on one side and her boots laced, and the rope slack across the planks at her feet. It had stayed within its eight spans the whole night. She looked at the walnut, and then at his hand, and did not say anything about the fingers, which was her own kind of manners.

"It's bad," said Kunal.

"It's very bad. It's lopsided." She crossed and sat on the log where Dhanu had been, close enough that the fire lit one side of her face. "She'll show it to everyone."

"That's the idea."

"Is it from you?"

"It's from me." He turned it so she could see the tail. "No one else could make it so ugly."

She laughed, short and sudden and loud, and it rang across the scree like a dropped pan. Out on the lake the ice answered. He put the walnut in his coat, over the heart, in its wool.

The fire had burned to a red bed. The moon was low behind the cliff, and above the Eye the stars stood thick and cold. Raghu's four were somewhere on the far shore, with their spyglasses and their patience. In an hour the line would show, blue, to the two of them and no one else.

Kunal looked at the woman beside him. She had a pencil lead in her sleeve and a letter in her hem and eleven years of true sheets in her head. She would not tell him what she meant to do with the way. She had said *when we're through*, and a rope had hummed under it. He did not know her answer. He had not asked for it, and he did not mean to wait for it.

"Ila."

"Yes."

"I'm taking you into the cleft tonight." He made himself say it all, with his hands open on his knees and nothing kept back. "The whole way I know, every turn, in order. And then past it. Past the place where my uncle turned me round. I've never been there. We'll find out together what's on the far side of it."

She did not answer. The wind came down off the shoulder and moved the smoke. He watched her face go still, the way it did over a closure that would not come out. The pencil lead in her fingers had stopped mid-roll, and under her breath, so low he almost missed it, she began to count paces.
