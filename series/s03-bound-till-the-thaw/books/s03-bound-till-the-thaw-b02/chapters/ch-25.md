# Chapter 25: Burned

Ila could have said she did not know. It was a small lie, and her fingertips would have told him so in the time it took to blink.

She told him a truth instead, because truth was what she had.

"The Drum fold sits under a cornice," she said. "North shore, forty paces off the ice. The ledge hangs over the shingle like a lip. A man up there sees the whole fold, the lake, and both ways in. Two more rises look down on it from east and west."

Kunal said nothing. The whistle hung on his chest.

"I measured it from the mouth of the Eye, on the first morning, with a sight bar. The figures closed." She made her voice flat, the way she would for a clerk. "Three sightlines, Kunal. Nobody walks into that fold unseen, and nobody walks out unless the men on the ledge allow it. You'll hand them the sheets, and they'll kill you."

"That's not an inference."

"It is. It's a good one." She held his eyes. "You know the way in your head. A man who knows the way can share it. Your uncle taught you what to do with that sort of man. Raghu learned the same lesson at the same table."

He looked at the lake. A long note rolled up through the ice and went on under their boots.

"Then I won't give them the true set," said Kunal slowly. His voice had changed. He sounded like a man picking up a tool he had sworn never to touch. "Draw them another. You can, in an hour. A wrong turn at the slot. A bad count at the weeping stone. Let them walk it for days."

"No."

"Ila."

"They'll test it." She said it quickly, to get the first part out before the second. "Raghu isn't a fool. He'll put Dhanu on the front of the rope and walk him down your wrong turn, and Dhanu will find out what a false line is in the dark, with his bad foot. That's the first reason."

"And the second?"

"I don't draw false lines." It sounded smaller aloud than it had in her head. "I never have. Not for the crown. Not for a bribe. Not once in eleven winters. A line is true or it isn't, and if I make one that isn't, then I don't know what I am. I'd sooner lose the sheets to a fire."

She heard what she had said and wished the word back.

He took it badly, and then he took it well. She watched both happen. His mouth twisted. Then his shoulders came down and he nodded, as though she had named a bearing and he had checked it.

"You'd say that if it were your sister on the ice."

"I'd say it and hate myself."

"Yes," said Kunal. "I know." He rubbed his face with the heel of the dead hand. "Then it's the true ones. I'll carry them. I asked you for them a quarter hour ago and I'm asking again. Hand them over, and I'll be at the fold by moonrise."

* * *

The wind dropped. The ice went quiet.

Five sheets lay sewn into her pattu, with a queen's letter below them in the hem. Last night he had put the princes' way into her palms and asked for nothing. This morning he asked for it back, and he was right to ask. She would have asked herself.

She did the sums the way she did all sums, in a straight line.

If she handed them over, Raghu had the Stair, the Moon, the Eye, and a cairn-lit road down into the mountain. Hari's goats would go on being driven up roads that had been drawn for someone else. Every route-keeper who had ever scratched a warning on a stone would be a witness Raghu didn't need standing. And Kunal would walk onto the shingle under the cornice and be neatly, quietly done with.

If she said no, he would ask again. He would ask a third time at dusk. At the fold, in front of Raghu, his eyes would slide to her coat, as a man's eyes do toward whatever he is trying not to look at. Raghu would see it. A man who has the way in his head is worth keeping alive, if you can't have the paper. A man who has the paper in his friend's lining is a different sum.

But a man who thought the sheets were ash would not look at her coat.

They might strip her anyway. Raghu would search a surveyor on principle, and a woman with sewn seams was a risk. Still, Kunal's eyes would not send them there, and that was a risk she could carry for his life.

The thought settled in her like a plumb bob coming to rest. It was also what she had refused, a minute ago, on principle. She had said she would not draw a false line. She had not said a word about her tongue.

Why not burn them, then, and make it true? She had offered fire herself. But the sheets were the only true record of these hills she would ever have: Hari's scratched warnings copied stone by stone, every turn Kunal had given her, and the map she meant to share with both courts at the same hour. Burn them and the hills went back to whoever held the ways in his head. And to burn them here, in front of him, would be one more thing he stood and watched go.

She thought of Meera at the drafting table in Garh Neshar, laying a left glove on the cloth with a thumb and two fingers and a gap where the ring and the little finger had been. *A great lie kills flesh,* Meera had said, quite cheerfully, while Rohan stared at his plate. *You'll know it's great when it's about something that matters.* Kunal had sat silent through two of those sessions. He had watched that glove. He knew exactly what one touch of her hand would teach him.

He waited with his hands open.

"They're gone," said Ila.

She said it too softly, so she said it again. "I burned them. This morning, when we came in. There were coals banked under ash. They're gone."

The cold took her hands first. It went in under the gloves like a blade laid flat, and her fingertips were gone before she finished the sentence. She folded her arms across her ribs and tucked both hands into her armpits as though she were only hugging herself against the wind.

Kunal did not move.

"You burned them," he said.

"Yes."

"The Stair. The Moon. The Eye."

"All of them."

He looked at her as if she had spoken in a language he had once known. His face went through it. Disbelief came first, then something nearly like relief, the feeling of a man told that the weight he must lift was a trick of the light. Last came the plain horror of the sum.

"You drew the Eye last night," said Kunal. "You had it at dawn. I watched you put it in your coat."

"I know what you watched."

"Then—"

"The coals were alive under the ash when we came in. You were shouting for Dhanu and your back was to me." She heard herself and thought, with the cold part of her that kept on measuring, that it was a thin story. She said nothing more. Silence was free, and every added word was one more she would pay for.

"There was no smoke," said Kunal.

"Banked coals don't give any."

He looked at her a moment longer. She saw him weigh it, find it light, and decide, with a small movement of his jaw, not to say so. He let it go. He chose it, and she saw him choose.

He stepped toward her. For one long moment she thought he would put out the good hand and take hers. She knew exactly what he would learn.

He stopped a pace off. He put his hands in his coat.

"Why?" he said.

"Don't ask me why."

"I'm asking."

"Then I'm refusing," said Ila. "For free."

The word hung over the ice. He heard it and did not know what to do with it. It was not a lie, which was her whole hope. It was a locked door, and he was a man who had been raised among locked doors.

"All right," said Kunal, very quietly. "I'll look."

* * *

The second hut still smelled of Gauri's tea. Ila sat on the plank shelf and pulled her boots off.

The cold had reached her feet while she walked up the shore path, an inch at a time. First came a numbness like the end of a bad winter day, then a wooden feeling, then nothing. She unlaced the left one and looked at the sock. The skin of her toes looked ordinary. That was the worst of it. She had expected it to look like what it was.

She pulled the boots on again, as tight as the laces would go, and tied them off in a double hitch. Anything that made the foot feel held would help. If she could not feel the ground, she would at least not lose the boot.

Behind her Kunal knelt at the hearth. He took a poker from the hook and stirred the ash, and grey dust rose and settled in the thin light from the door. He lifted a clot of cinder in his dead fingers and crumbled it. He held a black curl to the light.

"Bark," he said. "Or paper."

"Could be either."

"It's thin." He turned it. "It's thin enough." He put it down and turned over more, lips moving. He was counting. She knew the shape of that count, because it was hers. He was checking whether the ash was enough ash for five sheets, and it was not a question the ash could answer.

She kept her hands in her sleeves and her knees together. When he looked round, she was lacing the right boot, bent over it, so that her hair hid her face.

"Your hands," he said.

Her stomach dropped. "What about them?"

"They're cold. You came out of the Eye with them cold. You've been wrapping them in your coat since the shore." He said it mildly, a man noting a measurement. "You should put them near the stove."

"There's no fire."

"No." He looked at the grey hearth. "No, there isn't."

He did not take her hands. He did not say *show me*. He sat back on his heels, and she watched him look at the ash and then at her bowed head. He had spent twenty years reading rooms, and he had put all of it down. He wanted to believe her more than he wanted to know. She would remember that whenever it hurt, and it was going to hurt.

"I'm sorry," said Kunal, and it startled her. "That I asked you to hand them over. That I asked twice. I said it was yours, last night. I meant it as a gift, not a trade." He rubbed his thumb along the poker's iron. "I was going to spend it at the first price anyone named. Maybe it's as well you got there before I did."

"Don't," said Ila.

"It's true."

"I know it's true." She tied the knot. Her fingers did not work on it, so she did it with her wrists. "That's why I'd rather you didn't."

He laughed once, wretchedly, and stood up and hung the poker on its hook.

"Then there's nothing to give them," he said.

"No."

"They'll want the sheets. Raghu will want to know where they are, and I'll tell him the truth and he won't believe it." He stood in the doorway with the light behind him, and she could not see his face. "Moonrise isn't the hour any more. They'll have a man at the shingle by then, with a spyglass. If I go tonight, at dusk, I'm one man with a rope and a prince's name and nothing in my hands. He can't search a thing that isn't there. And if Dhanu's been waiting a day and a night on the ice, I don't want him to wait another."

"Kunal."

"If I'm all there is," said Kunal, "I'll be all there is." The whistle shone on his coat. "I'll offer myself. They want the hills' way. I'm the one man who can walk it without a paper. Maybe that's worth something to a man who needs a door opened."

It was what she had hoped, laid out in the plainest words. She had meant it as a hope and not a plan.

"The rope," said Ila.

"Yes." He did not look at her. "The rope says you come."

"I know what the rope says."

"I'd leave you here if I could." He said it as flatly as a bearing. "You know I'd leave you here."

"I know."

She did. It was almost the only thing she knew. She stood up on feet that were no longer quite hers, and the floor held.

* * *

They went down at the hour when the cliff swallowed the sun.

The shore had gone the colour of a bruise. Ice crept out from the stones in long grey fingers, and the Drum lay beyond, white and flat, with the first of the dusk stars pricking out above the far rim. Ila walked behind Kunal on the shingle and counted her steps under her breath, because it was what her feet did.

*Forty. Forty-one.*

Her feet had stopped telling her what they were doing somewhere around the sixtieth pace. She found the ground by looking at it. She set each boot down where it ought to go and trusted it, the way she had once trusted a chain she had not yet pulled tight.

Kunal did not turn his head. He had the whistle in one fist and the rope loose in the other, and he held his shoulders as he had in the cleft. In the hut, he had not asked again. The question rode his back all the same, and neither of them named it.

*Ninety. Ninety-one.*

Her hands were as dead as stones inside her sleeves. It was a mercy, in its way. She could not feel them shaking.

*I did not draw a false line,* she told herself. *Not one.* It was a ridiculous thing to hold onto. She clung to it all the same, one true coin in a purse of counterfeits, and she knew what it was worth.

A wind came up off the lake and smelled of iron and cold water. Below the shore, the ice answered it with a slow boom, and the pulse went through her boots and up her shins and ended somewhere near the knee, where the feeling stopped.

"Ila," said Kunal. "Are you with me?"

"Eight spans," she said. "I'm here."

"You've gone quiet."

"I'm counting."

"What?"

She looked down at her boots. They were not moving the way she had told them to. Her left foot came forward and set itself down, and she waited for the feel of it and nothing came. It was like standing on a drawing of a foot.

"Everything," said Ila. "I'm counting everything."

The shingle ended. The ice began. She put a boot on it, and it was not her boot any more, and the other one was nowhere, and the rope pulled hard at her wrist and then let go. She heard Kunal say her name from a long way off. The lake rolled its drum-note up through the dusk, and the stars swung slowly over, and the grey ice came up to meet her.
