# Chapter 7: Ewe's Rest

Ewe's Rest was three stone houses under a cliff, and Ila had measured all of it before anyone saw them.

Slate roofs, turfed along the ridge. Two chimneys breathing juniper smoke, a third one cold. A pen wall round a trodden yard, ninety paces, give or take a goat. The cliff leaned over the lot like a clerk over a ledger.

"Whatever Dhanu says about me," said Kunal, on the path above her, "you may enjoy it. I ask only that you don't write it down."

"I write everything down."

"I know. It's what I'm afraid of."

He said it lightly. The rope lay quiet between them, and she put the field book away without being asked, which annoyed her.

A voice called from the wall. "Is that a prince, or has someone dropped a sack?"

A broad man in a patched brown pattu had come out of the nearest house. He crossed the yard at a lurch, right foot, then the left swung out and round, like a gate on a bent hinge. A boyhood fall, she guessed, and badly set. He was laughing before he reached them.

"Dhanu," said Kunal, and nothing else, because Dhanu had him by both shoulders. For a moment they stood forehead to forehead. Then Dhanu stepped back and cuffed him round the ear, hard enough to ring.

"That's for no word since first snow."

"Dhanu—"

"That's for being thin." A second cuff. "And that's for tying yourself to a crown woman without asking me."

"Three," said Kunal, rubbing his ear. "You said one was interest."

"I'm charging by the month." Dhanu swung his bright black eyes to Ila. "You're the surveyor. I told my wife he'd lose you on the Stair."

"He nearly did," said Ila. "Ninth tread."

"Who held whom?"

"I held him."

"Gauri!" Dhanu bellowed at the house. "She *counts*!"

Kunal laughed. It was not the courtly lift she had heard across the drafting table at Garh Neshar. It was a bark, undignified, head back, and it went on until he had to lean on the wall. She had not known he owned it.

In the doorway stood a tall woman with flour to the elbows and her arms folded. She eyed the pencil showing in Ila's pocket the way you would eye a snake in the woodpile. A small face hung round the edge of her skirt, one round eye and a fist of braid.

"Boots," said Gauri. "Off. The prince first. He tracks."

* * *

Inside there was one long room, a chulha at the heart of it, bread on a hot stone, and a smell of juniper and wet wool that Ila could have drawn from memory. She sat where she was put. The rope lay across the flags to Kunal's wrist, and nobody stepped over it twice.

Dhanu had a story for every year of Kunal's life.

"Ten winters old," he said, tearing bread, "and he's sure he can carry a ewe. He gets her up on his shoulders. She sits down. He is *stuck*. Under a sheep. Weeping with rage."

"I was not weeping."

"In the feed trough, the pair of them."

"It was a bucket."

"Mini," said Dhanu gravely. "A trough or a bucket?"

"Trough," said the child, who had crept as far as the chulha and was staring at Ila's rope. Her voice was certain. She was six, and she had not been there.

"Betrayed," said Kunal, "by my own household."

"Outvoted," said Dhanu. "It's a hill custom."

Gauri set a bowl down in front of Kunal and cuffed him in passing, one flat smack round the ear, as if she were swatting a fly. "Elbows."

He took his elbows off the board. He was laughing again, and he lost the next argument as well, over whether he had ever once beaten Dhanu at stones. He had not. Dhanu proved it with three pebbles and a long account of Kunal's cheating, and Kunal conceded with both hands up, a prince in defeat, looking happier than she had seen him in a fortnight.

Ila laughed so suddenly that Mini jumped. Then Mini laughed too, in the same loud crack, and gazed at her with deep respect.

*He is loved,* thought Ila. She had not measured for that. In Taravan she had filed him under his uncle: an heir in a coat, playing at rebellion for a crown. The man cuffed round the ear in this kitchen did not fit the file.

Gauri stopped her at the door afterwards, a hand on the post.

"You're the one who measures." Her face gave away nothing. "The last of your lot told my mother the hill was crown land, and wrote it down, and went. So. My wall. Tell me if it'll hold."

The pen wall stood shoulder high, dry stone, laid with care. Ila walked it with a knotted cord and her palm flat on every course. She did not write. At the north side she knelt and put her hand into the gap at the foot, where the base met the turf.

She stood up slowly.

"Say it plain," said Gauri.

"A wall this high wants a base half its height wide. Yours is a hand short, along the whole north side." She kept her voice level. "It'll hold till thaw. Then the snow comes off the cliff and sits on it, and the north corner goes into the ewes."

Gauri looked at the wall for as long as it takes to hate a fact. Across the yard, Kunal had come to the door with the rope trailing, and he did not speak.

"His father built that."

"He built it well. He didn't have the cord." Ila held it out. "Lay a course of flat stones along the foot, a hand wide, running outward. It'll take two days and a back."

"Two days I haven't got." But Gauri took the cord and ran it through her fingers. "My husband says you don't lie. That your wrist won't let you."

"Not cheaply."

"Then I'll have it done by thaw." She turned the knotted cord over once. "Ila. You'll want a bigger bowl at supper."

It was the first time she had used the name. Ila found, with some surprise, that she wanted very much to have earned it.

* * *

At supper Dhanu told the story of the high way. It was a long one, with a bad bridge and a worse mule in it. He did not name where the way began or ended, and Ila did not ask. The rope hummed nothing. She noted that he told it with his eyes on Kunal, and that Kunal let him.

"And you were never afraid of it," Dhanu said. "Not once, even at nine. The first time you walked these ways, you went alone. All alone, the first time."

Kunal reached for the dish with his whole arm.

"Gauri, is there more of the barley? I could eat a ewe." He turned the dish to the light. "Ila, tell Dhanu what you called the black crag on the Stair. He'll want to hear."

It was a very quick swerve. Dhanu's eyes rested on him a moment too long. Gauri looked at her bowl.

*The first time,* thought Ila. *Which means there was a second.* She put the sentence aside, whole, where she kept things she meant to come back to, and told them.

"Nimmi's Knuckle," she said. "After a healer we both know. She'd hate it."

"She'd be insufferable," said Kunal.

Dhanu roared. Gauri, against her will, snorted into her bread. Mini, small and fierce at the end of the board, waited for the noise to drop and then said, "Can you do a star?"

* * *

The cloud had pulled back to the north and the sky over the cliff was a clean black with holes in it. Mini towed Ila into the yard by two fingers. Kunal followed, as the rope required, and leaned in the doorway with his arms folded and a look of great innocence.

"That one." Mini pointed at a small yellow star low in the notch of the cliff. "It hasn't got a name. Baba says it's too small."

"Stars have names," said Ila. "Old ones. A name is only a handle. I don't give them out, I write down what's already—"

"Uncle says you named a rock."

"A crag."

"A rock after a lady." Mini's chin came up. "I want one."

Ila looked at the star. It was a poor little thing, third magnitude at best, hanging over a slate roof with a crooked chimney. She thought of a dozen things to say. A neutral woman would have said, *I'm sorry, that's not how it's done.*

"Then it's Mini," said Ila.

The child went rigid. "That's *me*."

"It's yours. It's the one over your house. I'll write it down, if your father lets me."

"Does it go on the map?"

"A star doesn't tell anyone where a hut is," said Kunal from the doorway, dry as dust. "I think we can spare it."

Mini ran inside to tell everyone, shrieking. The door banged. Ila stood alone in the yard with the cold in her teeth and found that she was smiling.

Kunal came out and stood a pace from her, which was as far as the rope allowed without comment. For a while he said nothing. His breath smoked.

"May I ask a question," he said. "Not one of mine, from the bargain. A private one."

"You may ask."

"You told me a map is only ever true or false. That it has no more feeling in it than a plumb line." He kept his eyes on the star. "You named a crag for Nimmi. You named a ridge for a cook who fed you. Now a star for a child you met at noon. I am trying to find a place on your sheet where you named something for a person you dislike."

She opened her mouth.

There was a thing she could say. A label. A handle. A convenience of the margin. She had said it to her brother, to the Taravan survey office, and once to a very tired examiner who had asked about Tamar's Shoulder. She said it in her head now, and it sat there, round and smooth and true.

It was not the answer. She knew it and he knew it.

"There isn't one," said Ila.

"No."

"I haven't thought about why."

"I believe that." His voice was gentle, the voice she had heard him use on Mini. "That's what I wanted to know. It's all right. I only wanted to see if you knew."

"I don't." She heard how small it sounded. She held her bare hands out in the starlight, pink and ordinary, and he did not look at them. He looked at her.

"Then we're even," said Kunal. "Neither of us knew."

He went in. She stayed a moment longer under the little yellow light, and for the first time in her life she did not know what she meant to write beside it.

* * *

Ila lay on the sleeping shelf in the long room, under a blanket that smelled of goat, and did not sleep.

The rope ran from her wrist across the floor and out under the door. On the other side Kunal sat on the step with his back to the planks. Dhanu sat beside him. She could hear the scrape of Dhanu's bad foot as he stretched it out. The chulha gave a last ticking breath, and the voices began, low enough that a sleeper would hear nothing.

She was not a sleeper. She lay with her eyes open and counted the roof slates and listened.

"Raghu's pairs passed the upper fold two days ago," said Dhanu.

Nothing from Kunal.

"Two pairs, loaded, going up fast. They didn't stop for water. My boy saw them from the high pasture and ran. They were heading for the Hollow Moon."

The step creaked. The rope on her wrist went tight and stayed tight, the way it did when a man stood up and forgot himself.

"Two days," said Kunal.

*Two days.* Ila did the sum before she could stop herself. A day ahead of them at the burned hut. A day of climbing since. She had been right to the hour about the ash, and for once she wished she had been a poor reader of ground.

"Sit," said Dhanu. "You can't walk it tonight."

"I know."

"Then sit, Kunal."

The rope eased. The planks creaked as he sat.

She did not know where the Hollow Moon was. It was on no sheet she had drawn, in no shepherd's mouth she had asked, and she lay in the dark under a goat-smelling blanket with her hands quite warm, understanding that she was not supposed to.
