# Chapter 13: The Ringing Stone

They came down onto the basin floor at first light, after a night of walking by blue cairn-light with the rope between them. Kunal knew the storm would reach the water before they did.

He saw it as the last scree gave out underfoot. A long grey wall rolled down the far peaks like a lid sliding shut. Under it the three lakes lay pale and flat and shining. They would not shine long.

"Three hours," said Ila.

"Four. Perhaps five."

"Three." She had her watch open on her palm and her eyes on a spur across the basin. "Its shadow crossed that spur between my first count and my second. That's not a cloud drifting. It's a cloud arriving." She snapped the watch shut. "Walk."

They walked.

The floor was scree and old drift, hard under the crust. The rope ran eight spans of pale wool, taut and then slack and then taut again as their strides found each other. The wind was at their backs, which he disliked. A wind that pushes you is a wind that wants you somewhere.

Ila never glanced at the sky again. She worked. He had watched her for six days now and it had not once grown ordinary. She took a bearing off a crag without breaking stride, her elbow tucked, her lips moving on the count. When her pencil lead snapped she held out her left hand, palm up, and kept her head forward. He put a fresh one in it, warm from inside his coat. She wrote. She held out the hand again.

"The shore cairn," he said, to keep his voice steady. "It's close. A mile along, no more. We'll shelter there if the front comes early."

"A mile."

"About."

"Kunal." He heard the delight start in her voice before she turned. "I paced a baseline at the foot of the Ladder and took two bearings on that cairn off the Raven Spur. I've carried the figure down the basin all night. From where we stand, right now, it is nine hundred and ten paces, give or take a dozen. A third of a mile, with a following wind."

"It looks a mile."

"It looks a mile because you hill people measure distance by how tired you'll be." She was grinning. "I have never once been wrong about a pace."

"You were wrong about the Ladder's third ledge."

"I was told there were three. Possibly four. I was misled."

"Fair."

Then she laughed. She did it the way she always did, with no warning, her head going back and the sound going out across the basin so loud that two ravens broke off the scree and flapped away. It rang on the cliffs. It came back thin.

He had been set down in front of that laugh perhaps six times now, and each time he had thought he was ready. He was not. His boot went wrong on a loose slab and he caught himself on the rope, his count lost entirely. He had spent years teaching himself not to want a thing where others could see him wanting it. Now he stood on a frozen shore, caught wanting this, openly, to be proved wrong by her again.

"Walk," said Ila, still smiling, and he did.

* * *

The cairn stood where she had said it would, within a dozen paces of her figure.

It was a jogini cairn, old, chest-high, built of flat grey slabs the lake wind had polished. It stood on a shelf above the shore where the scree gave out. The storm had pulled the morning down to dusk, and the blue light in the stones showed even by day. When they came up under it the wind went through the gaps and made its low note, level as a bowl rim before a finger touches it.

The Mirror lay below them. It was the first lake and the largest, a flat white plate a mile across with no mark on it. Beyond it the second and third were smaller shapes, sunk in shadow. Kunal had never come this way in full light. He had come at night, by the ridge, twice, and had not looked down.

Ila stood on the lip of the shelf to take her bearing of the far shore. Her left hand went flat against her coat, over the lining at her ribs, and stayed there. It stayed through the sighting. It stayed while her right hand wrote, and she held the book against her hip and wrote one-handed, rather than take it away. She gave no reason. He had seen her do it once the night before, at the edge of the shelf above the lakes, and had not asked then either. Whatever lay under that wool, she kept her palm on it the way a person guards a sleeping child.

Behind her, the storm stood nearer than she had put it. The grey wall had crossed the second spur since the scree, and its lit edge had gone the colour of old pewter. Frost had caught in the loose hair at her temple, and she had not noticed.

"It's gained," she said. "Faster than I put it. Twenty minutes to be on the ice before it. After that, none."

"Then we have twenty minutes."

"That's what I said."

"I know." He was standing too close, and he knew it, and he did not step back. "You make a deadline sound like a dare."

She turned.

She had not meant to, he thought. She had turned to say something sharp, and then the sharp thing was not there. They stood a pace apart under the stones with the wind going through them. Her face was bare and wind-scoured, her lashes white. Her eyes moved, once, from his eyes to his mouth. He saw her see him see it.

Neither of them stepped away.

"You've been watching me since the scree," said Ila. Her voice was lower than he had heard it.

"I've been watching you work."

"Is there a difference?"

*Is there?* The words were already in his mouth. It was his oldest habit, a question handed back like a coin. But she had spent six days learning his habits. She would count it a refusal, and she would be right. He opened his mouth to say *yes*, which was true.

She spoke first. She glanced down at the field book in her fist and up again. "You've watched me draw all winter. Does it matter to you what goes on it?"

It was the question the whole rope had been walking toward. Thirty years of dinners answered for him. The smooth, warm reply that soothes a guest and costs the host nothing came out before he had weighed a word of it.

"It doesn't matter to me what you d—"

The stones rang.

It was not loud. The low note in the cairn lifted, the way a bowl's song climbs when a wet finger presses harder on the rim. It went up past the wind. The rope gave a hard cold pull at his wrist, and his fingertips went numb in his glove.

He stopped. His mouth shut on its own.

Ila had heard it. She went very still, her head a little to one side, the way she listened to ice.

"Finish it," she said.

"No."

"Finish the sentence, Kunal."

"I won't."

"Why not?" Her voice was perfectly even. "If it's true, it costs nothing. If it isn't, you've lost a few fingertips and I've lost an afternoon. Say it."

He looked at her, and at the stones behind her. He thought of his uncle, who had taught him the autumn he was nine, over supper, in the pleasant voice he used for terrible things. *Whoever knows the way owns the people, Kunal. Remember it. Everything else is manners.* It had sat in his mouth ever since, a stone he could neither swallow nor spit. And what had just left his mouth was manners, the cheapest kind, pressed on the one person who had asked him a simple thing straight. If he said what was true now, she would hold it in her hand, written down, with his name beside it.

But she had refused him a promise by the fire at the Ladder's foot because she would not give one she might break. She had still left the staircase off her sheet and told him why. She had paid for his people's ways with her own good name. She was owed something.

"It matters more than anything," said Kunal.

The stones dropped. The note slid down through the wind and settled, low and level, as it had been when they arrived.

"Your hand," said Ila.

He pulled the glove. Five nails the colour of candle wax, the white climbing a knuckle's depth. The stones had gone quiet, but the cost had not. A lie once begun did not unbegin because a true word came after it.

"An hour," he said. "It's small."

"I can see how small." She did not look away from the hand. "More than anything?"

"More than my seat on the council. More than my uncle's head on a plate. More than Neshar, on some mornings, and I'm not proud of that." It came out of him in a rush, rough. "The first thing was manners. It was out before I knew it, the way I've answered every question since I was nine. The mountain weighed it for me. So you'll have to have the truth."

"I can have it?"

"You've had it all winter. You'll do what you'll do." His throat hurt. "You'd write it down if I asked you not to."

"Yes."

"Then write it."

She did not reach for the notebook. She lifted her bare hand, slowly, as if there were a long way to bring it, and laid two fingers against the front of his coat. They rested over his heart for the length of one breath. They were warm.

"I'm not writing that one," said Ila.

It was not a kiss, and it left him weaker than any kiss would have.

The wind changed on the stones. It went from a hum to a groan.

"Kunal," said Ila.

"I know."

"The front."

He looked past her.

The grey wall had stopped being a wall. It had become the whole north, and it came on faster than any storm he had seen. Its edge was a white line eating the far shore, and under the line the lakes were going dark.

* * *

They ran the last of the shore with the snow coming in sideways. It was not yet a storm. It was a warning of one, hard small pellets that stung the face and hissed on the crust. By the time they came down to the lip of the Mirror, he could not see the third lake at all.

The cairn-light stood on the ice. He had expected it. Faint blue shapes ran out from the shore in a line, lamp to lamp, dead straight across the plate toward the far side. The winter line. Two bound people could walk it to the other bank.

Ila stopped at the edge and went down on one knee.

"Wait," she said. "I want to sound it."

"There's no time."

"Ten minutes. I'll tap the ice with the haft every ten paces out and listen." She had the axe off her pack already. "Clear lake ice rings. Rotten ice goes dull. If there's a pressure line, a spring, a seam, I'll hear it. I won't walk onto a plate I haven't read."

"The line is lit."

"The line is lit for people. It isn't lit for the weather."

"Ila." He took her arm, his bare hand on her sleeve, the nails still white. She looked at the hand, and then up at him, snow packed on her eyebrows, the axe haft in her fist and the hunger to be exact all over her face. He wanted to give her the ten minutes. He wanted to stand on that shore and let her read the whole lake like a page. "Look behind you."

She looked.

The north had gone out. There was no far shore any more, no cliffs, only a brown-white wall a few hundred paces off, and the sound that came out of it was long and flat and full of grit. Three minutes, he thought. Perhaps two.

"If we're on the shore when that hits," said Kunal, "we'll be in the open with no cave and no wall and the whole sky coming down on us. If we're on the line, we'll be moving. We'll be across in a quarter hour."

"On ice I haven't sounded."

"On the one road the mountain lit for us."

He watched her weigh it. It took her about as long as a breath. She had been misled about a Ladder and could not stand to be wrong about a lake, and he saw her hate the answer and take it.

"Then I tap as we go," said Ila. "Every step. You don't talk."

"I won't."

"You walk where I walk. You walk on my left foot's track and nowhere else."

"Yes."

"And if I say stop—"

"I stop."

She nodded once and stood. She stepped onto the Mirror with the axe haft low in her right hand, and the wood went tick on the ice, tick, and the sound ran out ahead of her, bright and clean.

He pulled the glove back on over the white nails and went after her.

* * *

The wind took them at the third lamp. It came on all at once, a hand between the shoulders and a roar in both ears, and the world went to white and rope. He could see her back, a dark shape, three paces off. He could see the faint blue gleam on the ice at his feet. He could see nothing else.

Tick, went the axe, and tick. He put his boots where hers had been. His fingertips lay cold and dead inside the wool, and would for the best part of the hour. He thought of her two fingers laid against his coat, and the warmth of them. He had never been so glad of a wind in his life, because it let him pretend the shake in his breath was the cold.

*It matters more than anything.*

He had said it aloud, to a surveyor, on open rock, and the stones had sung it low and true. It could not be called back. Anyone who could read could take a bearing off that sentence and find where he was soft. A gift, or a handle? He did not know. He had set a truth in her hand hilt first, so he counted lamps instead, blue and dim, one and the next, and kept his boots in her track.

By his count they had gone halfway, perhaps a little more. By hers, he was sure, it would be exact.

The wind dropped for a breath. In the lull he heard the ice.

It was a low groan first, far down, as if the lake were turning over in its sleep. It rose through the soles of his feet and the bones of his legs. Then the whole white plate under them split with a crack like green timber under the axe, a seam running away into the snow on both sides at once, and the sound rolled out to every shore and came back doubled.

Ila stopped dead.

She did not turn. She did not look at him. She stood with the axe haft lifted off the ice, her head tipped, her whole body gone to listening, from her boots to the snow on her hood.

"Don't move," she said.
