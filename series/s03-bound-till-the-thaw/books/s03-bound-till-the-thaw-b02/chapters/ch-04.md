# Chapter 4: The Walnut Man

The law, when she finally saw it work, looked like a man dropping a sack.

Ila stopped at the head of Ranu's lane with the rope slack between her wrist and Kunal's. Below her, Roop swung a sack of redbark off a mule and onto Nimmi's stoop. Chand took it from him, a big, shy man who held it as though it might take offence. It was noon. The lane was watching. Nobody hid anything and nobody ran.

She had spent six weeks in a Garh Neshar guest-house while eleven lines were argued into being. She had measured the drafting table twice. She had never once pictured a sack, a stoop and nobody afraid.

Over Nimmi's door, chalk tallies marched across the lintel in fives, one row for every dose poured. Beside them, faded to the grey of old frost, sat the print of a hand the width of a plate. She had heard that mark described. The description had not been big enough.

"Roop," said Kunal.

Roop looked up, looked at the rope on Ila's wrist, and gave Kunal a nod with no surprise in it. He said nothing at all. Ila filed that beside the stair count and the two spans of wall and the fourth light going out. She was collecting a great many things she had not been told.

The night had been long and unproductive. The east line had never come back. Kunal had walked her through the dark until he was sure of it, then stopped, and at first light turned them south onto the Garh road without a word of explanation. She had said nothing either, because she had her hands to think about, and because she had learned in one day that he could be asked anything and would answer a different thing.

"Why here?" she had asked at last, near dawn.

"Because the valley we saw has a road to it that starts at Ranu," he had said. "Part of it, anyway. And part of it you can draw without me."

That was a half answer, and she had written it down as one.

A shout came up the lane. It came from low down, from a small boy with a runny nose and both fists in the air.

"Walnut man! Walnut man!"

He ran straight at Kunal, with no caution at all, and Ila watched the prince of Neshar go down on one knee in the frozen mud as if he had been waiting all morning to do it. The courtly face was gone. Something plainer had replaced it.

"You've grown," said Kunal.

"I'm *seven*."

"Seven. Past all reason." He dug in his coat and brought out a walnut, brown and wrinkled, with a twist of red thread knotted round its middle. "Mind you don't eat it in one go. Your grandmother will have my head."

Pinku took it in both hands as though it were an egg. Kunal straightened the boy's cap, and his thumb lingered a moment on the child's cold ear, very gently, before he let go.

Ila stood quite still.

It came together the way a traverse closes, one angle after another. Meera at the drafting table, three weeks into the drafting, with her left hand lying in its glove like something she was minding for someone else. Meera telling it in her dry way, as a joke on herself: the masked buyer. The cord on Nimmi's latch with three hard knots and a long tail ending in a loop. The silver, thin squares of Neshar silver, each with one corner clipped off clean. And a child sent home from the grove with a walnut tied in red thread.

She looked at the coin-sized clip in her own memory. She looked at Kunal's coat pocket.

"Ila-ji," said Roop, with the tact of a man who had seen her face. "Nimmi's inside. She'll want you to see the slope."

"In a moment," said Ila. "Thank you."

* * *

The walnut terraces rose behind the village in steps, each wall built of the same grey stone. The trees were bare. She walked up to the second tier and stopped where the rope ran out its seven and a half spans and the chill began, and she turned.

Kunal had followed. He stood with his hands in his sleeves, looking at the ground, and she thought: he knows what I'm about to say. He's known since the boy ran at him.

"The masked buyer," said Ila.

"Yes."

"Two winters of medicine for Ranu. Paid for without haggling, in silver with a corner cut off."

"Yes."

"My sister-in-law that will be sat in that guest-house and told me the story as a mystery. And you came to the table twice and sat across from her and said nothing."

"I was not asked," said Kunal, "and silence is free." Then, before she could answer: "That's too neat. I'm sorry. I was asked, in effect, by everything. I chose not to."

She waited. She had learned that from the rope. If you waited, the rope did half the work.

"The silver was my uncle's," said Kunal. "From Garh Neshar's stores, the ones he holds in the crown's name. I took it a bag at a time and I ran it down the back road through the lower lanes. Two winters. The regent was selling grain to this village at four times the autumn price, and I paid for medicine with the profit." He looked at her. "It was the only honest use I could find for it."

Her hands were warm in their mittens. She could feel that they were warm.

"Would you have mapped the back road?" said Kunal. "The road Nimmi's runners used. If you'd known, when you took the commission, that a road existed where a man was keeping children alive. Would you have drawn it?"

It was the question she had been dreading, because she knew what the answer was. She could have told the truth, or she could have said something kind, and the rope would show which.

"Yes," said Ila.

She said it clearly. She did not look at her fingers, though she wanted to. Beside her the terrace wall hummed faintly in the wind and stayed low and level.

"I'd have drawn it," she said. "It's a true road. I'd have drawn it and given a copy to the queen and to the council, and I'd have believed that was neutral. I still don't know it wasn't." She took her mitten off, and her fingers lay pink in the cold. "I don't like that you've seen my hands."

"I like it very much," said Kunal quietly, "and I find I mind that I do."

He didn't smile when he said it, and she was glad. She put the mitten back on.

* * *

Nimmi met them at her own gate, a broad woman with a face like a fist and a voice that listed things.

"Surveyor. Cough, you can hear. No fever. The Dev boy has gained four pounds. Come with me. You've a brother who stood on this slope and I want you to see the rest of it."

She did not ask what the rope was. She did not ask why a prince stood at the far end of it and stopped, at eight spans, below the last terrace, respectfully, with his back turned.

The graves lay in the lowest terrace cut, in rows, with the frozen earth heaped over each. Ila began to count because there was nothing else she could do with them. The small mounds were child-length. Some carried a pine bough. One carried a wooden goat with one leg missing. One carried a bell.

"Thirty-two," said Nimmi, when she had finished. "Thirty-one at your brother's first count. The thirty-second was a man who'd nursed all of them. Your brother held the rope while we dug."

Ila's throat did what it had done at the winter line. "He told the Fair thirty-two."

"He said it plain. I've heard worse said in better coats." Nimmi stood with her arms folded. "We were to burn that night. Every marked house. You've heard it."

"I heard there were torches."

"There were torches. There was an officer on a tall grey horse in a slate cloak, with a grey beard, who rode up to the captain with the red moustache and said lower them. And the captain lowered them." She said it flatly, as though it were a figure she had checked twice. "I stood on that step and looked at his face the whole time. So I could say it afterwards."

"Who was he?" said Ila. "My brother never learned his name. The assembly's paper says the regent's general was exiled. It doesn't say why."

Nimmi's eyes went to the slope, to the graves, and came back. "I know who he was."

"Then—"

"That's not mine to spend," said Nimmi.

It was said without heat. It was the sort of refusal a ledger gives you. Ila had met a hundred witnesses who would not speak, and most were afraid. Nimmi was not afraid. She was keeping accounts of something that did not belong to her, and had decided to keep them closed.

"Ranu will say it, when it's time," said Nimmi. "Not before. Ask me what I'll tell you."

Ila thought. She had learned from Kunal what that was worth. "Is he alive?"

"He's alive."

It was a small answer, and it opened something larger beneath it. A man who gave an order that saved thirty-odd houses, and had been sent away for it, and had a healer who would not speak his name. Ila wrote nothing down. She had her hand on the field book and left it there, and for the first time in eleven winters she understood that not writing was also a measurement.

* * *

"The walnut," she said, when they were climbing again.

Kunal had said nothing for several minutes. He walked a pace ahead, scanning the slope, one hand idle on the rope. "Ask."

"Why a walnut? Why red thread? You gave it to a child as though it were her allowance."

"It's the oldest thing I own that isn't a name." He glanced back. "Route-keepers send true word with a walnut tied in red thread. Not a letter, not a seal. If a runner comes to a hamlet with news and no walnut, the news is a lie, or it's from someone who isn't mine. We've kept it ten generations. The regent's men never found it, because it looks like a gift for children."

"So when a boy brings a walnut, the message is true."

"When a boy brings a walnut, I'd stake a village on it." He seemed to hear what he had said. "I did, as it happens."

She walked on. The terraces gave out above them into scrub, and the path became a stony track. Ila turned her mind over, honestly, the way she'd turn a theodolite to check its level.

She had arrived in Neshar with a picture of him, and the picture was tidy. A prince of his uncle's house, a man who spoke of openness and kept every road in his head. A man playing at rebellion for a throne, with a good voice for it. She had even named him, privately, after a peak she did not like.

That picture did not account for a kneeling man in mud, or two winters of stolen silver, or a walnut. It was not that the picture was wrong in every line. He did keep every road in his head. He had told her so himself, by omission, at every stair. But he had thrown it at her wrongly, as a measurement taken from too far off. He was not his uncle's kind of prince. She did not yet know what kind he was.

Where the track forked, a line of old worn stones led off to the right, up behind the terraces and over a bare spur. She had not seen it from the village. It did not appear on her sheets.

"That one," said Kunal.

"What is it?"

"The salt road." He shrugged. "Ranu's runners use it to go round Garh Neshar's gates. It runs up behind the terraces, over two spurs, and comes down on the dry side of the Garh. No warden has walked it in fifty winters. It's illegal in nine places, and I'm fairly sure the smugglers have counted them."

She had her book out before he had finished. She sat on a flat rock with her cold fingers and drew it: the first spur, the angle of the terrace walls below it, the long slope of scree that carried the road up through the broken ground. She paced it. Two and a half spans a pace. She took a bearing off the spur and another off the corner of Nimmi's roof, and the road came up out of the paper line by line.

He did not stop her. He stood at the end of the rope and watched her draw, and she felt the watching like a hand laid on her neck, and kept her pencil level.

It was a true road. It was also a lifeline. She had drawn it as a smuggler's route, as a medicine road, as a road a prince had paid the regent's own silver to walk, and she had drawn it exactly as it lay, because that was the only way she knew to draw anything.

When she looked up, his face was the one she had seen at the cairn, the open one, startled by itself.

"You drew it," he said.

"It's there."

"You knew what it was."

"Yes." She turned the page so he could see it. "That's what a map is, Highness. I told you at the table. If I leave out a lifeline to be kind, it's not a kinder map. It's a false one."

"I was waiting," said Kunal slowly, "for you to say you wouldn't."

"Were you disappointed?"

"I'm trying to find out."

They went on up the track. The wind had risen, and the cold was a hard clean one. Above the last terrace, where the scrub ended, a little cairn stood against the sky, a knee-high stack of grey stones with a flat cap. It was so small that she would have walked past it. Down the slope behind it, faintly, she thought she saw a pale thread of blue.

Kunal stopped beside it and put a hand on the top stone, as he had put his hand on the lamp-oil jar in the kitchen.

"This is the salt road," he said. "It's the last way I'll show you that every smuggler in Neshar already knows. Anyone with a mule has walked it. You can draw it for the queen and you'll take nothing from anyone."

"And after this?"

"After this, you earn them."

It was a plain sentence. It sat in her ears like a stone dropped down a well, and she listened for the bottom of it. Behind her lay the village and the thirty-two graves. Ahead lay the road to a valley that did not exist on her sheets, and a man who had decided, and told her, that the next lines would cost her something.

She did not know what the price was. She did not know what he would count as earning. She knew only that a man who named a price before she asked had been thinking about it for a long time.

Ila shut the field book on the salt road, with her pencil in it.

"Price first," she said.
