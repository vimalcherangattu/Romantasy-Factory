# Chapter 4: The Walnut Man

The law, when she finally saw it work, looked like a man dropping a sack.

Ila stopped at the head of Ranu's lane with the rope slack between her wrist and Kunal's. Below her, Roop swung a sack of redbark off a mule and onto Nimmi's stoop. Chand took it from him, a big, shy man who held it as if it might take offence. It was noon. The lane was watching. Nobody hid anything and nobody ran.

She had spent six weeks in a Garh Neshar guest-house while eleven lines were argued into being. She had measured the drafting table twice. She had never once pictured a sack, a stoop and nobody afraid.

Over Nimmi's door, chalk tallies marched across the lintel in fives, one row for every dose poured. Beside them, faded to the grey of old frost, sat the print of a hand the width of a plate. She had heard that mark described. The description had not been big enough.

"Roop," said Kunal.

Roop looked up, looked at the rope on Ila's wrist, and gave Kunal a nod with no surprise in it. He said nothing at all. She was collecting a great many things she had not been told.

At dawn, where the east track met the Garh road, she had watched Kunal choose.

The broken line had not come back. All night the fourth light stayed dead, and the dark beyond it with it. Ila had stood with her book shut on a page that had no east bearing.

"Raghu's men are at that fold," she said. "Or they've been. You walked us toward it all night."

"I did." He was looking east at the grey hills, rubbing the ink stain on his thumb. "Two bound pairs, at the pace we'd keep, reach Sarn fold tomorrow night. Ranu's runners can be there by dusk today. They go by the low lanes, where no rope's needed, and they know every door."

"So you'll send them."

"I'll ask." He said the word as if it had a taste. "It should be my feet. A man who sends others to a door he ought to knock on himself is my uncle's kind of man. I know exactly what it costs." He looked east a moment longer, and the courtly face slipped, and what lay under it was only tired. Then he turned south. "Ranu is a day down the Garh road. Part of the way I mean to show you starts there. Part of it you can draw without me."

That was a half answer, and she had written it down as one.

A shout came up the lane. It came from low down, from a small boy with a runny nose and both fists in the air.

"Walnut man! Walnut man!"

He ran straight at Kunal, with no caution at all, and Ila watched the prince of Neshar go down on one knee in the frozen mud as if he had been waiting all morning to do it. The courtly face was gone. A younger one had replaced it.

"You've grown," said Kunal.

"I'm *seven*."

"Seven. Past all reason." He dug in his coat and brought out a walnut, brown and wrinkled, with a twist of red thread knotted round its middle. "Mind you don't eat it in one go. Your grandmother will have my head."

Pinku took it in both hands like an egg. Kunal straightened the boy's cap, and his thumb lingered on the child's cold ear, very gently, before he let go.

Ila stood quite still.

It came together the way a traverse closes, one angle after another. Meera at the drafting table, her left hand lying in its glove like something she was minding for someone else. Meera telling it in her dry way, as a joke on herself: the masked buyer. The cord on Nimmi's latch with three hard knots and a long tail ending in a loop. The silver, thin squares of Neshar silver, each with one corner clipped off clean. And a child sent home from the grove with a walnut tied in red thread.

She looked at Kunal's coat pocket. She thought of him at that table, twice, silent, across from the woman telling it.

"Ila-ji," said Roop, with the tact of a man who had seen her face. "Nimmi's inside. She'll want you to see the slope."

"In a moment," said Ila. "Thank you."

* * *

The walnut terraces rose behind the village in steps, each wall built of the same grey stone. The trees were bare. She walked up to the second tier and stopped where the rope ran out its seven and a half spans and the chill began, and she turned.

Kunal had followed. He stood with his hands in his sleeves, looking at the ground, and she thought: he knows what I'm about to say. He's known since the boy ran at him.

"The masked buyer," said Ila.

"Yes."

"Two winters of medicine for Ranu. Paid for without haggling, in silver with a corner cut off."

"Yes."

"My brother's betrothed sat in that guest-house and told me the story as a mystery. And you came to the table twice and sat across from her and said nothing."

"I was not asked," said Kunal, "and silence is free." Then, before she could answer: "That's too neat. I'm sorry. I was asked, in effect, by everything. I chose not to."

She waited. If you waited, the rope did half the work.

"The silver was my uncle's," said Kunal. "From Garh Neshar's stores, the ones he holds in the crown's name. I took it a bag at a time and ran it down the back road through the lower lanes. Two winters. The regent was selling grain to this village at four times the autumn price, and I paid for medicine with the profit." He looked at her. "It was the only honest use I could find for it."

Her hands were warm in their mittens. She could feel that they were warm.

"Would you have mapped the back road?" said Kunal. "The road Nimmi's runners used. If you'd known, when you took the commission, that a road existed where a man was keeping children alive. Would you have drawn it?"

It was the question she had been dreading, because she knew the answer. She could tell the truth, or she could say something kind, and the rope would show which.

"Yes," said Ila.

She said it clearly. She kept her eyes off her fingers, though she wanted to look. Beside her the terrace wall hummed faintly in the wind and stayed low and level.

"I'd have drawn it," she said. "It's a true road. I'd have given a copy to the queen and to the council, and I'd have believed that was neutral. I still don't know it wasn't." She took her mitten off, and her fingers lay pink in the cold. "I don't like that you've seen my hands."

"I like it very much," said Kunal quietly, "and I find I mind that I do."

He said it without a smile, and she was glad. She put the mitten back on.

* * *

Nimmi met them at her own gate, a broad woman with a face like a fist and a voice that listed things.

"Surveyor. Cough, you can hear. No fever. The Dev boy has gained four pounds. Come with me. You've a brother who stood on this slope and I want you to see the rest of it."

She never asked what the rope was, or why a prince stopped at eight spans below the last terrace, respectfully, with his back turned.

The graves lay in the lowest terrace cut, in rows, with the frozen earth heaped over each. Ila began to count because there was nothing else she could do with them. The small mounds were child-length. Some carried a pine bough. One carried a wooden goat with one leg missing. One carried a spinning top, set upright in the dirt as though the child had just let go.

"Thirty-two," said Nimmi, when she had finished. "Thirty-one at your brother's first count. The thirty-second was a man who'd nursed all of them. Your brother held the rope while we dug."

Ila's throat did what it had done at the winter line. "He told the Fair thirty-two."

"He said it straight. I've heard worse said in better coats." Nimmi stood with her arms folded. "We were to burn that night. Every marked house. You've heard it."

"I heard there were torches."

"There were torches. There was an officer on a tall grey horse in a slate cloak, with a grey beard, who rode up to the captain with the red moustache and said lower them. And the captain lowered them." She said it flatly. "I stood on that step and looked at his face the whole time. So I could say it afterwards."

"Who was he?" said Ila. "My brother never learned his name. The assembly's paper says the regent's general was exiled. It doesn't say why."

Nimmi's eyes went to the slope, to the graves, and came back. "I know who he was."

"Then—"

"That's not mine to spend," said Nimmi.

It was said without heat. Ila had met a hundred witnesses who would not speak, and most were afraid. Nimmi was not afraid. She was holding something that did not belong to her, and had decided to hold it shut.

"Ranu will say it, when it's time," said Nimmi. "Not before. Ask me what I'll tell you."

Ila thought. "Is he alive?"

"He's alive."

It was a small answer, and it opened something larger beneath it. A man who gave an order that saved thirty-odd houses, and had been sent away for it, and had a healer who would not speak his name. Ila wrote nothing down. She kept her hand on the field book and left it there, and for the first time in eleven winters she understood that not writing was also a measurement.

* * *

"The walnut," she said, when they were climbing again.

Kunal had said nothing for several minutes. He walked a pace ahead, scanning the slope, one hand idle on the rope. "Ask."

"Why a walnut? Why red thread? You gave it to that boy like it was his allowance."

"It's the oldest thing I own that isn't a name." He glanced back. "Route-keepers send true word with a walnut tied in red thread. Not a letter, not a seal. If a runner comes to a hamlet with news and no walnut, the news is a lie, or it's from someone who isn't mine. We've kept it ten generations. The regent's men never found it, because it looks like a gift for children."

"So when a boy brings a walnut, the message is true."

"When a boy brings a walnut, I'd stake a village on it." He seemed to hear what he had said. "I'm about to. One goes to Sarn fold before dark. Nimmi's eldest runner is saddling now."

She walked on. The terraces gave out above them into scrub, and the path became a stony track. She turned her own mind over, honestly.

She had arrived in Neshar with a picture of him, and the picture was tidy. A prince of his uncle's house, a man who spoke of openness and kept every road in his head. A man playing at rebellion for a throne, with a good voice for it. She had even named him, privately, after a peak she did not like.

That picture did not account for a kneeling man in mud, or two winters of stolen silver, or a walnut, or a man who hated sending others to his own door. It was not wrong in every line. He did keep every road in his head. He had told her so himself, by omission, at every stair. But she had taken his measure from too far off. He was not his uncle's kind of prince. She did not yet know what kind he was.

Where the track forked, a line of old worn stones led off to the right, up behind the terraces and over a bare spur. She had not seen it from the village. It did not appear on her sheets.

"That one," said Kunal.

"What is it?"

"The salt road." He shrugged. "Ranu's runners use it to go round Garh Neshar's gates. It runs up behind the terraces, over two spurs, and comes down on the dry side of the Garh. No warden has walked it in fifty winters."

She had her book out before he had finished. She sat on a flat rock with her cold fingers and drew it: the first spur, the angle of the terrace walls below it, the long slope of scree that carried the road up through the broken ground. She paced it. Two and a half spans a pace. She took a bearing off the spur and another off the corner of Nimmi's roof, and the road came up out of the paper line by line.

"Where is it unlawful?" she said.

"Nine places. I'm fairly sure the smugglers have counted them."

"Then count them for me."

He did, slowly, and she put a small neat cross at each. A ford on the closed side of the border stones. A gap knocked through a boundary wall. A goat-bridge over a toll gully. Six more. Under the lee of the second spur there was a stone-ringed hollow with a soot-black hearth, where Nimmi's runners halted to sleep out of the wind, and she drew that too, a little ring with a flame in it.

He stood at the end of the rope and watched her, and the watching lay on her neck like a hand. She kept her pencil level.

It was a true road. It was also a lifeline. When the page was done she held it up to the light, and the nine crosses looked back at her like nine charges.

"A warden could hang men from this page," said Ila. "Here, and here, and the place where they sleep. You know that."

"I do."

"I drew it anyway." She turned it so he could see. "That's what a map is, Highness. I told you at the table. If I leave out a lifeline to be kind, it's not a kinder map. It's a false one."

When she looked up, his face was the one she had seen at the cairn, the open one, startled by itself.

"I was waiting," said Kunal slowly, "for you to say you wouldn't."

"Were you disappointed?"

"I'm trying to find out."

They went on up the track. The wind had risen, and the cold was a hard clean one. Above the last terrace, where the scrub ended, a little cairn stood against the sky, a knee-high stack of grey stones with a flat cap. It was so small that she would have walked past it. Down the slope behind it, faintly, she thought she saw a pale thread of blue.

Kunal stopped beside it and put a hand on the top stone, as he had put his hand on the cold north wall of the tower.

"This is the salt road," he said. "It's the last way I'll show you that every smuggler in Neshar already knows. Anyone with a mule has walked it. You can draw it for the queen and you'll take nothing from anyone."

She looked at the page in her hand, and then at him. Every smuggler in Neshar. Nobody on that sheet was in more danger than the day before.

"You chose it for that," said Ila. "The one road where drawing costs nobody anything. You wanted to see whether I'd draw it, and what I'd say when I did. I was the weight on a scale."

"Yes," said Kunal. He did not apologise.

"And after this?"

"After this, you earn them."

The sentence sat in her ears like a stone dropped down a well, and she listened for the bottom of it. Behind her lay the village and the thirty-two graves. Ahead lay the road to a valley that did not exist on her sheets, and a man who had decided, and told her, that the next lines would cost her something.

She did not know what the price was. She did not know what he would count as earning. She knew only that a man who named a price before she asked had been thinking about it for a long time.

Ila shut the field book on the salt road, with her pencil in it.

"Price first," she said.
