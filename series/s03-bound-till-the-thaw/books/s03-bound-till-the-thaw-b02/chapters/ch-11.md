# Chapter 11: Folded Gloves

The storm took the far cairn first, then the near one, then the end of the rope.

Ila watched the rope go. One moment it ran eight spans of pale wool to Kunal's wrist. The next it ran into white and came back out again at his glove. The wind had a voice now, a long flat roar with grit in it, and it came straight over the lip of the bowl.

"Down!" she shouted, and pointed with the axe.

The double cairn stood ten paces back, two heaps of stone with a drift piled in the lee between them. A wall of packed snow, deep as a man was tall. She had noted it an hour ago, the way she noted everything, and never thought she would need it.

"We dig," said Ila. "In. Now."

"With what?"

She held up the axe in one hand and the cooking pot in the other.

He took the pot.

It was ugly work. She cut blocks from the drift with the axe and hauled them clear. Kunal bailed loose snow out behind her with the pot, on his knees, in a rhythm that did not break. Neither of them wasted breath. The snow came sideways. It filled the hole as fast as they emptied it, and she began to count, because counting was what she had. Forty strokes. Sixty. A hand, an arm, a shoulder deep.

At two hundred she could sit up inside. At three hundred she could lie flat. She cut a bench along one wall, shoulder-wide, and a shelf above it for the packs. Then she poked her axe haft up through the roof for air and plugged the door with the biggest pack.

The roar dropped to a hum.

Inside, the world was blue. It came through the snow from the old cairn above, a sickly shine, enough to see his face by. His brows were white. There was ice in his lashes. He was grinning like a boy.

"Two hundred and ninety strokes," said Ila.

"You counted?"

"I always count."

"That's a very comforting thing to say," said Kunal, and she remembered who had said it first, and looked away.

* * *

There could be no fire. The lip had no fuel and the wind would have killed any flame. They ate sattu, the dry flour wetted with snow, scooped in cold lumps from the lid of the pot. It tasted of nothing and sat like clay in the belly. Ila ate hers because it was fuel too, of a kind.

They lay on the bench with the packs behind them, a hand apart, and let the cold settle round the edges. The rope lay in a loop on the floor. It did not hum. Nothing needed to be true yet.

"A name," said Ila, because the rules were the rules. "Tonight's."

Kunal thought. "The two heaps. The double cairn." He turned his head against the snow. "Shepherds on Kuhl side call them the Two Aunts. They stand together and disagree about everything."

She found the pencil in her cuff and wrote it by the blue light, exactly. *Two Aunts.* "Who's the second aunt?"

"Whichever one you ask."

She nearly laughed and did not. The cold had made her careful with her face.

"My question," said Kunal. He had said it softly, as if the walls could be woken. "Not about your orders. Not tonight. I'd rather know why you do it. Why this map, why this much of your life. A woman doesn't walk eleven winters of hills for a salary."

It was a better question than the one she had braced for. She lay and looked at the snow ceiling and felt the answer come up from a place she did not usually go.

"My father copied maps," said Ila.

"Copied?"

"At the Taravan survey office, for thirty years. Other men went out and measured. He sat at a desk and made the fair copy, in ink, with a ruling pen. He was very good. You couldn't tell his hand from the original." She heard her own voice go flat and precise, the voice she used for a bad figure. "Every one of those maps has a surveyor's name in the corner. Seven hundred, perhaps. His name is on none of them."

"Not one?"

"Copyists don't sign. It's a rule." She turned the pencil in her fingers. "He used to say it was an honour to be exact. He used to say it so often I stopped hearing it. Then he went blind in the left eye at fifty-nine, and the office gave him a pension and a clock."

The blue light lay across Kunal's face. He did not say he was sorry. He said, "So you'll put your name in the corner."

"I'll put it on the whole sheet. Chief Surveyor, and the whole sheet, and nobody's hand underneath mine."

"That's all?"

"That's quite a lot."

"I didn't say it was small." He was quiet, then went on in a different voice. "It's why you wrote *K. avoids NE fork* and not *hides.* Because you know what it is to be copied out of your own work."

She had not thought of it that way. She lay very still, and found it was true, and found that nobody had ever put it so.

* * *

The wind rose and fell over the roof like surf. Hours went by, or what felt like hours. Her toes had gone from pain to a dull, far-off ache she did not trust.

"My turn," said Ila. She had not meant to say it. The words arrived on their own. "You gave me a name. I asked you a question on the cliff and you answered. I'll give you something that isn't in the field book."

"You don't have to."

"I know. That's why."

He turned on his side, so he could see her.

"My grandfather was a warden," she said. "Of the Shilak post. Sixty winters ago. Before there was a hall, before the Closing Law. He went out in a night storm to check the cairns for a lost party, and he didn't come back."

"I'm sorry."

"They found him at the thaw. He was on the slope above the third bend, and he hadn't fallen. He wasn't hurt. He was sitting against a flat stone, quite whole. And his gloves were folded on the stone beside him. One on the other." She held up her bare hands and laid the left flat over the right. "Like that. Folded."

"Folded," said Kunal.

"No one in my family has ever known why."

She had told it before, three times in her life. Each time the listener had said *how sad* or *the cold makes men strange* and moved on, and she had let them, because it was easier. She waited for it now. It was already dull in her mouth, a thing worn smooth.

"How exactly?" said Kunal.

She turned her head.

"The gloves. How were they laid? Left on top, or right? Palms in, or palms out? Were the cuffs to the stone's edge, or the fingers?" He was not smiling. He had propped himself on one elbow, and he looked at her as she looked at a baseline. "Did they lie square? Or like someone who'd set them down quickly?"

Nobody had asked. In sixty winters, not one person had asked.

Her father had not asked, and her brother had asked only once, whether it was true, and their father had poured a drink and not answered. She had a picture in her head and had never had anyone to hand it to. She took a breath and found it was unsteady.

"Left on top," said Ila. "Palms in, together. Square to the edge of the stone. The fingers pointed the way he'd been walking." She stopped. "Like a pair set out for the morning. That's what my father said, once, and then wished he hadn't."

"Set out," said Kunal, very quietly.

"Yes."

"That isn't what a frozen man does. A man dying of cold drops things. He doesn't square them."

"No."

"Someone folded those gloves with a steady hand, and knew what he was doing."

She could not speak. There was something in her throat. It had never, in sixty winters, had a place to go, and now it had one, and it did not know what to do with itself.

Kunal did not say *I understand.* He did not touch her. He lay with his elbow on the bench and let her have the next ten breaths, and then he said, "Thank you for telling me," in the plain voice a man uses for a plain thing.

She thought of Prince Udai, who had walked up the princes' way sixty winters ago with a guest, and come down alone. She thought of the number sixty. She held it in her closed hand like a coin, and told him nothing about it.

It was free. It was a silence, and silence was free. She lay awake and knew exactly how much it cost.

* * *

The storm stood on the roof all the next day.

They knew it by the sound and by the dark. Twice Ila dug out the vent and found the world a white wall, the cairn-light a smear in it. The cold crept deeper. Kunal's lips had a blue tinge, and when she looked at her own nails they were the colour of old candles. They ate more sattu. She counted the packets, the one thing she could do, and there were six days in them, and then four if the cloud did not lift.

At dusk, by her watch, he began to shake.

It was a small shake, the kind a man tries to keep in his jaw. She watched it for ten breaths. She was a woman who measured, and she had measured wrong.

"We're both losing it," said Ila. "The heat. The packs are no use, they're as cold as we are." She made herself say the next part clearly. "Two bodies keep warmer than two bodies apart. It's arithmetic. I'm saying it plainly so you know it's arithmetic."

"Is that an order?"

"It's a proposal."

"I accept." His teeth were chattering, and his voice was dry as dust. "On the terms stated."

They did it carefully. She turned her back to him on the bench, and he put his arms round her and folded the coat over them both, and the whole length of him lay behind her like a wall that was only slowly learning to be a wall. Her shoulder blades found his chest. His breath went into her hair. She could feel the shaking in him, and then, after a while, a little less of it, and then, after longer, a heat that was no longer hers or his.

"I should say," said Ila, to the snow in front of her, "that this isn't harbouring."

"It isn't?"

"Under the new law. Shared shelter on a licensed crossing is expressly lawful. Clause nine. My brother and the council argued it for a week."

"Ah."

"Under the old law you'd have hanged me."

"Under the old law," said Kunal, behind her ear, "I'd have been the one hanged. For harbouring a crown surveyor. In a snow cave."

"Is that worse?"

"It's worse for the cave."

She began to laugh. It started low in her chest and she tried to stop it, and it only got bigger, and then she felt him laughing too, helplessly, the whole of him, against her back. It shook the packs. A little powder of snow fell from the roof onto the pair of them. She laughed until her eyes ran and the ache went out of her ribs.

"It's worse for the cave," she wheezed. "You said it so seriously."

"It's a very serious cave."

"It's a beautifully measured cave."

She felt him go still. Then, lower, "That's the first time I've heard you laugh out loud," said Kunal.

"I do it all the time."

"Not near me."

She thought about it. It was true. She lay in the circle of his arm, with her back warm, and did not move away.

* * *

"Half of nine," said Kunal.

It was the second night. The wind had dropped a little. She could tell by the voice of it, which had lowered to a groan. They had not moved from the bench, and nothing in her wanted to.

"I'm sorry?"

"You gave me your grandfather. It would be uneven to hold back." His breath moved in her hair. "I was nine. That's the age. I'll give you half."

She said nothing. She had learned, this winter, that saying nothing was sometimes the response.

"My father died that winter," said Kunal. "He was the kind of man who left a room better lit than he found it. Everyone said so. My uncle was his younger brother, and he came to the funeral, and a week after it he took me up a ridge above the house. To see the valley, he said. To see what would be mine." The breath stopped. It started again. "And I came home."

He did not go on.

She waited. She heard the whole weight of what was not said. She heard that he had stopped on the edge of a slope and was standing on it, not going over, not going back. She did not ask what happened on the ridge. She did not ask what *and I came home* meant, or why a boy of nine would say it like that. She knew a refusal when it was held out to her, like a glove, and it was his to hold.

"Thank you," said Ila. She turned her head and put it on the white wool of his sleeve. It was all the answer she could think of, and she had no way to measure whether it was enough.

He let out a long breath.

"You didn't ask," said Kunal.

"You didn't say I could."

"No." He was quiet. "Most people would."

"Most people aren't good at leaving a hole in a map."

He made a sound that was almost a laugh and almost not. His arm tightened round her, once, then eased.

They lay and listened to the wind. After a long while he said, "My question, then. My real one. I'd rather ask it tonight than in the morning."

She knew, at once, that this was the one. She had known it was coming since the lip, since the sheet, since the line he had read twice in the cloud. She felt it the way she felt a slope under her boot.

"Ask," said Ila.

"Suppose you found a way. A true way, through the hills. One that let my uncle's men into every hamlet of Neshar, down to the last fold, with no road they could be stopped on." He said it level, without heat. "A way nobody had drawn. And you were the one who'd found it. Would you draw it?"

The cave was very quiet. The cairn-light made the snow overhead into a slow blue sky.

She could have said *I don't know.* That would have been free. She could have said *it depends who asks,* and that would have been free too. She lay with her back to his chest and her hands in her lap, and looked at them in the light, and they were pink and warm.

"Yes," said Ila.

Her hands stayed warm. The rope on the floor lay still. It did not whiten her fingertips and it did not hum, and she had never in her life wished so much that a thing had been a lie.

He did not move. His arms stayed round her, and for a moment she thought he had not heard. Then she felt him turn his face away, out of her hair, toward the wall of snow, and stay there.

"A map is true or it's false," she said. She heard how thin it sounded. "What it does after is not the map's business. If I drew less than I found I'd be lying on paper. I won't. Not for the queen. Not for you."

"I know," said Kunal, to the snow.

"I'd tell you first. I'd tell you before I drew it."

"I know."

"That isn't nothing."

"It isn't nothing," he agreed. His voice was perfectly gentle. It was the voice of a man setting something down that he had carried a long way. "It's very much more than a man could ask for. It's only that I'd hoped you'd lie."

She lay quite still with the answer in her mouth. It was true, and it was hers, and it was warm. Outside, the storm went on talking to the stones.
