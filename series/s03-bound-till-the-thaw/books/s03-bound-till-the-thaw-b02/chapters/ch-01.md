# Chapter 1: The North Wall

Ila had never been wrong about a distance she had walked herself, so when the tower came up short she walked it again.

Two and a half spans to a pace. She had checked that stride against the brass rule at Taravan when she was nineteen, and against a chalk line her father laid every spring after. The brass had drifted twice. Her legs had not.

The yard of Garh Neshar lay grey and trampled under a sky the colour of old pewter. It was the eve of the Snow Fair, the thirty-first winter of Queen Sarala's reign. Down on the river meadow someone was testing a drum, one slow boom at a time, and the smell of roasting barley had begun to climb the hill. At the yard's far end stood the tower where Regent Pritam sat under guard, square and ugly, and lying to her.

She stopped at the north-west corner and turned to face the wall. Outside, the north face ran twenty-two paces. Inside, that afternoon, with the hill-council captain tapping his boot in the doorway, she had paced the long room behind it and taken off the wall's thickness, which the window cut gave her to the finger. The room should have run a full pace longer than it did.

A pace was two and a half spans. She allowed half a span for a drunk mason and sloppy plaster. That left two.

"Kiran," she said. "Chain."

He was sitting on the mounting block with the brass chain across his knees, chewing a strip of dried apricot. He was twenty-five now. Since the new law went up he had stopped looking like a man who expected to be arrested for the rope he'd once tied. "I did chain it, Ila-ji. Twice. It said what you said."

"Then do it a third time. I like the answer better when I've made someone else do the work."

He got up, grumbling, and walked the end of the chain along the foot of the wall. The links rang one against another, cold and bright. At the corner he held his end to the stone and she read the last link against her mark.

Twenty-two paces, outside. She opened her field book and wrote under it, in the cramped hand she used for things that mattered: *North wall, two spans out of true, void?*

She did not like question marks. She left this one anyway. A question mark was honest. A guess dressed as a measurement was not.

"Void," said Kiran, reading over her arm. "That's where he keeps it."

"Keeps what?"

"His conscience. Two spans is about right. Tight little room, nobody visits." He grinned. The grin was quick and slightly too bright, the way it always was when he had decided in advance that nothing was serious.

Ila snorted. Then the joke snagged on something. Conscience, kept in a locked place. Last autumn she had written to the clerk of the Taravan archive for the Shilak Cairn file, and he had written back, very pleasantly, that the crown kept that one in a drawer she had no key to. She tucked the pencil behind her ear and looked up at the tower, and the thing she had been carrying for a month came out of her before she had decided to say it.

"Rohan wrote that the stones at the Shilak Cairn spoke at the thaw," she said. "Everyone there felt it in their hands."

"Stones talk," said Kiran. "Mine mostly say 'move your foot.'"

It fell flat, and he heard it fall. Kiran's grin did not drop. It simply stopped being used. His hand rose to his left ear, to the torn rim, and rubbed it once with two fingers.

He did that when the weather turned, and when the talk did.

Ila watched the fingers. Then she went on, more quietly. "They said the Old Bargain is unpaid. The pass closes for good after the fourth winter unless somebody pays. And now the crown wants every way across mapped, and the archive won't show me what it knows about the cairn. I'd like to know whether a map of a pass that is going to close is a map or an obituary."

"Ila."

"Yes?"

"Cold," he said. "It aches when it's about to snow." He bent and began to wind the chain onto its frame, link over link, very carefully. "Come on. The light's going. You'll want to eat before you start measuring the kitchen."

She let it be. Silence was not an answer, but it was a measurement of a kind, and she wrote it down in her head with a mark beside it. His sister had tied a rope on Ila's brother last winter, at the cairn where the law began. Kiran was about to become some sort of brother to her too, and she had no intention of prying him open in a yard.

* * *

They went out by the main gate, where the notice was.

It was nailed to the left post, the new law in eleven lines on good rag paper, with two seals pressed under it in two colours of wax: the Shilak post's brown, the hill council's blue. Ila knew the eleven lines by heart. She had stood beside her brother while he cut the last of them down from twenty-six. *Licences carry two seals. Medicine always passes. No person is hanged or held to blame for a death on a bound crossing.* Somebody had already scratched a small, careful goat into the margin beside the third line. The clerks had not scraped it off.

The road below the gate was busier than a winter road should have been. Two pairs of crossers were coming down off the Neshar road, each pair tied wrist to wrist with a short grey rope, each carrying a roll of oilcloth strapped flat to the back. A clerk in a blue sleeve stopped each pair, read the paper they held out, and compared the seals with deep suspicion. Redbark. She could smell it, a dry bitter smell like the inside of a medicine chest. A year ago it had come over the pass in the dark, and a crosser could hang for carrying it.

"They're allowed to smile," she told the second pair, who were looking at the clerk as if he were a hanging judge.

The man did, uncertainly. The woman tugged the rope, and they went on.

"Hill-council men on the grain," Kiran said, nodding along the lane. Four of them stood in the lee of the long barn, in undyed wool with blue cords on their shoulders, leaning on spears they clearly did not enjoy. The barn doors had a new bar and a new lock. The old lock hung on a nail beside it, so everyone could see it had been replaced. "Pritam's grain. They count it every morning and put the number on a slate. Chetu's boy told me yesterday it's gone up by a sack since the last count."

"Up?"

"Somebody gave it back." He shrugged. "Or somebody's lying about the slate."

"Slates don't lie. People who write on them do." She meant it as a joke and it came out as a rule. She frowned.

Beyond the barn the lane dropped toward the guest-house, and the drum on the meadow boomed again, closer now. Kiran kicked at a frozen rut.

"I watched Varun go out of that gate in the summer," he said, not looking at her. "Hands tied, a guard each side. Rohan's old lieutenant, the one who sold the regent every road he guarded. I never heard what happened when he got to Taravan. Did Rohan say?"

"He said Varun cried at the Taravan gate," said Ila. "Rohan's never said what he felt about it, but he mentioned the crying twice." She considered. "Once in each letter. He's not subtle."

Kiran laughed, and the laugh was real this time, and they walked the rest of the way in a better temper.

* * *

The guest-house kitchen had been given to her in the first week of snow, with the cook's blessing and the cook's own stool. Ila lit two lamps instead of one and spread the sheets across the table.

She did not need to unroll them. She knew what was on them. But she liked to see them under light, the way some people like to count coins.

Twelve sheets, fixed at the corners with river stones. The summer passes lay across them in her finest ink: every col, every scree-slope, every saddle that a laden goat could manage, with contours so close that the paper looked grey in places. The commission had come up with the summer post, sealed in the queen's wax. It was short. *Map every winter way.* Not a word on how.

She had drawn what she could reach without being killed. Of the winter ways she had nothing. A bound pair could cross a closed pass, and the stones would light the line for them, and nobody had ever drawn it, because a map was a thing you made in daylight and the winter line was seen only by the bound.

She ran her thumb down the edge of the nearest sheet. There on the third ridge was the little hand-lettered name she should not have lettered: *Tamar's Shoulder*, for the cook who had fed her when she was seven and sulking. Farther east, a long ugly knob of rock she'd have entered a year ago as Point 4410 was *Kiran's Elbow*. She had other names too. A ridge for her brother. A tarn for a ferryman who'd once shared his lunch. Nobody had asked her to name them. The crown's own ledger called the first Point 4410 and the cook's ridge Point 4392, and the queen's clerks would, she supposed, cross out every name of hers.

A map was true or it was false. She believed that the way she believed her own legs. And she knew, in the part of her that was honest about small things, that naming a peak after a person she liked was not a measurement at all.

She turned the lamp up.

Without the winter ways the map would sit in the Taravan archive as a very good summer map. They would nod at it. They would thank her. And the chief surveyor's chair, which had been promised by a dozen men to a dozen other people, would go to Pell Orlan, who had drawn three hills from a window and never been cold in his life.

Her winter licence went before the hill council tomorrow, at the Fair. With it, the council would name a licensed guide for her, and she could be bound to him at the cairn and cross the Saddle. Without it she was a woman with a lovely sheet of paper, and Orlan would have the chair by spring.

She stared at the sheets for a long time. Then she picked up her chain, her field book and a lantern, because she had remembered something, and the remembering was like a stone in a boot.

The stair. She had counted it from the outside, twice. She had never counted it from the north landing, where the room began.

* * *

The tower stair was a spiral of grey stone steps with an iron rope for a rail, cold enough to burn through her glove. The guard at the foot, a hill-council man with a fresh blue cord on his shoulder, looked at her pass, then at her chain, then at the sky, and waved her up with the air of a man who would rather not be asked later what he'd seen.

She climbed with the lantern low and counted aloud under her breath.

On the north landing she stopped. The stair here widened to a flat of old boards, and the north wall, rough-dressed and sweating a little, ran a few paces away to the dark of the long room's door. She put her palm on the wall. It was colder than the rest. Not very much. She might not have noticed it if she had not been looking.

"The crown sent you to measure a prison."

The voice came from below her, pleasant and level. She did not jump. She put the lantern down on the step and turned.

Prince Kunal of Neshar stood three steps down with one hand loose on the iron rail. He was thirty-three, tall in the way mountain men were tall, with a plain dark coat and a long face that looked as if it had been made for laughing and had not been used for it lately. He had no circlet. He had a tired look and a small ink stain on his right thumb. She had seen him twice across a drafting table in the guest-house, arguing over a rope.

"Highness," she said.

"Kunal, on a stair." He came up one step. "I'd like to know why the crown wants a prison measured."

"You'd have to ask the crown."

"Would the crown tell me?"

"It might." Ila lifted her chin. "It told me. The commission says every way. A tower with a prince's uncle in it is, in the narrow sense, a way."

"Is it?" said Kunal. "A way to where?"

She had stood at the drafting table beside her brother and a man who answered every question with another one, and she had found it funny. It was less funny at close quarters, on a cold stair, with her lantern at his feet. She looked at him.

"How many steps from the yard door to here, Highness?"

"Does it matter?"

"I'd like to know if you know."

"Thirty-nine," he said at once. He said it easily, a child's number, one that had been counted a thousand times with a hand on the rail. "That's what everyone gets."

"Forty."

"Is it?"

"There's a half-height step under the north landing, just there." She pointed with her chin. It was set into the boards so low that her boot would have missed it if she had not been counting. "Everyone steps over it. Nobody counts it. It's still a step."

Kunal looked at it. For a moment, only a moment, he looked at the north wall beside it instead, the cold wet length of it, and then back at her face.

"Children's count," he said. "My cousins and I learned thirty-nine. We were small and the step was for ghosts."

"You can't leave out a step because it's embarrassing."

"Can't I?" He smiled slightly. "I'm told it's done in every court in the world."

She stopped herself laughing. It was a close thing. She held up the field book instead.

"The north wall," she said. "Inside, the long room is two spans shorter than the outside allows. I've walked it three times and had my chain-man do it twice. There's a void."

She said it flat out. She was a surveyor, and a number was most of what she had. And she watched him, because she was also a person who watched.

Kunal stood still. After a second he stepped past her onto the landing and put the flat of his hand on the cold stone, the same place she had, as if it might say something.

"Do you know," he said, "how often my family has been told our house is crooked?"

"Often, I'd guess."

"Garh Neshar was built by a mason named Madan, who was drunk for most of the work. He's in the family's songs, mostly for that. The kitchen leans, the hall has a floor like a stormy lake and if you put a ball at the door of the east wing it will roll out the window." He turned and leaned against the wall with his arms folded. "A drunk man built a lopsided tower. Add it to your demands at council tomorrow. I'm sure you have a list."

"I have no list."

"None at all?"

"I have a licence application, which asks the council to name a guide and bind me to him at the cairn, so I can cross the Saddle in winter and draw the passes. It's four lines. I wrote them myself." She heard her own voice go brisk. "You read it. You were at the table when we cut the Law down to eleven lines. You argued I should say ten spans and not eight, and when I said eight, exactly, you asked whose arm."

"I did ask whose arm."

"It was the guide's own."

"I remember." Something moved behind his face. "It was a good answer."

The drum on the meadow boomed once, very faint through the stone.

Kunal pushed himself upright. He was not smiling any more. He looked almost kind, which she distrusted more than she had distrusted the smile.

"I'll say it straight, Surveyor, since you've been straight with me. Tomorrow I will speak against your licence."

She waited. He didn't add anything.

The field book lay against her ribs with its one question mark. Tomorrow the council would ask what she had found in the yard, and a void was a number she could give them. Give it, and she would be the crown's woman prying at a prince's wall, handing him the best reason to vote her down. Hold it back, and her licence might survive his speech. Orlan's chair against her own creed. She had never once drawn a line she knew was false, and silence was a line of a kind. She turned it over like a loose tooth. Keep it out of council until she knew what it was, she decided, and hated the decision at once.

"Against," said Ila.

"Against."

"All of it? Or the crossing?"

"Does it matter?"

"Highness," she said, "I measured your uncle's tower for six weeks. I know when I'm being given a number."

He opened his mouth to answer, and she saw the question coming, ready and polished, and something in her came loose.

She laughed. It burst out of her, loud and sudden, off the stone and down the stair, a bray that had been getting her sent from dinner tables since she was eleven. It rang in the stairwell. Below, the guard must have flinched. She put the back of her hand to her mouth and could not stop.

"Against," she managed. "You came up a spiral stair in the dark to tell me, to my face, that you'll vote against me, and then you tried to give me a stair count to take with me."

Kunal stared at her. It was the first time she had seen him with nothing to say. He looked startled, as a man looks when the door he has put his shoulder to turns out to have been open all along.

"I did say it straight out," he said at last, and for once it was not a question.

"You did." She wiped her eye with a knuckle and picked up her lantern. "Thank you. It's the most honest thing anyone in this house has said to me since the snow." She stepped carefully over the half-height step, into the light. "Good night, Highness. It's forty."

She went down the stair counting, and did not look back, and on the thirty-first step she began to wonder what he had been standing so very close to the north wall to hide.
