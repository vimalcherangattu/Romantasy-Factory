# Chapter 21: A Promise with a Date

Gauri knocked with the flat of her hand, twice, as if the door might be asleep.

Ila had been up for an hour. She sat on the floor of the herders' hut with a field sheet pinned under her knee and a pencil lead warming in her fist, and the rope lay slack across the boards to the shelf where Kunal slept with one arm flung over his eyes. She had not slept much. She had spent the grey hours counting the roof slates, and the count had come out the same every time.

"Open," said Gauri through the planks. "I've bread, and I've news, and I'm not carrying either back down."

Kunal was awake before the bolt was drawn. He sat up with the blanket at his waist and his face already shut, the courtier's face, and Ila saw him put it on like a coat.

Gauri came in smelling of cold. She set the bread on the shelf, looked once at the two of them and once at the rope, and said nothing about any of it. It was a kind of courtesy Ila was beginning to value above speeches.

"Dhanu's boy was out on the north shore at first light," she said. "Two pairs. Four men. They're on the far side of the basin, past the Eye."

"Raghu's?" said Kunal.

"Grey wool, no blue cord. The boy says they walk like men who've been told to be quiet." She drew a line in the air from right to left. "Tracks along the west side of the Eye, close under the cliff. Fresh. They've been looking at the water."

Ila's pencil had stopped. "How far from the cleft?"

"A morning's walk. Less, if they stop looking at the water."

*Sixteen men bound,* Ila had thought last night, over the soup, and had not said it. Here was the first four, small as a pencil mark on a sheet.

"They'd have come down the Kuhl shoulder," she said. "That's the only way onto the west shore without crossing the ice." She heard herself, brisk and exact, and found it a comfort. "If they have the shoulder, they have a view down the whole basin. The Ladder's foot, the path to the lake huts, and the slope under this hut."

"Dhanu says the same," said Gauri, with a short nod, as if Ila had passed another inspection. "He wants you both told before the sun's up the cliff. After that he'll ask what you mean to do."

She went. The door banged. The bread steamed on the shelf, and neither of them touched it.

"Tonight," said Kunal. "We go into the Eye tonight. The cleft is the one place four men with spyglasses can't follow unless they're bound and know where it opens." He rubbed his face with a bandaged hand. "Draw me what's behind us. Dhanu will need to know which ways to shut."

It was the sort of request she could do in her sleep. She had walked every one of those ways with a chain.

"I'll draw it," said Ila.

* * *

She drew the exits.

The Widow's Ladder first, because it was the biggest and the easiest, with its four pitches and its hundred and sixty steps. Then the shore path along the Mirror, with the lakeside cairn at nine hundred and ten paces from the scree edge. The foot trail from the lower huts. The scree slope where they sat now, steep and loose, with a sheep track going up it that no one had ever bothered to name. The cleft at the head of the Eye, as far as she had seen it, which was as far as a dropped spike would fall in a count of three.

She did it true. She could not do it any other way. The line was what her feet had made it, and the numbers sat beside it in her small square hand, bearings and paces and the fall of the ground.

It was only halfway through the Kuhl shoulder that she saw what she was making.

Every line on it was a door. Every bearing was a key. Raghu's four men would pay dearly for this sheet. The hills would pay to keep it from them, and the crown might do anything with a copy. It was also an excellent piece of work. The closures were tight. There was not one wrong figure on it.

*A map is only true or false.* She had said it to herself for eleven years like a woman checking a latch. She looked at the shore path, sharp as a hair on the paper, and for the first time the sentence did not close.

"You've stopped," said Kunal.

He had come to sit against the wall opposite, with his bandaged hands round a bowl of tea. He had eaten the bread. He was watching her draw, as he always did, with the open and slightly stunned look of a man watching someone do a thing he can't.

"I'm checking a closure," said Ila.

"Does it close?"

"Yes." She turned the sheet toward him. "That's the trouble."

He looked. He did not reach for it. His eyes went along the Ladder and the shore and the cleft, and she saw him name each place in his head, in the old speech. He did not say any of them.

"It's very good," he said.

"It's the best sheet I've made in eleven years." She tried a laugh, and it came out short. "And if it goes the wrong way, it opens the whole basin like a purse."

"So don't let it go the wrong way."

"Which way is that?" said Ila.

He opened his mouth and shut it. She watched him decide not to say the neat thing.

She took her pattu from the peg and turned the hem out. The corner of cream paper still showed through the loosened stitch. She had meant to mend that before he woke. She sat with the needle and thread and the sheet folded small, and unpicked the lining along the side seam, above the hem, where it would not show. Then she laid the sheet against the wool with the three older ones from the Stair and the Moon, and began to sew them in. The queen's letter slept in the hem below.

Kunal said nothing. He watched her stitches go in, small and flat and close.

He had asked her for the exits. Not the sheets. It would have been simpler to give him the paper. She did not.

"Ila," said Kunal.

She knew from the weight in his voice what was coming. It had been in the room since dawn, with the hem and her hand over it.

"I promised you I'd ask nothing back," he said. "I'm going to break that, and I want you to hear why first." He set the bowl down. "Raghu's four are under the cliff. Your letter has been sewn into you since the Fair, and it came from the crown's drawer. If it names the cleft, then the crown's friends know the Eye better than I do, and I'd be leading you in blind. I won't do that. Not to you. Not tonight."

He held out both hands, fingers spread, in the light from the door. They were bandaged to the knuckle, and the tips were pink.

"So I'll ask where you can see it."

"Kunal. You promised."

"I know what I promised."

"Then you know what it does to your hands." Her voice had gone hard, and she let it. "You've set a price on the table so I have to look at it. If I refuse, I watch you pay. If I answer, you've bought it with your fingers. That's pressure, whatever you meant by it."

"I know." He did not look away. "I'd rather you were angry than that I walked you blind into the cleft."

"What did the letter say?"

The cold came at once. She saw it arrive. The pink in his fingertips went waxy, then grey, as if a hand had wiped the blood out of them. The rope at his wrist drew taut and still. His breath went out in a short, hard push, and under the bandages the skin lifted in blisters, fat and clear, and the grey beneath them did not change.

He did not drop his hands. He held them where she could see, shaking a little, and kept his eyes on her face.

"I'll pay it," said Kunal.

She was on her knees before she knew she had moved, her hand out to take his. He drew them back against his chest.

"Don't. It's paid." His voice was rough. "Take it onto yourself and you'll be climbing with them as well. I need one of us whole on stone tonight."

She stopped with her hand in the air between them. The sheep track ran up the scree on her sheet, in pencil, in her own hand. A broken promise struck like a great lie, and a great lie killed flesh. Those tips were gone, and nothing she did now would bring them back.

She obeyed. She drew her hand in and set it on her knee, and it was the hardest thing she had done since the Ladder.

She had known the question was coming since the Mirror. She had had the answer ready since the Drum, folded small. *It says what the crown wants of this mountain, and what the crown owes it, and a name I know.* All of it was true. She could have said all of it, and it would not have given him back his fingers, and then she would have told him her grandfather's gloves lay palms together on a stone.

"I'm not going to tell you," said Ila. Then, because he had a reason and deserved an answer to it: "Raghu's four don't need my letter to find a cleft. They've seen its shadow from the shoulder."

"No," said Kunal slowly. "They don't."

The hum did not rise. Her own fingertips stayed pink.

His face did what she had been afraid of. It did not go cold or courtly. It went quite still, and then it opened, a little, and what showed under it was a boy of nine sitting under a rock with a knife, who had asked which way and been told that was the whole lesson.

"Right," he said. "That's yours to refuse."

"It's not about you."

"I know."

"It's not." She heard her voice go up. "It's mine. And an oath I swore before this crossing. I took a commission from the archive, and it binds my silence on what their papers hold. If I break it, I'm a clerk who sold the crown's drawer." She pressed the needle into the wool. "That's *one* reason."

"What's the other?"

"I don't know yet." It was the truth, and it came out of her like something torn. "I don't know what it does to you."

He did not push. That was the worst of it. He nodded slowly and laid his ruined hands on his knees, where she could still see them. He had swallowed the question once before, in the grey light, and now he had asked it and paid and was swallowing the answer, with nothing at all in his face.

She could not bear it.

He had held her weight at the Eye, and his fingertips had burst on the rope. She had said *I want you* on the Drum shore, with her hands out. She had not known you could lie warm in a man's arms all night and still be keeping a locked room in the same house.

"When we're through the mountain," said Ila.

He lifted his head.

"When we're through." She held the sheet against her ribs, and the words were out before she had checked them against anything. "Through the cleft, and the Eye, and whatever's under it. I'll tell you everything in it. The whole of it, and the oath be damned."

The rope hummed.

She felt it through the bone of her wrist, low and level, steady as a held note. It did not climb. It was the sound of a thing meant, and she had made it, and it would not be unmade.

Kunal had gone white about the mouth. "Ila. A promise, with the bond on. You know what a miss would do."

"I know."

"You don't have to—"

"I'd like to," said Ila, and found it was true, which was the most alarming part.

* * *

She told him the rest because it was the same sum she did over any survey, and he deserved to see it written.

"There's a date on it," she said. "I haven't chosen it. The bond ends when meltwater runs at the cairn where we tied it, and that's the Meadow Cairn. South-facing shoulder. Soft weather." She tapped her field book. "I have eleven winters of its thaw dates. They spread wide. A hard year gives me weeks. A soft year gives me fewer, and nobody on this hill can say which one this is."

"And if it runs before we're through?"

"Then the blue light goes out of the cleft, and the path under the Eye closes to us like any lone crosser's. The promise lapses the same hour. Not kept. Only ended, with the words still in my mouth and the oath still on me." She made herself say the last of it. "You'd be standing in the dark at the head of the Eye, with ten dead fingertips, having been given a refusal and a debt I couldn't pay."

It was the first time in her life she had made a promise with a date on it. She sewed the last sheet in. Her hands were steady, and it was a very long way down.

Kunal had not moved. He was looking at his fingers, turning them in the light from the door. The grey had not crept anywhere. It had simply stayed, and the blisters had settled over it like glass. He closed his hand slowly, and it closed, but the last joints went down a beat behind the rest.

"The cleft is stone and ice," he said, to the hand. "I'll need those on it tonight."

"Then we'll rope the hard moves," said Ila. "I'll lead them. You'll hold with the palm."

"Yes." He flexed it again. Then he looked up, and the courtier was nowhere in his face. "Dhanu wanted till dusk to see what Raghu's four did. I'm not waiting for that."

"Kunal—"

"I was going tonight for Raghu. To get ahead of him." He shook his head. "It's your date now. Hard year or soft, I'd rather be through the mountain with a week to spare than be right about the weather. We go tonight, and we go for yours."

It was a choice, not a promise. She heard the difference, and so did the rope, which stayed quiet.

"You didn't have to give me a date," he said, lower.

"It has one whether I name it or not," said Ila.

He laughed, once, without sound. Then he held out his right hand, palm up, the fingers curled away from her, and she put the pattu aside and took it by the palm. It was warm. The dead tips lay against her wrist like cold pebbles, and she held the living part of him and let them lie. The sun came up the cliff and laid a bar of cold gold across the floor.

* * *

Dhanu came at midmorning, limping, with a whistle round his neck and a waxed cloth full of cheese.

He stopped at the door and looked at Kunal, who was at the lip of the scree with Gauri, pointing at the far shore, and then at the rope. It ran from Kunal's wrist back across the stones to the hut, eight spans exactly. The whole length was the width of a courtyard.

"He's at the end of his string," said Dhanu.

"He's at the end of his string," Ila agreed.

"Then he can't hear me, can he, if I speak low."

She understood. She stood, and walked the pattu to the doorframe, and let the rope go taut behind her in a long straight line. Kunal looked round once. She nodded. He turned back to the shore, and the wind took what was left.

Dhanu set the cheese on the sill. He did not sit. The limp had worn the heel of his right boot to a slant.

"My boy says the four on the west shore aren't looking at the lake any more," he said. "They're looking at the cliff. They've seen the cleft's shadow from the shoulder." He rubbed his leg. "It'll be tonight, then, as the prince says."

"Yes."

"And when you're through, and you've seen what he'll show you, and you go back down with your lead and your string." He said it carefully, as a man sets down a full bucket. "What will you do with the map?"

Ila looked at the pattu on its peg. She could feel the sheets in it, like a second spine.

"Draw it true," she said.

It was the answer she had given the survey office, and the archive, and herself in the dark for eleven years. It came easily. It was solid as a surveyor's stone.

Dhanu waited a moment. He was not unkind about it.

"True for who?" he said.

The wind came over the scree. Out on the lake the ice spoke once, a long note, and the whole basin answered it.

She opened her mouth, and the word she wanted was not there. There was only the lined shore, and a small yellow star in a margin, and a woman at a table in Taravan with a key she had never been given.

Ila said nothing. She laid her hand flat on the hem of the pattu, over the stitches and the paper under them, and the ice sang again across the dark water.
