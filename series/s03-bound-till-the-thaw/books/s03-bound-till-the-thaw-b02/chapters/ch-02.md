# Chapter 2: The Council's Price

The runner would not speak until he had counted the silver.

Kunal let him. He stood in the lee of the long barn with the petition tube under his arm and the Fair waking below him, and he counted along in silence as four bits of Neshar silver went into the boy's grubby palm. Thin squares, each with one corner clipped off clean. The boy tilted the last one to the light and checked the clip, the way Dhanu had taught him.

"Well?" said Kunal.

"Dhanu says you're to hear it in council. Not in the yard." The boy shut his fist. "He says you'll want to be sitting."

"Does Dhanu often tell me when to sit?"

"He said you'd ask that." The boy was already backing away. "He said don't answer it."

Then he was gone between the barley roasters, small and quick, and Kunal was left with the drums.

They were up all along the river meadow, a dozen skins beating out of step beneath a white sky that had not yet decided whether to snow. Smoke from the roasting pans lay low on the frost. The Snow Fair smelled as it had when he was six, of scorched grain and wet wool, and for the length of a breath he let himself like it.

Then he shifted the tube under his arm. Inside, on four sheets of the best rag paper, lay the hill council's petition. It asked for self-rule for Neshar under the crown, sealed by every valley, to be set before Queen Sarala at the Shilak Cairn once Shilak's meltwater had run. A year of dinners had gone into those sheets. A year of saying one sentence in forty kitchens. *Nothing in Neshar should be hidden from Neshar.*

He had said it so often that it no longer felt like something he had decided. It felt like weather.

He climbed to the hall.

* * *

The council sat under the brass bell, nine of them at the long table. Madan's drunken floor tipped Old Bhim's chair a little toward the fire. Bhim spoke for the lower terraces, and he sat as though the whole slope sat behind him. Someone had rung the bell to call them in, and its hum was still in Kunal's back teeth.

Ila sat at the foot with her licence flat before her and the crown's packet beside it. She had ink on one cuff and her hair was escaping its pin. She gave him a short nod, as one surveyor to another. He returned it and wondered, not for the first time that morning, whether he had been laughed at on a stair.

"The crown's cover sheet," said Bhim, and slid a loose paper down the table. "Read it, Highness. My eyes are for goats."

Kunal took it. Good paper, the queen's hand at the foot. He read it to the table in the voice he used for petitions, clear and a little warm.

"'To the council of the hill valleys of Neshar, from Sarala, queen of Hemkot. We commission our surveyor Ila, of the Shilak post's family, to draw every way by which a person may cross between Neshar and Hemkot in winter, so that the crown may hold every way.'" His eyes were a phrase ahead of his tongue, and the next words were wrong. He read them anyway. "'And the way beneath the Shilak Cairn.'"

His voice went on through the seal line and the date, and under it the cold went all the way down.

The princes of Neshar had a way. It had no name outside his family's mouths and no line in any book, because his family did not write down what it meant to keep. Now it had a place in a crown commission, in a clause of seven words.

"That clause isn't in my covering note," said Ila.

She said it to the paper, not to him. Her note said *every way*, she added, and nothing after. He watched her face. Either she had not known, or she was the best liar he had ever sat across from. He had spent a year learning that the crown did not write clauses it did not mean, and he did not believe in luck.

"Refuse it," said Bhim.

"Bhim—"

"Refuse the licence. The crown has held these passes sixty-one winters with a rope and a peg. Now it wants them held with ink. Every way, it says. So it can hold every way." The old man's hands lay on the table like two roots. "Let her draw the summer roads. Everyone knows the summer roads."

"Or sell it," said Chetu.

Chetu of Upper Kuhl was round and pink, and he sweated in any weather. "Forgive me, but we're all friends here. Upper Kuhl has forty sacks left and every one wears Pritam's bell. The queen wants a map. Let the queen pay in grain, and let the surveyor name a ridge or two after whoever pays."

"I have named no ridge after anyone who—" Ila stopped. "That's not the question."

"It's my question," said Chetu mildly.

Bhim turned his whole head toward Kunal. "You said it at midsummer, Highness. At this table. A hidden road is a rope round a neck."

He had. He had said it with his palm flat on the wood, and the table had liked it, and it had been a good sentence. Now it had a hook in it, and the hook was in his own mouth.

He saw his position clearly, which was the worst part. To argue for refusal was to argue that a way ought to stay hidden, and he could not say that aloud in this room. Not after a year. And a council that turned away the queen's own surveyor, a few weeks before the queen heard its petition, would arrive at the cairn as a council with something to bury. Anyone who wanted self-rule refused would only have to point.

"I'll speak," said Ila.

She stood. She was shorter than he had remembered and she planted her feet like a woman at a theodolite.

"A map is true or it isn't. If it's true, it serves whoever reads it. The crown will have a copy. I will give the council one, and every valley can read where its own roads run. What anyone does with a true line is not in the line." She looked along the table, level and unhurried. "I can't draw what isn't there, and I won't leave out what is."

It was a clean argument. It was also the sort of thing said by someone who had never had a true line used against her.

*Whoever knows the way owns the people,* thought Kunal, and said nothing, and thought it harder.

At the back wall, Dhanu's runner cleared his throat. He had stood there through all of it, and now at last he spoke.

"Highness. Dhanu says to say it to the table." He drew a breath and said it fast. "Captain Raghu, with the red moustache, who ran from last winter's Fair with a dozen of the regent's men. He's in the high hamlets. A shepherd saw him at Sarn fold. He's going door to door asking for the princes' way."

Nobody spoke. The brass hum had finally died out of the room.

"What princes' way?" said Chetu.

"A story," said Bhim slowly. His eyes were on Kunal. "The old people's."

"Is there a valley in Neshar," said Kunal, "without a story about its lords' secret road?"

It got a thin laugh. It was also not an answer, and he saw Ila write something in her field book. Two courts, then, one bearing a seal and one bearing a red moustache, and both had the same words in their mouths. Somebody had told both. He did not know who, and there was a tower in this yard that held one possibility.

He put the petition tube on the table and made himself speak slowly.

"The council seals the licence," he said, "on one condition. A member of this council is bound to the surveyor for the whole survey. On her rope, at the cairn, every step of the way."

"Who?" said Chetu, already pleased.

"Me."

For a moment he had the satisfaction of watching nine faces fail to find an answer. Then Bhim leaned forward, and the chair tilted him on toward the fire.

"So you can choose what she sees."

"Would you rather Chetu chose?"

"I'd choose a price," Chetu admitted, and two of the council laughed in spite of themselves. Kunal had already done the sum. A bound pair could not lie to each other without paying for it in their hands, but silence was free. He could hold his tongue for a winter. He had held it for a year.

"We vote when the bell rings again," said Bhim heavily. "After the meat. Let the surveyor think about her rope."

* * *

The kitchen passage was the warmest place in Garh Neshar and the only one where nobody wanted anything from him.

It smelled of onions and barley bread. Chuni sat on an upturned crate with her sleeves pushed past her elbows and a pot in her lap, scraping at it with a shell. She was nine. She was the scullery girl, and she had a way of looking at a visiting prince as if he were a large cat that might or might not bite.

Kunal sat down on the flour bin.

"Count with me," he said. "You're slow after ten."

"I'm not slow."

"You're thorough. Hands off the pot." He tapped the stone floor with two fingers, a steady beat. "One for the goat who knows the gate."

"One for the goat who knows the gate," said Chuni, who had a perfect ear.

"Two for the stone that will not wait. Three for the bend where the wind turns back."

"Four for the fold with the broken latch?"

"Four for the fold with the broken latch." He was almost smiling. "Go on. Five."

Nobody who heard it would hear a road. It had no place names in it, only goats and latches and wind. A child could carry it in her head all her life and never know what she carried. Route-keepers died. Songs outlived them, and Kunal had never found a safer way to hand on a road he would not hand to anyone.

He had built it that way, and it had cost him a year of not thinking about what he asked of everyone else.

"Nine and thirty," he sang, low, tapping, "and the ghosts go by—"

A shadow crossed the passage door.

Ila stood there with a roll of paper under one arm and a pencil behind her ear. She had stopped on the threshold the way a person stops when someone else's counting touches their own. She looked at the girl. She looked at the pot. Then she looked at him.

*Nine and thirty. Children's count, for ghosts.* He had given her those very words on the north landing, with his hand beside a cold wall, and now he had sung them in her hearing, to a child, in time to a road. She had a pencil behind her ear. She had walked in on the tune.

His fingers stayed on the stone. They wanted the next beat, and he held them there and let the line die in his mouth.

Neither of them spoke. Ila's eyes dropped, very briefly, to his tapping fingers. Then she gave Chuni a nod, one counter to another, and walked on down the passage. Her footsteps went away at an even pace, two and a half spans each.

"You stopped," said Chuni. "Nine and thirty and what?"

"And nothing," said Kunal. "It's a silly song. Finish your pot."

* * *

The bell rang again, and the hall filled, and the vote was quick.

It was six to three. Bhim voted no and Chetu voted yes, and the other seven sorted themselves between them without much heat. Kunal, who had named the condition and stood inside it, sat out the count. The clerk wrote it down. Then Bhim, with the sourness of a man who had lost a small battle and expected to lose a larger one, turned his chair a hand's breadth toward the foot of the table.

"The council seals your licence, Surveyor, if you take the condition. A member bound to you, at the cairn, for the whole of the survey. His Highness has named himself." His mouth stayed flat. "Will you take him?"

She had been sitting through it with her field book shut and both hands on the cover.

Now she stood. She did not look at Bhim. She looked at Kunal, down the whole length of the table, and he saw nothing in her face that resembled surprise.

"Then you'll walk where I measure, Highness," said Ila.

It was said lightly, and it landed like a weight on a scale. Some of the council laughed. Chetu clapped once. The clerk bent to write.

Kunal sat very still, with the petition tube under his hand. Her licence lay open at her place, and he could read it from here. On the line left for the guide's name, the pencil had already been there. *Kunal of Neshar*, in her cramped small hand, rubbed soft by a day of her sleeve. It had been written before the bell. Before Bhim asked. Before he opened his mouth.

She had wanted him on the rope. He had walked up and put himself there, in front of nine witnesses and the queen's seal, within eight spans of her, where every lie would show in his fingers.

*Whoever knows the way owns the people.* The thought came back with a different face.
