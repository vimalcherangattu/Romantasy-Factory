# Chapter 24: Proof of Life

Kunal was running before he knew it, and the rope took him by the wrist and hauled him round.

"Eight spans," said Ila, behind him, hoarse. "Kunal. I'm coming. Don't pull."

He had pulled. He stood on the shore path with the frozen stones turning under his boots, and the huts were still a hundred paces off, small and dark in their fold below the Drum. No smoke. No goat bell. No Gauri at the door with her sleeves rolled and a rag in her hand, ready to say nothing and hand over bread.

"Walk fast," said Ila. "Don't run. If it's a trap, I'd like to see it before it sees us."

He walked fast, counting the hundred paces by the ache in his teeth. She kept every one at his shoulder, her breath loud, the rope between them swinging low.

The door of the second hut hung from a single leather hinge. It had been split down the middle by something heavy, and the wood was pale and raw at the break. Inside, the blanket lay on the floor. The plank shelf where he and Ila had slept was empty and cold.

"Dhanu," he said. Not loudly. Then, loudly: "Dhanu!"

The lake answered with a low note and nothing else.

Dhanu's own hut stood a little apart, on the better ground. Its door was shut. Kunal went to it with the dead hand out in front of him like a man feeling for a wall in the dark, and then he stopped, because he had seen the snow.

It lay trampled in a wide ring before the sill. Many boots had been through it, and it had frozen again in ridges. In one place the ridges were dark. The stain was the width of a spread hand, the colour of old wine, crusted at the rim. Beside it, a sack lay slit and spilled. Dried apricots had been ground into the ice under heels, orange and brown, flat as dropped coins.

Gauri's apricots. They came to the huts by the pony load every autumn, and she handed them out like favours. He had eaten them off her palm at twelve.

"Don't touch it," said Ila quietly.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were." She did not say it unkindly. She crouched, rope trailing, and held a bare finger near the stain without quite touching it. "Not much. A cut, not a wound. A man could lose this and walk." She looked up. "A woman could."

She was giving him a measure to stand on. He took it, and hated that he needed it.

There was more. Two grooves ran from the doorway through the apricots and away over the snow, side by side and a boot's width apart. Between them, at long intervals, lay the scuff of a heel that had dug in and not held. Kunal followed the grooves to the end of the shore, where the stones gave way to the white of the lake.

They ran straight down to the Drum.

"They didn't carry him," said Ila. "Or her. Not all the way."

"They dragged them to the ice."

"Yes."

"Mini," he said.

He had the door of Dhanu's hut open before she answered. The shelf was bare. He dropped to his knees and looked under it, where a six-year-old fits and a grown man doesn't, and found dust and one knucklebone. He went out to the goat pen and the pony shed, calling her name, not loudly now, in the voice he used with children. He lifted the lid of the meal chest. He put his arm into the hay in the loft until the hay ran out.

Nothing. No small boots. No small voice saying *here, Kunal, you're so slow.*

"Kunal." Ila stood at the pen's far corner, rope taut behind her, pointing past the goat pen. A pony's tracks, older and half filled with blown snow, went away down the valley path and not toward the ice. "Her blanket's gone from the shelf, and her boots. Somebody put her on the pony before the boots came. One small rider."

His breath went out of him. Gauri. Gauri would have heard them on the shore and sent the child down the valley before she thought of anything else.

Then the other thought came up behind it, cold and quick. A pony track, snowed half over. A child alone on it, or a child caught at the first bend. He did not know which. He could not see the lake without wondering, and he could not look away from the valley without wondering that, too.

He looked at the door of Dhanu's hut instead, and that was when he saw the paper.

* * *

It was pinned to the lintel with an iron nail, driven in at an angle, hard. A scrap of pale hide, the kind herders use for tally marks. Under the nail, looped over its head by its thong, hung a small curved thing the length of a finger.

Dhanu's whistle.

Kunal had heard it on four hundred mornings. He knew how it sat in a man's palm and the smell of it, goat and smoke and Dhanu's skin. It was polished dark where a chest had worn it. Two long notes and one short: *come on, it's clear.* Three short: *turn back.* It had hung against Dhanu's breastbone since before Kunal could walk the ridge.

A man did not take that off over his head and leave it. It had been cut or torn away. The thong was frayed at one end.

He took it down with the good hand. He could not work the nail with the other, so Ila did, without a word, and drew out the hide and flattened it against the door with her palm.

The hill script was square, in charcoal. It was not Raghu's hand. It was a clerk's, or a man who wished to look like one.

*The surveyor's sheets, carried by the prince, to the Drum fold by moonrise tomorrow, or the two go under the ice.*

That was all.

He read it three times. By the third reading it had stopped being words and become a shape, and he could see the whole of it at once, like a ridge from the shoulder. *The two.* Not *the lame man.* Not *his wife.* Not *the child.* Whoever wrote it had not wasted ink on names, and they had left the whistle.

Two, and Mini somewhere down a valley path. Either she was out of their hands, and the note was counting right. Or she was in no hand at all, uncounted, and nobody would be looking for her. He could not tell which, and the not telling sat in him like a stone.

"They're alive," he said.

"The note says they are."

"The whistle says it." He closed his hand on the horn. His voice was steady, which surprised him. "Look at it. They took the one thing he'd never give. They can't show me a finger, so they showed me this. If they were dead there'd be nothing to trade, and no note."

"That's an inference."

"It's a good one."

"I didn't say it was bad." Ila stood very straight, her hand still flat on the hide. She was looking at the bottom of the scrap, below the last word, where a clean strip of leather lay bare. Her eyes moved along it and back, as they did along a chain. "Kunal. There's no walnut."

He had known it. He had known it from the first reading, in the part of him that heard the marsh-note under a stone before the foot came down. He had put the knowledge aside as one puts aside a bill.

"No," he said.

"You told me the rule. A walnut with red thread, or the word isn't his." She said it gently, the way she would tell a man his sum was out. "This isn't his word. It was never meant to be. It's theirs."

"I know whose it is."

"Then you know that if Dhanu had time, he'd have sent one. He gave you his walnuts. A man who hands out tokens keeps one back for himself. He'd have used it to say *it's a lie, don't come.* They didn't give him time."

*They didn't give him time.*

She had meant it as a fact, small and dry, to be laid on the table with the others. It went into him like the nail into the door.

He saw it. Dhanu in the doorway with the lamp behind him, the lame foot planted, hearing boots on the shingle. Reaching for the pouch. Not reaching quickly enough.

"No time to send it," said Kunal. "That doesn't make him dead."

"No."

"It makes him alive and taken."

"Yes," said Ila, after a moment. "That's what I said."

It was not what she had said. He let her have it.

* * *

He walked down toward the Drum as far as the rope allowed, which was not far, and she came with him and gave him silence. The grooves ended at the lake's rim. The ice there was bare, wind-scoured and grey, and it took no print past the first yard. Still, the stones at the edge were scuffed, and a strip of blue wool lay caught between two of them. Gauri's. Or a stranger's.

Out on the Drum a long note rolled up from the depths and passed under his feet.

*Where the Drum answers, lie still till morning.*

He stood in the wind with the whistle in his fist and did the sum, because that was what she would have done, and because his own head would not do anything else.

Moonrise tomorrow. A day and a night and part of another. The fold lay at the far end of the lake, under the cliff, and a man could reach it in the time it took to eat. He would be there with her sheets, or without them. If without, they would be in the water.

He could not fight them. Raghu had eight bound pairs in the Hollow Moon, sixteen men, and one of them wrote in a good clerk's hand. He could not send for the council. Bhim would debate it. Dhanu's runner boy was a child, and every other boy was a day's walk off. He had his dead fingertips and a surveyor who had lied to him once, and told him so by dawn.

And he had what she carried. Five sheets, sewn in a lining, with every turn of the hills drawn true. The Stair. The Moon. The way into the Eye, past the weeping stone, down into the dark. He had put it into her hands himself last night, with his palms open. *Then it's yours. All of it.*

Last night he had meant it as the first free thing he had ever done.

Now it was a price.

He thought of it without flinching, which was the horror of it. It came to him whole, neat, and almost easy, as a clean line comes to a draughtsman. They wanted the sheets. He would give them the sheets. The princes' way for Dhanu. The hills' one door for one lame man who had taught him to throw knucklebones and let him lose.

Every argument Dhanu had made at the fire before the Eye stood up in him and went on standing. *A garrison is a house with soldiers in it, and the house goes where the way goes.* It was all true. He would still sell it.

His uncle's voice came back to him over a supper table, mild and reasonable, with a boy of nine on the bench beside him and the funeral meats still on the cloth. *Whoever knows the way owns the people, Kunal. Remember it. Everything else is manners.*

Twenty-four winters he had spent hating that sentence. He had built a council on its opposite. He had stood before the hills and said that no door belonged to one man.

And here he stood, with a whistle in his hand, ready to be the man who owned the way. Not to rule with it. Worse. To spend it. To count it out coin by coin on a frozen shore, as Pritam would have, and call it love.

*I'd rather be wrong that way than right in the way I've been.* He had said that at the fire before the Eye, with his hand on a walnut. He had not known that being wrong might come with a price in blood.

It did not feel like a defeat. That was what frightened him. It felt like a column of figures closing, every line agreeing with every other, and every creed he owned lay behind him on the shingle.

He pulled the thong over his head. The whistle settled against his chest where Dhanu's had sat, and the cold of it went through his shirt like iron.

"Kunal," said Ila.

"I need you to listen to me."

"I've been listening for a quarter hour. You haven't said anything."

"I know." He turned. The rope swung between them. She stood three paces off with her arms wrapped across her coat, her hair crusted white at one temple where the lake had breathed on it. She looked small, and she looked like the person on the whole mountain who understood best what he was about to do. "I'll say it plainly, because you taught me to. They want your sheets, carried by me. They'll let them go if they get them. I believe that. I have to believe it. I'll go to the fold tomorrow at moonrise and give them what they ask."

She did not answer at once. Her eyes dropped to his left hand, hanging at his side, the one hand whose fingertips could still tell on him. She looked at it as she looked at a closing chain.

The tips were pink.

"*I have to believe it,*" said Ila. "Not *I believe it.* Your hands let you say the second one, so it's true as far as it goes. It just doesn't go far." She lifted her eyes. "You don't think the trade holds."

"I think it's the only one there is."

"That's another sentence." A stillness came over her face, the one that came when a closure would not come out. "You'd give them the princes' way."

"I'd give them every turn I know. And every turn you drew." It came out level, and he was proud of that, and ashamed of the pride. "Last night I said it was yours. It is. I'm not taking it. I'm asking for it."

"Asking."

"For one night. For one man." He heard how the words sounded on the cold air. "Ila, I carry these knots in my teeth because I broke my promise to ask you nothing. I know what asking costs. I'm asking anyway. Dhanu told me I'd never given anything I couldn't take back. I'm giving this, and I can't take it back, and it's for him. I'm sorry. I know what it costs the hills. I know what it costs you."

"You don't."

"Then tell me."

"Not yet." She stood with her hand pressed flat to the hem of her pattu, where the letter lay. He had asked once, and paid for it, and not asked again. "Kunal, what happens to the route-keepers on the Stair, and the Moon, after Raghu has the sheets?"

"I don't know."

"You do. You've known since I said it. A man who draws a bearing for a captain isn't a witness he leaves standing. Dhanu's the one who knows where the lines run. If they have the sheets, they don't need Dhanu."

It was a fair argument, and the kind he would have made himself, an hour earlier, from the other side. He had no place to put it.

"If I go with nothing," he said, "they die for certain. If I go with the sheets, there's a chance. A bad one. I've done the odds." He swallowed. "One in three that they walk off that ice. Maybe less. It's all I have to buy."

"One in three isn't what I'd have said."

"What would you have said?"

She did not answer. She looked at the whistle on his chest, then at his face, and her mouth set in the line he had last seen when she measured the pen wall at Ewe's Rest and told Gauri it was a hand short.

The wind turned. Under the ice the Drum spoke again, a slow, deep stroke, and every stone on the shore rang with it. The rope stirred between them. He did not know what she would say. He knew only that every answer she had would be true.

Once, in a cleft, he had asked her to hand him a way. She had said *I drew it, I don't know yet whose it is.* It was the only honest answer he had ever heard about a map.

Now he needed another.

"Where are the sheets?" said Kunal.

Ila opened her mouth.
