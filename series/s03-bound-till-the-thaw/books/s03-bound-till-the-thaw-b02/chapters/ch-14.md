# Chapter 14: Orders

Ila stood still. Behind her the rope went slack and stayed slack, which told her Kunal had stopped dead on her left foot's track, as promised.

The boom rolled away to the shores and came back doubled. Then she heard the other sound under it.

It was a thin, rising crackle, like a long strip of cloth being torn very slowly. It came from ahead and a little left, and it ran. She turned her head to follow it. It slid across the plate in front of them from left to right, and it did not fade the way an echo fades. It crossed the winter line a dozen paces on.

"Pressure line," said Ila.

"What?"

"The ice is pushing against itself. The cold has locked the plate and the wind is working it, so it cracks along a seam." She was whispering. "The boom is the lake letting go. That's what we heard. The crackle is where."

"The lamps run straight through it."

"I know."

The lit line was a good road. It had been laid for the mountain's purposes, which were to keep two bound people off the cliffs and out of the whiteout. The mountain had never promised them dry feet. She tapped the haft against the ice, and it rang clear. She took two steps forward and tapped again, and the note went flat and dull, like a spoon on a sack.

"Stay here," she said, and went on to the end of her track with the rope paying out behind her. Tick. Dull. Tick. Dull. At four paces the dullness lifted, and the note went bright again, and she was a long way past what she'd have called safe. Slush and old fracture ran under there. She could hear it in the wood.

She worked it as she would have worked a river ford. The seam was a ridge of broken ice that had heaved and frozen again. It was thin at its crown, where the lamps crossed it. The plate was thicker to either side, because the heave had stacked the old floes there. A thick edge, then. Somewhere along it the sound would turn clean.

She went left. Forty paces, tapping. The wind tore at her hood. Behind her the rope hummed along the ice as Kunal walked her track, and she never looked round, because she did not need to. He put his boots exactly where she had said. It was a very odd thing to be trusted so entirely.

"Here," said Ila.

The note rang. She tapped again a pace to the right, and a pace further, and each answer came back bright and long, the sound of ice you could build a house on. She laid the haft down flat across the line of the crack, and it spanned the seam with a hand's breadth to spare on either side.

"Narrow," said Kunal.

"A step and a half, and a ridge in the middle. Put your feet where mine go. Don't look down. There's nothing to see, only a bit of grey."

"I'm not looking down."

"You're looking at me."

"Yes."

She went over. The ridge under her boots groaned and settled, and the far side took her weight with a sound like a door closing. She turned, and he was a dark shape in the white, with his hand out for the rope. He crossed in one long stride, his boots exactly in her marks. On the other side he stood a moment with his chest heaving.

"The lamps are over there," he said. "Forty paces."

"We'll go back to them past the seam. We've no use for them where they cross it."

"You tapped the whole way."

"I told you I would."

He laughed. It was short and surprised, and the wind took it. Then the storm came down in earnest, and there was nothing to do but walk.

* * *

They crossed the rest of the Mirror in a white roar, with the cairn-lights guttering blue at their feet. When the far shore came up, it was a darker grey in the grey, a slope of scree with a black bulk above it. Kunal said something she could not hear. He took her elbow and turned her left, and a few hundred paces on a wall stood in the snow, low, square, and roofed with slabs.

"Dhanu's," he shouted.

It was a stone hut with a door of split deodar and a smoke hole in the roof. Inside, it was black and bitterly still, and the silence rang after the wind. Kunal found a flint at the back of a shelf and struck it. The first spark showed her a chulha built of river stones, its mouth scraped clean, and beside it a pile of stunted juniper and dung cakes laid up to the rafters. There was one sleeping shelf, a plank wide enough for two if they were careful, with a folded blanket on it. A line of jars stood above the hearth. She read them by the lamp as it caught: barley meal, salt, dried apricots, a wrapped brick of tea.

"Dhanu stocked it," said Kunal. "He came the short way, with Gauri and the child. He'd want the pot used."

"He's thought of everything."

"He's thought of what he'd want, which is the same." He was already kneeling at the hearth, laying kindling with those scraped, careful hands. He flexed his fingers. The wax was gone from the nails. It was an hour and more since the stones had rung for him.

Ila stood in the door and let the storm go on without her.

The lakes were out there. She could not see them. She could hear the third one, far off, booming through the snow, a deep note that shook the doorframe in her hand. Three frozen tarns. She had measured the first by pacing and two bearings off the Raven Spur, and now she stood in the middle of them, exactly where the letter said.

*Open when you stand at the Glass Lakes. Not before. S.*

Her hand had gone to her ribs without asking. The paper was a flat dry square against her side, its edges plain through the wool. She had carried it for a week. She knew every thread of the stitching that held it.

It was a queen's letter, and the queen had named this place. She had not named it to Kunal, or to the council, or in the commission Ila had signed. She had named it only to her, in a fold of paper, with a thread of blood in the wax.

Open it. She was standing at the Glass Lakes. The condition had been met.

And she knew, with a clean cold certainty that she did not like, that the first thing the letter would do was tell her what she had been sent to find. Then she could not un-know it. Then it would be a line on a sheet before her pencil moved.

Kunal had walked her down the Stair and the Ladder and the Hollow Moon, one way at a time. He had given her names and bearings and a staircase of ice she had not drawn. He had said *it matters more than anything* with his fingertips going white. Where did his road go next? She wanted to see it. She wanted to walk it with her own eyes before the queen's hand came between them.

It was not a good reason, and she knew it. She shut the door on the wind.

"Soup," said Kunal. "If you'll hold the pot."

* * *

They ate in the red light of the chulha, sitting on the floor with their backs against the shelf. The barley was thin and salty, and she thought it the best thing she had ever put in her mouth. Kunal had spread the wet wool to steam. His hair was dark with snow-melt, and he had a smear of ash on his cheekbone that she did not tell him about.

He gave her the night's name first. That was the order of it. He gave it with his eyes on the fire.

"The seam you stopped for. The families call it the Sleeper's Spine. When it cracks, they say the sleeper's turning over. They don't cross it with a laden mule." He glanced at her. "You'd have known it for what it was. Nobody told me you read ice."

"Nobody told me you'd walk on it with your eyes shut."

"I had them open."

"You said you weren't looking down."

"I wasn't." His mouth moved. "I'd have told you if I was frightened. I'm not so good at lying. I've learned that this week."

She wrote it down. *Sleeper's Spine.* The pencil lead was one of his, still warm from his coat, and the line came out clear and black. Her hand was steady and pink in the firelight, and she looked at it for a moment, and then at the page.

"Your question," said Ila.

It was the rule. One name from him, one answer from her, for anything she knew, or a refusal that cost nothing. She had never once refused a night question. Nothing he had asked had been worth the price of the silence.

He turned the bowl in his hands, eyes on the fire. She saw him decide.

"Do your orders name these lakes?"

It came very evenly, and she heard everything under it.

She had known he would ask, had known it since the Ladder, if she was honest, and in the cave before that. It was the question his whole winter had been walking toward, and it was a fair one. He had given her the Glass Lakes freely. She had done nothing to earn it except leave a staircase off her sheet. He wanted to know what he had bought.

The letter named them. *Open when you stand at the Glass Lakes.* The words were in a woman's hand, folded against her ribs. She had only to say so.

And he would stop.

She could see it clearly, as she might have seen a road in a survey. She would say *yes*, and his face would close. He would be courteous. He would be so courteous. He had said he'd not ask her to carry his people's ways at the cost of her own, and he meant it, and that was why he would turn them round. He would take her back across the Mirror to Ewe's Rest in the morning, and the lakes would be a sound she'd heard once through a storm. And the letter would stay sealed in her coat, a thing she carried and could not read.

She tried the narrow truth first, because it was hers to try. "My commission names every—"

"Not your commission." He said it gently, and looked up. "Anything you carry."

So he had read her. A week of her careful sums had taught him where she kept the small exact true things, and he had shut that door before she reached it. That left a refusal, and a refusal from her on this was an answer. He would read it, because he was Kunal.

That left one word. Her hands would show it before he did, she knew that. She weighed it, quick and cold. Pattu sleeves, a bowl to hold, the fire at her back. She could keep them hidden for an hour, and an hour was all the lie would need.

"No," said Ila.

It came out level. It was a small word. She had said it a hundred times in her life, to clerks and cooks and men who wanted her pencil.

For a moment nothing happened at all.

Kunal's shoulders came down. She watched them do it. He let out a long breath through his teeth and shut his eyes, and he laughed, softly, as at a joke on himself.

"Good," he said. "I'd hoped."

"You'd hoped?"

"All winter. I'd stopped believing it." He opened his eyes, bright and a little wet in the fire. "I thought, however I led you here, I'd be leading you with a knife at my back. Your orders were the first thing I asked you. They're the last thing I've needed to hear. And you said no." He shook his head. "It's very small to be so large."

She said, "Yes."

That was true. It was the only true thing she had said in a minute, and she held to it.

He reached over and took the empty bowl from her lap, gently, as he'd have taken a dropped pencil, and set it by the hearth. His fingers brushed her wrist. They were warm. She wanted to say something, and her mouth would not make the shape.

Then the cold came.

* * *

The cold began in her fingertips, as it always began. She had read about it. In the hall at Garh Neshar, Meera, who had lost two fingers to one lie, had told it flatly: the hands first, then the feet and ears. Ila had thought she understood. She had measured it as a quantity, a size of lie against a size of loss.

Under the nails it came like a needle drawn through, and then like a hundred. Her right hand rested on her knee, and it looked the same. She flexed it, and it did not feel like hers.

This was not the cold of the hut. The chulha was roaring now, and the stones were heating, and her cheeks stung with warmth. This cold sat inside her fingers like a thing set there. It ate its way up from the tips. Her ears began to burn, and then to prickle, and then, worse, to go quiet, and she put a hand up and found the rim of one numb as wax.

*Middling*, she thought. It was a clerk's word. A middling lie. Frostnip, and a night of it. She had seen her own face in the rule and had not liked the mirror.

She shifted closer to the fire. It did nothing. She held both hands out to the coals until her knuckles ached with the heat, and the cold stayed, bright and exact, under the skin. A shudder took her at the shoulders. She clenched her teeth to hold it, and it went down her back instead.

"Ila."

"I'm all right."

"You're shaking."

"It's the storm. I've been wet since the scree." Her teeth clicked on the words. "I'm cold, Kunal. That's all. The fire hasn't got into the wool yet."

The cold answered before he did. It slid up her fingers like water up a wick, past the first knuckle, and stopped there with a hard small ache. She had paid for that, and she knew exactly what coin. She had bought nothing with it. She had only told another lie on top of the first and made it larger.

*A map is true or false*, she had told her father and the clerks at nineteen and at thirty, and once, across a fire, him. Here was a line she had put on a sheet. It was not a mistake. She had done it with her eyes open, and she could not rub it out.

She pulled the pattu round her shoulders and held it shut with both fists. Her knuckles had gone from pink to a dead cream. She tucked the fists in against her ribs, where the letter lay, and bent over them.

Kunal had gone very quiet.

He sat with the iron pot in his hands, still half full, the last of the soup he'd put by for the morning. He had been about to set it on the hearth. Now he did not move. She heard the fire ticking in the dung cakes, and the third lake booming far away, and her own breath, ragged and loud.

"Ila," he said again.

"It's nothing."

The white climbed again, a slow cold step, to the second knuckle. She bit down on a sound and did not let it out.

"Show me your hands."

"I said—"

"You said you were cold." He said it gently. "Then it'll cost you nothing to show me."

She could have refused. It was free. She knew what the refusal would say, and she was too tired and too shaken to build the sentence that would make it say something else. Slowly she uncurled her fists and held them out in the firelight, palms down, as she had held them out above the fire at the foot of the Ladder.

Her hands were white. The white went past the tips and past the first knuckle, up to the second, a clean hard line like the edge of a map, and below the line her skin was livid. The fire lay along it and did nothing at all. She watched his face as he looked. It did not change, and that was the worst of it. He looked the way a man looks at an old, true thing he has been reading for some time and has only now seen the date on.

He set down the pot.
