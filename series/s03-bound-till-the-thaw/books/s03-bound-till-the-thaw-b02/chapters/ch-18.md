# Chapter 18: The Eye

The ice on the Eye rang clear, and Ila trusted it about as far as she could throw it.

She tapped her axe haft along the shore for a hundred paces, then knelt and chipped a sounding hole with the survey spike and Kunal's knife. Fourteen fingers. Fifty paces on, sixteen. Near the middle, where the lake was deepest, the ice ran so clear that her hole went down like a blue throat.

"Well?" said Kunal.

"It'll carry us. It won't carry us kindly."

Frost smoked off the lake in the low sun, and the morning was so still that she could hear her own pulse. The third lake lay long and dark between steep walls. The Drum was behind them, and the Mirror behind that. At the head of it, where the ice met a black cliff, was the cleft.

It was a slot twice a man's height. A curtain of blue ice hung from its lintel, half drawn, like a lid over an eye. Somewhere deep inside, water ticked on stone, slow and patient, like a clock in another house.

She stopped fifty paces short and let the rope go slack.

Low in the dark, a long way in, there was light. It was faint and blue, and steady as a lamp left burning in a far room. She had seen it on the Stair and on the Hollow Moon, strung out along the winter line. Cairn-light. It never flickered. The mountain had put it there to be seen.

"You see it," she said.

"Yes." Kunal's voice came from behind her shoulder. He had stopped a pace short of where she stood. "I was twelve when he brought me here. There was no light. There was a hole in a cliff and my uncle's hand on the back of my neck."

"You weren't bound."

"No."

"So it shows itself to the bound." She said it flatly, to the ice. "The mountain marks it. It wants us to find it."

Kunal said nothing. She looked round at last. He stood with his arms away from his body and his hands open, the way he had stood in the doorway of the hut. His feet had not moved since the last sounding. She knew that stance. It was a boy's, when a hand had been laid on the nape of his neck.

He had walked her to the one place in the world he was afraid of, on a morning when he had every reason to think she would draw it and sell it.

Ila sat down on a flat tongue of rock at the shore and took out her field book.

She drew the lakes first, because they were the part she knew. The Mirror, the Drum and the Eye lay one below the other like three beads on a string, and every number she had paid for in holes went down in her small hard figures. Then she took her bearings on the cleft from two points along the shore. She paced the ice to the lip. Eighty-eight paces. She wrote *88* and underlined it.

Then she said, without turning, "I'm drawing the mouth."

The wind had no voice. Kunal's boots shifted on the snow.

"Where it is, how wide, how high, and the light inside. All of it, to the pace. I'm telling you because the book goes where my commission sends it, unless I decide otherwise. I haven't decided. I won't promise you that I will."

"I know."

"I can draw it less true." She made herself turn and look at him, full in the face. "Move it a hundred paces. Leave off the light. People do it. Maps lie by being kind." Her pencil pointed at the slot. "Somebody will come here in a storm with a sheet in his hand, and he'll believe it. A sheet that's wrong is a grave with a tidy lid."

He thought it through while she watched. When he spoke it was slowly, like a man reading a bill aloud.

"If you draw it true, the crown has it by spring. An engineer will lay your sheet on a table and put his thumb on the eighty-eight paces. Twenty men on that shelf. A fire on this tongue, where we're sitting. Sentries along the shore, so nothing goes in or out that the crown hasn't counted." His hands stayed open, though it cost him. "My uncle taught me that a line is an owner. I know exactly what you'd be handing them."

"Then say it. Tell me to move it."

Kunal looked at the cleft for a long moment. Then he looked at her.

"Draw it true," he said.

It came out rough. He cleared his throat and said it again, quieter. "Draw it true, Ila. I'd rather a hundred men knew where it is than one went in the wrong place."

She said no thanks, because it was not a gift. She turned back and wrote the bearing down, and her hand was steadier than her breath.

* * *

To take the width of the mouth, she had to go in.

From the shore the slot looked shoulder-wide. It was not. The blue curtain had grown down over the rock for years, and the true walls were behind it, out of sight and out of reach of any sight bar. She could chain only what she could touch.

She sounded the first pace of the shelf under the curtain, and the haft rang clean. She sounded the second and it rang clean. She stood with the rope slack behind her and weighed it, as she weighed everything.

"One in eight," said Ila.

"One in eight what?"

"That it's hollow under there. I can hear meltwater, and meltwater undercuts." She tugged the rope twice. "Three paces. I take the width off the rock itself and come out."

"One in eight is a great deal of mountain."

"It was one in five on the Hollow Moon and you didn't say a word."

"I said a word. You didn't hear it." He moved up behind her and took the rope in both hands. The wrappings he had made, torn from his shirt, were dark where the blisters had broken. "If you go through, I'll hold you."

"Hold. Don't haul." She looked at him over her shoulder. "If I drop, don't pull until I say. A rope dragged over a lip cuts. Let me climb."

"You want me to stand there while you hang."

"I want you to stand there while I climb."

He looked at her a long moment. Then he set his heels in the snow. "Go."

Under the blue curtain, the cold closed round her like a wet cloth. The ticking was suddenly louder, and the cairn-light lay ahead, calm and low. One pace. Two. Her haft rang, then rang again. She laid her palm on the left wall, wet and glassy, and stretched the chain to its end.

On the third pace the haft gave a short dull note, like a spoon on a pillow. She knew what it meant, and her weight had already gone onto the foot.

The shelf went with a sound like a dropped plate. She dropped through to the armpits, and her chin cracked on the edge. The rope snapped taut across her ribs and wrenched the knot on her wrist. Under her boots there was nothing at all, only a cold breath coming up from far below, smelling of stone and water.

The survey spike slid out of her belt. She heard it fall. One, two, three. Then a small bright sound, very far away.

"Hold," said Ila.

"Holding."

His voice was ten paces behind her and perfectly level. The rope did not move. It might have been tied to the cliff.

She swung her axe with her right arm and sank the pick into the ice wall at the back of the mouth. It bit. She tried it with her weight, and it held. She kicked, found a crack with the toe of her boot, and pushed.

"Don't pull."

"I'm not."

She could hear what it cost him. His breath came harsh through his nose. Every muscle in him must have been screaming to haul her out like a sack. He did not. He stood in the snow and let her climb on her own axe, a hand's breadth at a time, as she had asked.

Then she was over the lip on her belly, lying with her cheek against the ice and her heart going like a mallet.

"Ila."

"I'm out." Her voice had a crack in it. "I'm out. Let go."

The rope went slack. She crawled the last three paces on her knees and her good hand, and sat down in the snow. Then she looked round.

He was on one knee, with the rope wound twice round his hip and still locked in both fists. The wrappings had come off. Red had run into the wool.

* * *

They sat on the stone tongue out of the wind, with the sun on their backs and the Eye shining below them. She had the chain in her lap and the field book safe in her coat. She had the left wall, from the lip to the centre, and nothing of the right. The spike was gone. Without it the chain had no anchor, and the figure would stay half a figure until she found another way to hold it.

"Give me your hands," said Ila.

The words went through her like a draught. She had said them on the shelf above the Drum.

He gave them. She laid them across her knees, palms up.

They were worse than she had thought. The fingertips were swollen and shining, with blisters at each pad. Four had burst on the rope and wept pink into the creases. She had known what the cold had cost him. She had not looked at it this closely, and she made herself look now.

*Because of me,* she thought. Then she put the thought down, because it did not help, and reached into her coat.

The jar was small and brown, with a cork bound on by a thong. He frowned at it.

"That's Dhanu's. From the hut."

"The second morning." She worked the cork out with her teeth. "It was on the shelf by the barley. I took it and said nothing."

"You wouldn't look at me for two days."

"I was looking at your hands." She warmed a thumbnail of the fat in her fist until it softened. "I was also not looking at you. Both are true."

He made a short sound. She kept her eyes down. She cleaned the broken blisters first, with snow-melt from the flask. He flinched, once, and held still. Then she smoothed the fat over each pad in turn, as lightly as she could, and wrapped the fingers one by one in strips torn from her own shirt hem. She was good with small, exact work.

"It's not cold you can warm," she said. "Fire doesn't touch it. But the skin heals."

"You've been carrying it for two days."

"I've been carrying a good many things for two days." That was true, and she heard it as she said it. She kept her eyes on the knot she was tying. "Does it hurt less?"

"It does." He turned his hand over in hers, very slightly. "That's the first time anyone has dressed my hands since I was nine."

She had no answer for that, so she talked, and reached for the safest ground she had.

"Rohan sends me numbers," said Ila.

"Numbers."

"Every summer. A packet from the Shilak post, tied with the post's grey cord. Snow depth at the door by the week. Thaw dates at the cairn. How many crossers turned back, how many sacks the wagons carried in the autumn." She pulled the last knot snug. "Eleven winters of columns. There's never a word of his own. Once, in a margin, he wrote *ice late*."

"That's all?"

"They're love letters." She heard how it sounded and let it stand. "That's what he can do. He sends me his sums, and I send him mine, and neither of us has to say a thing. I read them when I'm homesick."

Kunal began to laugh.

It came out of him by surprise, and it was not the courtier's laugh she had learned in the first weeks. It was lower and rougher, and it shook his shoulders, so that she had to hold his bandaged hand still. "The most feared captain in Hemkot," he said, "sends you his thaw dates."

"With the sacks."

"Tied in grey cord."

"He'll be insufferable once he's married. Meera counts things aloud." She was laughing too, the loud sudden one, and two ravens went up off the scree. The sound rang off the cliff and came back to her from the cleft, thin and strange, and she stopped.

For a long time she looked at the mouth. It was no longer a place she was afraid of. It was a place she had drawn, which was worse.

She opened the field book on her knee and laid her rule against the *88*. She meant only to finish the line, the way she finished every line, from the ice to the lip. Her pencil ran along the rule, out across the shore, and onto the flat tongue of rock where they sat.

It stopped there.

*Twenty men on the shelf,* he had said. *A fire on this tongue.* She could see it as clearly as the sheet. A clerk at Taravan with the bearings in a drawer. An engineer's thumb on the paper. A watch-fire where her own boots were, and a chain of sentries drawn along the shore as neat as stitching. Every figure would be accurate. Every line would be hers.

Ila lifted the pencil off the page and held it away from her, as if it might go off.

Where she would aim it, she did not know. Only that the choice had stopped being someone else's.

The book went deep into her coat, beside the letter and flat against her ribs. Kunal asked nothing, and she was grateful, and she hated that she was.

"I'll draw the right wall from the shore, and mark it as not measured," she said. "I won't put a number on what I haven't touched."

"I should hope not."

"You held."

"You asked me to." He lifted the wrapped hand a little and looked at it. "I find I'm bad at it. Not pulling."

"You were very good at it."

He smiled at her, plainly, without having first checked it, and she had to look away over the lake.

It was then, past the pale curve of the Eye, that she saw the smoke.

It stood up from the far end of the chain, a grey thread against the rock, where the lower huts hid in the fold of the shore. It rose straight and thin. Someone was burning dung and juniper, and had been for an hour.

Kunal had seen it too. She felt it in the rope.

"That's the lower hut," he said. "Nobody's lived there since autumn."

"Raghu's men?"

"Raghu's men burn pitch. They'd have no reason to hide the smell." His eyes narrowed, and the tension went out of his shoulders. "That's a chulha that draws well. That's Dhanu's. Dhanu and Gauri have come."

She stood up. The sun was bright on the ice.

Dhanu would not have climbed this far up in the snow for nothing. Whatever he carried, it was news. She turned toward the smoke and took the first step down the shore.
