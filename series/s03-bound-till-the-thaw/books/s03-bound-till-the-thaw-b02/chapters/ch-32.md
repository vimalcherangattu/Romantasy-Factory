# Chapter 32: The Winter Oath

"Not alone," Ila had told the queen at the door of the little room. "I promised two people they would hear it first. I won't read it to one and tell the others after."

Sarala had looked at her a long moment, with the lamp low and the stick across her knees. Then she had said, "At the cairn, then. At dusk tomorrow. We would not read a thing like that under a roof."

So the next dusk found Ila at the Shilak Cairn an hour early, with the rubbing in her coat and her crutch sunk in the wet snow, and Kunal beside her.

The ring was quiet. Three weeks of thaw had left the ground below it black and running, and the stones stood in their chest-high circle with water ticking off their shoulders. A line of grey wool hung forgotten on the nearest slab. Someone had tied Badami's big bell to the east side. It rang once, in the wind, and was still.

"I left the rope in the salt house," said Ila.

"I saw."

"Eight spans of Neshar's best wool, and I put it in a chest." She tried to laugh and it came out thin. "I've never been this frightened of a plain ring of stones."

"You've crossed the Eye."

"The Eye told me what it wanted. This doesn't."

He turned to face her. The light was going the colour of slate, with a bar of rose along the Saddle's lip. His right hand hung at his side, its tips grey. She had stopped looking at it as a wound some time ago and looked at it now only as his.

"There's nothing to hold us to it," he said. "You understand that. The bond's over. Nobody's watching the cold. If we say it here, it's only us saying it, and if either of us breaks it, nothing happens at all."

"I know."

"I want to say it anyway."

"So do I." She stepped into the ring. The crutch left a hole in the snow. "You first. You've the worse habit."

He did not smile. He put his left palm flat on the cairn and said it the way he said hill names, plainly, as if the thing were already so.

"I, Kunal of Neshar, will keep no way from you. Every route I know, and every one I learn, I'll put in your hand before I put it in my own head."

She put her hand over his, on the stone. It was cold and slick and entirely ordinary.

"I, Ila, will keep no line from you. I won't draw a thing of yours without your name beside it. And I won't tell you a number I don't believe, not to spare you, not to spare myself."

"Together," he said. "The last."

"Every winter," said Ila. "We walk it together."

"Every winter," said Kunal.

Nothing happened.

She had known nothing would. She listened anyway, with her whole body, for the hum, for the low even note that had lain under her wrist for thirty-five days. She would have taken the ringing of a lie, even. There was only the drip of meltwater and the far-off bell, and a boy somewhere shouting at a goat.

The stones had not heard them, or had heard and did not care. Either way the promise lay in the air between them, with no mountain behind it. It was only theirs.

She found that she was crying a little, and was not ashamed of it.

"It didn't hum," she said.

"No."

"I wanted it to."

"I know." He had a hand inside his coat. "I wanted the stones to say it was true. Then I thought, I've never once believed a thing because a stone said so. Not until this winter." He drew out a walnut. A thread of red wool was wound round it, wrapped twice and knotted. "Gauri gave me two of these. One was for Mini's string. She said you should have the other."

"Why?"

"She said you were somebody's runner now, and should wear the token."

He tried to tie it to her surveyor's chain. His right fingers could not pinch the thread, and she watched him lower his head and take the end in his teeth, and work the knot with his teeth and the heel of his hand. It took him a long time. She stood still for all of it. The chain was the short brass one at her belt that had measured every pass she had ever drawn, and when he was done the walnut hung from its last link, a small brown thing with a bright red knot.

She closed her hand round it.

"It'll knock against my rule," she said.

"Then I'll know when you're coming."

* * *

The queen came up the path at full dusk, on foot, with Gopal on one side and Meera on the other. Rohan walked behind with his hands clasped at his back. Sarala refused the stick for the last twenty paces and Gopal looked at the ground.

Kiran came last.

Ila had sent for him the day the council crossed. He had come over the Saddle with the hill pairs, Roop's crew, a long thin shape in a blanket coat with his torn ear showing under the cap. He had said little since. At the cairn's edge he stopped outside the stones, and she saw him look at the ring the way he looked at a rope he had no wish to be tied to.

"Inside," Ila said. "You, too. It's yours as much as anyone's."

He stepped in.

Bindu was already there, with a lantern. Ila had asked for her. She stood with her hands in her sleeves and her eyes on the ground, a woman who was loud with rope and quiet with everyone else, and she looked up only when Ila spread the paper on the flat slab at the cairn's heart.

It was a rubbing of a stone she had touched for an hour in the dark. The graphite had gone soft in the damp. Kunal laid his lantern beside it, and Ila put her finger under the top line.

"Here," she said. "The old script. I can't read it. He can."

"Not all of it." Kunal bent. The light caught his face. "The hill hands learn it in the cradle, for the songs. It's close to what the grandmothers still use. Carried." He followed the strokes. "Carried till no." His finger stopped. "And then the stone's worn and the lead tore. There's a stroke and a loop. A knot sign, I think. But I don't know it."

"Let me," said Bindu.

She said it almost too low to hear. Kunal moved his hand.

Bindu leaned over the paper. Her lips moved. After a moment, under her breath, she began to sing a thing Ila had heard her hum in the top room while she wound the red rope. A knot-count. *Two over, one under, and loose it at the end.* She stopped at the last line.

"It's crew work," she said. "The loop and the hook. When you finish a pattern, there's a sign for the day you let it go. The thaw. We say it when the cold gives and the knot comes loose." She touched the paper. "This is the knot-sign. It's *thaw*."

"Carried till no thaw," said Kunal.

The wind moved the lantern flame. Nobody spoke.

"It's the line on the door," said the queen. She had not drawn nearer. She stood outside the ring of lantern light, small and straight, and her voice was careful. "The line the archive holds. We have it in a different hand, from the first chronicle of the Shilak post. *Carried.*"

"What does the archive say it means?" Ila said.

"That a death is owed." Sarala said it as an old woman reads a bill. "Each time the pass calls, a body is carried off. Carried out of the world. The Bargain was struck so that the mountain would let the living cross, and the mountain took one in return. One for each calling. That is what my mother's mother left me in the drawer, and what her mother left her." She breathed. "Sixty winters ago the pass called, and it was a warden of the Shilak post who answered. His name was Harish."

Rohan made no sound. Ila did not turn to look at him.

"Our grandfather," she said.

"Yes."

"Say it, Majesty," said Meera. "Say what the post's chronicle says about his dying. For the count."

The queen did, with one hand on the stone. "He was the last. Harish of the Shilak post, who went into the snow at the pass's call, and was carried down at the thaw. The Closing Law was written the next spring, that none should be asked again."

"And the hills say it differently," said Ila.

She had not planned to speak first. It came out of her as the tally came out of her on a sheet, flat and exact. She felt every face in the ring turn.

"On the Widow's Ladder," she said. "There's a song. *The widow was carried down.* In every hamlet from the Kuhl to the Eye. Dhanu's wife told me what it means there. It means she was seen across. By hand, by someone's hand, over the bad ground." Her voice shook and she let it. "The chronicle uses the word for the dead. The hills use it for the living. It's the same word, and the same stone. I think the Bargain was written in the hills' sense, and the crown has read it in its own for sixty winters."

"You think," said the queen.

"I can't prove it." She made herself hold the old woman's eyes. "I measured the Under-Cairn's door. I read the marks. A death is owed, or a crossing is. Both fit the line, and neither fits it better. There's nothing on the stone to choose between them."

"Then we have two readings and no judge," said Sarala.

"Yes."

"We have had that situation," said the queen, with some bitterness, "for sixty winters."

Meera had her count open on her forearm. She wrote something, blotted it, and said without looking up, "I'm putting down both."

Kunal had gone very still beside Ila. She felt the shape of his thought and said nothing. If the hills' sense was true, nobody had ever had to die. If the crown's was true, someone would, at the next calling, and the stone could not tell them which.

"Majesty," said Rohan. It was the first word he had spoken. "You called for the thing to be found. It's found. What now?"

"Now we do nothing we cannot take back." The queen's gaze went across the ring. "The Under-Cairn is sealed from today. Wardens at the gap and at the crawl's mouth, day and night, until the fourth winter has passed. Nobody goes down. Nobody goes in to pay or to look. Not a prince, not a surveyor, not a captain." She did not say *not a boy who wants to die.* She looked at Kiran for one breath and looked away. "When the pass calls, we will have read it correctly, or we will have read it wrongly, and we would rather find out with the door shut."

"The door will not stay shut," said Bindu, to the rubbing, and went red to the ears.

Nobody answered her.

Ila turned her head.

Kiran stood at the edge of the lantern light with the paper in front of him. He had not moved while the queen spoke. He was staring at the last word, the knot-sign, as a man stares down a well. His lips were slightly open. His good ear had gone white at the rim, from the cold or something else.

She had known him all winter as the man who joked. Who sang rude songs to the chain and called her *boss* and teased Kunal about his hands. She had never seen his face with the joke gone out of it.

"Kiran," she said.

He did not answer. His eyes did not leave the page. After a time he put out one finger, carefully, as if the graphite might burn, and touched the loop that Bindu had read as *thaw*. He took the finger back.

He said nothing. Not one word.

Meera made a small movement toward him, and stopped, and Ila saw her hold herself still the way a guide holds a rope that has not yet gone taut. She wrote nothing in the count.

* * *

The rider came at full dark.

They heard him before they saw him: a horse blown to staggering on the lower path, and a man's voice shouting a warden's name. Gopal went down at a run with a lantern. The queen did not move.

He was a hill-council guard in undyed wool with a blue cord on the shoulder, and he was grey to the eyes with road dust and snow. He had to be held up between two wardens. When he was brought into the ring he looked at the queen, then at Kunal, and said to Kunal, because he had been sent to say it to him:

"Highness. The regent is out."

"Pritam is in the tower," said Kunal. His voice was quite level.

"He was in it three nights ago. The cell door was barred and the guard was at it. The guard was at it all night, and in the morning the cell was empty." The man swallowed. "There's a stair, Highness. Inside the north wall. A turn nobody knew was there, behind the plaster, between the landing and the lower room. The wall sweats on that side, and the old women said so for years. He went down it into the kitchen passage. He had a key to the postern. Somebody gave him a key."

Ila heard her own voice before she decided to use it. "Two spans."

"What?" said the queen.

"The north wall. It's two spans out of true. I wrote it in my field book the first month, from the yard. The room is a full pace short of the outside." She felt the cold go through her, from the crown of her head down. "I told the guest-house keeper. I told my brother, in a letter. I recorded it as sloppy plaster. And there's a half-step on the landing that nobody counts." She turned to Kunal. "It was forty steps. I told you forty."

"You told me forty," said Kunal. He was not looking at her. He was looking at the black Saddle. "My family counts thirty-nine for the ghosts. The ghost was the stair."

"The void," said Ila. "I measured the void. I put it on the sheet as a room's thickness, and it was a stair, and I never went to find out. Because I thought a map was true or false and what a wall hid was not my business."

"Ila," said Rohan.

"I'm all right." She was not, quite. She drew breath. "Where did he go?"

The rider had been waiting for it. "East, Highness. Over the Garh road and then off it. Six men with him, perhaps more, and a pony string. The tracks are two days old and the thaw's been at them. But they don't take the Kuhl cut or the lower road. They go east, up the dry side, toward a way nobody's drawn."

The ring was silent.

*East.* Ila stood very still with her hand on the walnut. She was back on the scree on the first night, with the pale blue line burning along the east wall, broken at the fourth light, a valley that was not on her survey sheets. A way no stream, no path, no shepherd had pointed her to. She had left the bearing out of her field book, and had then forgotten that she had.

He had been going to it all along. Or something had been calling him there.

"Majesty," said Gopal quietly. "Do we go after him?"

"With what?" said the queen. "Wardens do not fight over closed ground. The passes are free this week, and he walks them as free as you." She turned toward the dark that the rider had come out of. "Four winters. The fourth is the one that matters, and Pritam knows it as well as the archive does. He did not escape for a crown. A crown he could have bought."

Nobody asked what he had escaped for.

Ila took the field copy out of her coat.

It was the pencil sheet she had kept for herself, rolled in a tube with her rule, the one the crown had never been shown. She spread it on the cairn slab beside the rubbing. Her hand was steady. She found the east wall of the Hollow Moon, and the blank above the Kuhl cut where the valley should have been. She laid her rule down and drew a line across the white, dotted, short strokes with spaces between them, the way a surveyor marks a way she has not walked.

At its end, in her small hard hand, she wrote: *unknown way. Pritam.*

Kunal stood close. He did not say anything while she wrote. He watched her finish the last letter. He put his hand flat on the table, left and right, the dead tips beside her pencil.

"Then we'll find it," he said.

It was not a question. It was the thing she had wanted, and she heard it settle in her the way the walnut hung from her chain, small and light and entirely real.

"Winter," she said. "When it closes."

"Every winter," said Kunal. "Together."

Behind them, in the dark of the ring, the bell on the cairn rang once in the wind, and the water ran on.

* * *

She found the salt house yard full of mules at first light.

She had come down the stair with her crutch and the field sheet in her coat, looking for tea, and stopped on the last step. The big door stood open. Outside in the yard a string of mules was being unloaded by a dozen drovers in Taravan blue, and what they carried came off in bales the colour of old brick: wool, undyed and madder-red, and the lanolin smell of it filled the room to the rafters.

A tall woman in a travelling cloak stood in the doorway with a ledger under her arm. She was perhaps forty, with a dry, handsome face, and she was surveying the goat pen as though she meant to price it.

"Early," said Meera, from the table. She had her left hand in its glove around a mug, and an expression Ila had learned meant she was trying not to laugh. "It's thaw. Nobody sends a convoy at thaw. You'd have to wait six months to cross."

"I know it." The woman did not turn. "I know the Winter Crossing Law better than the man who wrote it. First licence on the council's list goes to the first guide with a pair at the cairn, and I mean to be at the cairn before the snow's off the road. I've been waiting forty years to be early for something."

"Lata," said Meera. "This is Ila. Rohan's sister."

"The surveyor." Lata's glance took in the crutch, the chain, the red-knotted walnut. "You're the one with the map. They're singing it in the Taravan taverns, and your name's in the last verse, spelled wrong."

"How wrong?"

"Eleven." Lata's mouth moved. Then it stopped, and her face changed, and she said to Meera, much more quietly, "I've been two days in Ranu. On the way. I wanted to see the walnut slope. I wanted to see where my husband's ledgers ended." She shifted the book on her arm. "They tell a story there. About the torches. About a man who rode up in a slate cloak and told the soldiers to lower them. Grey-bearded. Old soldier's seat on a horse. Nobody will say who he was. I asked the healer. She said, *not mine to spend.*"

Ila felt the stair rail under her palm. She did not breathe.

Meera set the mug down. She said, evenly, "I never learned his name either. I saw him once, at the Fair. He laid a sword belt on the grass."

"A sword belt," said Lata.

"And walked away from it."

"Who was he, Meera?"

"I don't know." It was the truth, and nothing happened to her hand. "But Ranu does. And Ranu is keeping him."

Lata looked at the wool and at the mules and at the white road beyond the gate. She said, to no one, in her warm, tart voice, "Well. That's the first account I've ever been given that doesn't balance."

She went back out into the yard to count her bales, and Ila stood on the stair a long time, with the walnut in her hand, thinking of a dotted line in the east and a name that no one would say.
