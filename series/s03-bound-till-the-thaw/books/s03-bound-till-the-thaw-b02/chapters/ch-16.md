# Chapter 16: The Grave Map

She did not unfold it.

The seal lay in two halves in her palm, red wax with its thread of darker red. The paper sat in her other hand, still folded in three. Ila looked down the length of the Drum at her own small black holes. Eleven fingers. Nine. Seven, with a cross. A lake was a place where numbers held. She did not think a letter would.

"Not out here," she said.

"No," said Kunal.

They walked back along her tracks, hole to hole, and she tapped the haft at every step although she had sounded it all morning. The rope lay slack between them. It was eight of her arm-spans, and he kept to the near end of it, close enough that she could hear his breathing. At the lip of rock above the shore she found a flat shelf, dry and out of the wind, and she sat down on it with her back to the cliff and the letter on her knee.

He did not sit. He stood two paces off with his hands hanging, as a man stands in a doorway when he has not been told to come in. His fingertips were still the colour of tallow. They had been that colour since dawn, because of her.

Ila was thirty-one years old. She had measured four mountains, two rivers and one man's grandfather's grave-field, and she had never once been at a loss for what to do next. She was at a loss now. So she did the thing in front of her.

"Give me your hands."

He gave them. She wrapped them in her own, and they were cold as river stones. She had thought she knew that cold. She had held it for an hour at nineteen fingers' depth, in a manner of speaking. This was only what was left.

"I'm going to kiss you," said Ila. "I'm saying it first so it's on the record. It's thanks. Some of it isn't thanks."

Something went across his face, quick and young. "Which part is which?"

"I'll let you work it out."

She kissed him. It was not a graceful thing. She had to rise on her knees, and the shelf was narrow, and his hands were still folded in hers, so for a moment they were two people holding a bundle between them. His mouth was cold and then it was not. He made a small sound, low in the chest, as if he had been handed something he had stopped expecting. When she let go of his hands to take his collar, they came up and held her face, so carefully that she wanted to laugh. The laugh came out against his mouth and he felt it, and she felt him smile.

She drew back an inch. His eyes were open. He had snow-melt in his lashes.

"I promise nothing," said Ila. She said it plainly, because it was a rule of the place. "Not one thing. If that paper says what I'm afraid it says, I may stand up and draw a line you'd hate. I want you to know it before this is the last thing you remember."

"I wouldn't take a promise today. The rope would hold you to it."

"I know."

"Then why say it?"

"Because I'd rather you were angry at me honestly."

He was quiet. His thumbs moved once on her cheekbones. He was not looking at her hands or his own. She noticed that. A week ago he would have glanced down at his fingertips for the colour.

"I don't know what you'll do with any of it," said Kunal. "The lakes. The numbers. Me. And I'd show you anyway."

It was not a speech. It came out the way her laugh did, before anyone had checked it. She looked, because she could not help looking, at his hands against her jaw. The nails were wax-pale from last night. They had not gone one shade whiter.

"That's true," said Ila.

"I know. I could feel it."

She sat back on her heels, and he let her go. She picked up the paper.

* * *

*Ila,*

*If you are reading this you are at the Glass Lakes, and you have walked there beside him. I hoped it. I will not pretend I was sure.*

*Sixty winters ago the pass began to close. The archive holds the reason in one line, and I will give it to you plain, because nobody gave it to me plain. The price is a life, paid beneath the Shilak Cairn.*

*It was paid. Warden Harish of the Shilak post went down to the Cairn in the dead of that winter. Prince Udai of Neshar guided him, by the way that begins at these lakes. Prince Udai came home. Warden Harish did not.*

*He was your grandfather.*

*The Closing Law was written the next spring, so that no one would walk that way in winter and ask what it cost. I have kept that drawer thirty winters. I will not be the last to keep it unless you help me.*

*Draw the way and read the price before the fourth winter. Tell no one Neshar's part until it is found.*

*Sarala*

She read it once. Then she read it again, slowly, with her finger under the lines, as she read a bearing she meant to trust her life to.

The words did not go in at first. She noticed that the paper was good, and that the queen's hand was small and upright and steady. She noticed that *Harish* was written with a long first stroke, the way a clerk lengthens a name he has written ten thousand times in other men's ledgers. She noticed these things the way you notice a stone in your boot on the way to a cliff.

*He was your grandfather.*

She had known the name. She had known it in the way she knew the name of the first king of Hemkot. It lay in her father's chronicle, in her father's careful clerk's hand, with a date. *Carried down at the thaw.* Her father had said it over his shoulder with his good eye on the page, and she had been a girl, and she had thought, what a tidy word.

*Carried.*

In the hamlets it meant seen across by the hand. In the post's chronicle it meant dead. He had been carried, then. Carried to where?

She remembered the gloves.

Her father had told it once, late, with a cup in him, and never again. Her grandfather had been found at the thaw, sitting whole against a flat stone on the slope above the third bend. His gloves lay on top of it, palms together, set square to the edge, the fingers pointing the way he had been walking. *Like a pair set out for the morning,* her father had said, and shaken his head, and let it go.

She had always thought it a sad picture. A neat man making a neat death. She had even liked it, a little.

It was not neat. It was a receipt.

A man who had been told where he was going, and what it cost, and who had gone anyway. He had taken off his gloves, because he would not need them, and laid them out where someone would find them. Where Rohan would one day find them, in a drunken story, and never work out why.

"Ila."

She had stopped breathing, or near it. She started again.

She felt for the right words, and could not find a single one that was not a measurement. Sixty winters. The Closing Law, written the next spring. Every warden hanged since for crossing a pass in the cold. Meera's mother. The ropes. Ranu's graves, thirty-two of them, in rows on a terraced slope. The whole law had been a lid, and she had spent her life drawing lines on the ground beneath it, and calling that neutral.

*A map is true or false.* She had said it at nineteen and at thirty. And here was the truest map she had ever been handed, and it was a grave map. Her own family's. She could not draw one line of it as a thing with no owner.

Then the other half struck her, and she looked up at him.

*Prince Udai of Neshar guided him.*

Kunal was watching her. He had not moved. His face wore the careful, courteous quiet of a man who has made himself wait. Behind him the Drum lay dark and white and open, and she thought of the thing he had said by the fire three nights ago, as lightly as he might say the time. *Sixty winters ago, in the old women's reckoning. My grandfather walked the princes' way in winter with a guest.* An unnamed guest. The year before the Closing Law.

*I'm counting,* she had told him. He had taken it for arithmetic.

It had been arithmetic. It had come out at a man's name.

She went through it as she would have gone through a field sheet, point by point, and every point turned over under her hand and showed its other face. His family's secret ways, never written, never said to anyone below the passes. *Whoever knows the way owns the people.* The hill council's route-keepers, burned out of their huts. Raghu's men digging nine holes in the snow round a toppled cairn, hunting a carved marker that had never existed. Kunal's own voice, with his fingertips going white at the Mirror: *it matters more than anything.*

She had thought he was guarding his people's road. He was, but he was guarding something else with it. Perhaps he did not know it himself. The way began at these lakes and ended beneath a cairn, and at the end of it was a man's bones, or a debt, or a door. The secrecy that kept his people free also kept a grave unmapped.

Her neutral map was a family grave map. His closed hand was a hand over a grave.

And neither of them knew, except the one holding the letter.

* * *

She folded it along its creases. It took three tries. Her hands were pink and steady, and she looked at them, because that was a thing she did now.

She weighed it. She sat on the shelf with the cliff at her back, and she weighed it in the open, as he had taught her to weigh a name.

She could tell him. *Your grandfather walked mine to his death.* She could put the letter in his hands, sealed or unsealed, and watch his face. She had sworn the archive oath to take her commission, on a long gold-edged page in a hall in Taravan, and it bound her to silence about whatever the crown's papers held. Taking it had not cost her a night's sleep. Now the oath had a shape. The queen had added a line of her own on top of it: *Tell no one Neshar's part.* She did not owe that line her obedience. She owed it, she thought, a hearing.

And she could not tell Kunal what his own grandfather had done, until she knew what it was. A man could walk another man down a mountain and come home. It might have been a crime. It might have been a mercy. The letter had no word for which. Udai had been one of two men on a mountain sixty winters ago, and only one had come back to say.

She would be accusing him before she had any right to. She would be turning a prince's grandfather into a murderer on a queen's say-so, in front of a man who had, an hour ago, put his open hands on her face.

Silence was free. She turned that over too, as coldly as she could. She knew how it would look from the other side, how it would read, since she had taught herself last night to read it. A refusal on this question was an answer, and the answer was *yes, there is something.* But *something* was true. She could not make it smaller. The rope would not make her pay for it.

Under the rope, silence was free. She hated that it was.

"Ila," said Kunal again, very quietly. "What does it say?"

He did not move closer. He had settled on his heels at the rim of the shelf, and the rope lay slack across the snow between them.

She said, "It says that I'm to find where your way goes. And read what's written there." She watched her own hands. They stayed pink. "That's the most of it. The rest I can't tell you yet."

It was true. She felt it go through the cold air as a plain thing, and the stones above the shore did not sing. She kept her eyes on his face, because she owed him that.

He looked at her hands. He did it openly, and it cost him to do it openly, and she saw the cost. It went across him in a long breath. He had taken her cold last night. He knew precisely how a lie looked on her skin. He knew, now, how a true refusal looked, and it looked like nothing at all. It looked exactly like a closed door.

*Can't tell you yet.* He turned the words over, the way she did with a bearing. She could almost see him count the ways it might go. A warrant. A garrison. A line across the Under-whatever his grandmothers had never named. Pritam would have asked again, softly, and kept asking. Kunal's hand moved to his knee and stayed there.

"You said 'yet,'" he said.

"I did."

"That's a thing you'd say if you meant it."

"I mean it."

"Yes." He let out a breath. "Your hands say so."

She had thought he might argue, or be angry, or turn courteous again. He did none of those things. He sat on his heels at the edge of the shelf, with the Drum behind him and the whole white morning on his shoulders, and set something down. She watched him do it. It was the size of a mountain. He set it down quietly, as a man puts a sleeping child on a bed.

He had been born to the principle that a secret kept was a life kept. He had been shown, with her hands held out in the firelight, what it was to be on the other side of one. He was choosing, anyway. It was not trust, exactly, and not simple belief. It was something that had to be built again each time, with a hole in the middle where the answer should have been.

It was the most generous thing she had ever watched anyone do.

And she was sitting on the answer.

*He guided him.* The sentence stood in her mind with its long clerk's stroke. *Prince Udai of Neshar guided him, by the way that begins at these lakes.* It was not a question of whether Kunal's grandfather had done a terrible thing. It was that Kunal did not know there was a thing to do. All his life he had been told the princes' way was a gift to the valley, a road for the hill people when the crown closed the passes. Not once had anyone told him it was a road a man walked another man along, to a stone, and left him.

She held the letter flat against her coat. It was a very small piece of paper.

"Kunal," she said. "Whatever's there, I'll tell you the first moment I can. I'm not making that a promise. The rope would hold me to it, and I don't know that I can keep it."

"I know."

"I'd rather you were angry at me honestly."

"You said that." The corner of his mouth moved. "Do you think I'll be angry?"

"I think you'll be right to be."

He got up. He did it slowly, and offered her his hand, and she took it, and he drew her to her feet on the narrow shelf. They stood very close. The wind had dropped, and the cliff stood over them with its black seams. She looked at his face and saw that it was perfectly steady. His eyes were bright, and not with anger.

"The way begins at the head of these lakes," said Kunal. "It's mine to show. It was my grandfather's, and he'd have said it wasn't his to show either." His hand tightened once on hers and let go. "I won't make you swear to what you'll do with it. I haven't the right, and you've said you'd not give it. But you came here to find where it goes. So."

He turned to the east, where the third lake lay hidden behind its spur, booming softly, a deep note that came up through the rock into her boots.

"Then I'll take you."

She said nothing. She could not say anything that was both kind and true, and she had learned this week what the second kind cost.

She knew what his family had done, and he did not. And he had just offered, with his open hands and his steady face, to walk her down the road where his grandfather had walked hers.

She folded the letter into her coat and tied the stitches shut with a shaking hand, and went after him.
