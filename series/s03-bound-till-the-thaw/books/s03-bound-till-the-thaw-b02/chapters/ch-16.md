# Chapter 16: The Grave Map

She did not unfold it.

The seal lay in two halves in her palm, red wax with its thread of darker red. The paper sat in her other hand, still folded in three. Ila looked down the length of the Drum at her own small black holes. Eleven fingers. Nine. Seven, with a cross. A lake was a place where numbers held. She did not think a letter would.

"Not out here," she said.

"No," said Kunal.

They walked back along her tracks, hole to hole, and she tapped the haft at every step although she had sounded it all morning. The rope lay slack between them. It was eight of her arm-spans, and he kept to the near end of it, close enough that she could hear him breathe. At the lip of rock above the shore she found a flat shelf, dry and out of the wind. She sat with her back to the cliff and the letter on her knee.

He stood two paces off with his hands hanging, as a man stands in a doorway when he has not been told to come in. His fingertips were still grey at the ends. They had been grey since dawn, because of her.

Ila was thirty-one years old. She had measured four mountains and two rivers, and she had never once been at a loss for what to do next. She was at a loss now. So she did the thing in front of her.

"Give me your hands."

He gave them. She wrapped them in her own, and they were cold as river stones. She had thought she knew that cold. She had stood half a morning on eleven fingers of ice and trusted it more than this. This was only what was left of a night.

"I'm going to kiss you," said Ila. "I'm saying it first so it's on the record. It's thanks. Some of it isn't thanks."

His brows went up and his mouth pulled in at one corner, the look of a man who has been handed a riddle and means to enjoy it. "Which part is which?"

"I'll let you work it out."

She kissed him. It was not graceful. She had to rise on her knees, and the shelf was narrow, and his hands were still folded in hers, so for a moment they were two people holding a bundle between them. His mouth was cold and then it was not. He made a small sound, low in the chest, surprised out of him. When she let go of his hands to take his collar, they came up and held her face, so carefully that she wanted to laugh. The laugh came out against his mouth and he felt it, and she felt him smile.

She drew back an inch. His eyes were open. He had snow-melt in his lashes.

"I promise nothing," said Ila. It was a rule of the place, and she said it like one. "Not one thing. If that paper says what I'm afraid it says, I may stand up and draw a line you'd hate. I want you to know it before this is the last thing you remember."

"I wouldn't take a promise today. The rope would hold you to it."

"I know."

"Then why say it?"

"Because I'd rather you were angry at me honestly."

He was quiet. His thumbs moved once on her cheekbones. He was not looking at her hands or his own. Ila marked that. A week ago he would have glanced down at his fingertips for the colour.

"I don't know what you'll do with any of it," said Kunal. "The lakes. The numbers. Me. And I'd show you anyway."

It was not a speech. It came out the way her laugh did, before anyone had checked it. She looked at his hands against her jaw. The nails were wax-pale from last night. They had not gone one shade whiter.

"That's true," said Ila.

"I know. I could feel it."

"You could not."

"I could feel you decide not to argue." His thumb moved again. "It's a different thing from being agreed with. I'm learning the difference."

She sat back on her heels, and he let her go. His hands stayed in the air a moment, shaped to her face, before he made them rest on his knees. She picked up the paper.

* * *

*Ila,*

*If you are reading this you are at the Glass Lakes, and you have walked there beside him. I hoped it. I will not pretend I was sure.*

*Sixty-one winters ago the pass began to close. The archive holds the reason in one line, and I will give it to you plain, because nobody gave it to me plain. The price is a life, paid beneath the Shilak Cairn.*

*It was paid. Warden Harish of the Shilak post went down toward the Cairn in the dead of that winter. Prince Udai of Neshar guided him, by the way that begins at these lakes. Prince Udai came home. Warden Harish did not come home.*

*He was your grandfather.*

*The Closing Law was written the next spring, so that no one would walk that way in winter and ask what it cost. I have kept that drawer since my crowning. I will not be the last to keep it unless you help me.*

*Draw the way and read the price before the fourth winter. Tell no one Neshar's part until it is found.*

*Sarala*

She read it once. Then she read it again, slowly, with her finger under the lines, as she read a bearing she meant to trust her life to.

The words did not go in at first. The paper was good, and the queen's hand was small and upright and steady. *Harish* was written with a long first stroke, the way a clerk lengthens a name he has written ten thousand times in other men's ledgers. She noticed these things the way you notice a stone in your boot on the way to a cliff.

*He was your grandfather.*

She had known the name as she knew the name of the first king of Hemkot. It lay in her father's chronicle, in her father's careful clerk's hand, with a date. *Carried down at the thaw.* She had read it over his shoulder as a girl, and she had thought, what a tidy word.

*Carried.* In the hamlets it meant seen across by the hand. In the post's chronicle it meant dead.

Then she remembered the gloves.

Her father had told it once, late, with a cup in him, and never again. Her grandfather had been found at the thaw, sitting whole against a flat stone on the slope above the third bend. His gloves lay on top of it, palms together, set square to the edge, the fingers pointing the way he had been walking. *Like a pair set out for the morning,* her father had said, and shaken his head, and let it go.

She had always thought it a sad picture. A neat man making a neat death. She had even liked it, a little.

It was not neat. It was a receipt.

He had been told where he was going, and what it cost, and he had gone anyway. He had taken off his gloves because he would not need them, and laid them out where someone would find them. Where her father would one day tell it, drunk, and never work out why.

Out on the Drum the ice let go of something with a long low note. She felt it in the rock under her.

She hunted for the right words and found only measurements. Sixty-one winters. The Closing Law, written the next spring. Every crosser a warden had hanged since. Meera's mother, dead of the cough behind the closed pass. Ranu's graves in their rows on the terraced slope. The whole law had been a lid, and she had spent her life drawing lines on the ground beneath it and calling that neutral.

*A map is true or false.* She had said it at nineteen and she had said it at thirty. And here was the truest map she had ever been handed, and it was a grave map. Her own family's. She could not draw one line of it as a thing with no owner.

She took out her field book and shut it over the Drum soundings, the eleven and the nine and the seven with its cross, and put her palm flat on the cover, as if the numbers might get out.

Then the other half struck her, and she looked up.

*Prince Udai of Neshar guided him.*

Kunal was watching her. He had not moved. His face wore a careful, courteous quiet. Behind him the Drum lay dark and white and open, and she heard him again at the toppled door cairn on the Hollow Moon, as lightly as he might say the hour. *Sixty winters ago, in the old women's reckoning. My grandfather walked the princes' way in winter with a guest.* An unnamed guest. The year before the Closing Law.

*I'm counting,* she had told him. He had taken it for arithmetic.

It had been arithmetic. It had come out at a man's name.

She went through it as she would have gone through a field sheet, point by point, and every point turned over under her hand and showed its other face. His family's secret ways, never written, never said to anyone below the passes. *Whoever knows the way owns the people.* The route-keepers, burned out of their huts. Raghu's men digging nine holes in the snow round a toppled cairn. Kunal's own voice, with his fingertips going white at the Mirror: *it matters more than anything.*

She had thought he was guarding his people's road. He was, but the road also guarded something. Perhaps he did not know it himself. The secrecy that kept his people free had also kept a grave unmapped.

Her neutral map was a family grave map.

And neither of them knew it, except the one holding the letter.

* * *

She folded it along its creases. It took three tries. Her hands were pink and steady, and she looked at them, because that was a thing she did now.

She could tell him. *Your grandfather walked mine to the Cairn, and mine did not come home.* She could put the letter in his hands and watch his face. But the archive oath sat on her like a hand. She had sworn it, on a long gold-edged page in a hall in Taravan, to take her commission, and it bound her silence about whatever the crown's papers held. Taking it had not cost her a night's sleep. The queen had laid a line of her own across it: *Tell no one Neshar's part.* Ila did not owe that line her obedience. She owed it a hearing.

And she did not know what Udai had done. A man could walk another man down a mountain and come home, and it might be a crime or a mercy. The letter had no word for which. To say it now was to name a prince's grandfather a murderer on a queen's say-so, to a man who, a quarter hour ago, had held her face in both hands.

So she would say nothing. She made herself look at that squarely, since it was a choice and not a thing that merely happened to her. Silence was free. The rope would not make her pay for it. That was exactly what she hated. Any other cost she could have carried to him and laid down.

"Ila," said Kunal, very quietly. "What does it say?"

He had settled on his heels at the rim of the shelf, and the rope lay slack across the snow between them.

She said, "It says I'm to find where your way goes, and read what's written there. The rest I can't tell you yet." She watched her own hands. They stayed pink. It was true. It went through the cold air and the stones above the shore did not sing. She kept her eyes on his face, because she owed him that.

He looked at her hands. He did it openly, and it cost him to do it openly, and she saw the cost go across him in one long breath. He had taken her cold last night. He knew exactly how a lie looked on her skin. He knew now how a true refusal looked, and it looked like nothing at all. It looked like a closed door.

*Can't tell you yet.* He turned the words over the way she turned a bearing. She could almost watch him count the ways it might go. A warrant. A garrison. A line drawn across land his grandmothers had never named. Pritam would have asked again, softly, and kept on asking. Kunal's hand moved to his knee.

"You said 'yet,'" he said.

"I did."

"That's a thing you'd say if you meant it."

"I mean it."

"Yes." He let out a breath. "Your hands say so."

She had thought he might argue, or turn courteous and cold. He did none of those things. He sat on his heels with the Drum behind him and the whole white morning on his shoulders, and she watched his hand leave his knee and turn palm-up on the snow between them, open, empty, there to be seen. The snow took the print of it, four fingers and a palm, the tips still grey.

And she was still sitting on the answer.

"Kunal," she said. "Whatever's there, I'll tell you the first moment I can. I'm not making that a promise. The rope would hold me to it, and I don't know that I can keep it."

"I know."

"I'd rather you were angry at me honestly."

"You said that." The corner of his mouth moved. "Do you think I'll be angry?"

"I think you'll be right to be."

He got up slowly, and offered her the same hand, and she took it, and he drew her to her feet on the narrow shelf. They stood very close. The wind had dropped, and the cliff stood over them with its black seams. She looked at his face and saw that it was perfectly steady. His eyes were bright, and not with anger.

"The way begins at the head of these lakes," said Kunal. "It's mine to show. It was my grandfather's, and he'd have said it wasn't his to show either." His hand tightened once on hers and let go. "I won't make you swear what you'll do with it. I haven't the right, and you've said you'd not give it. But you came here to find where it goes. So."

He turned east, where the third lake lay hidden behind its spur, booming softly, a deep note that came up through the rock into her boots.

"Then I'll take you."

She could not say anything that was both kind and true, and she had learned this week what the second kind cost. She said nothing. He took the silence for what it was and did not press it.

She knew what his family had done, and he did not. And he had just offered, with an open hand and a steady face, to walk her down the road where his grandfather had walked hers.

She folded the letter into her coat, buttoned it inside against her ribs with a shaking hand, and went after him.
