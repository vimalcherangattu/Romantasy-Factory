# Chapter 26: The Drum Fold

Ila did not wake when he cut her laces, and that frightened him more than the ice had.

He had dragged her off the lake and laid her on the shingle with his coat under her head. The rope lay between their wrists in a slack loop. Dusk was going fast. Out on the Drum the ice held the last light like a pewter plate, and under the cliff, a short walk off, the fold waited with no lamp in it. He did not look at the fold.

Her boots had frozen into a hitch he could not pick. He sawed the lace with his knife, the handle pinned in the heel of his dead hand, and worked the left boot off, then the sock, which came away stiff as bark.

Her foot was white to the instep. Not pale. White as tallow, with a shine on it.

He put both palms around it.

The cold came up his arms the way water climbs a wick. He had known hill cold all his life, the honest kind that bites and lets go. This had a size to it. It poured into his wrists and kept pouring, and with it came a fact as plain as a stone set on a table: *a lie was told, and it was great.*

He did not learn what the truth was. The cold did not carry that. It carried only the weight of what she had hidden, and the weight told him how much it mattered.

*They're gone. I burned them.*

"Don't," said Ila. Her eyes had opened. Her voice was thick. "Kunal. Your hands."

"Be quiet."

"It's mine. I paid for it."

"You did." He held on until the cold stopped arriving. His palms went past burning into something worse, and when he lifted them they were red and tight, with blisters rising along the lines of his fingers. "Now we've both paid."

The foot lay between them. It had pinked up, all but the edge. The two smallest toes were the colour of old candle-ends, with a dull grey bloom under the nails. He touched one with a knuckle. It did not give back any warmth.

The cold had reached them before he did.

"Those two," he said.

"I know." She had pushed herself up on one elbow and was looking at her own foot as she would at a stranger's chain. "I couldn't feel them to know. I'd have guessed by dark."

"You told me they were ash."

"I did."

"They aren't ash." He heard how level he sounded. It was the voice he used in council when he wanted to bleed a man quietly. "A great lie, and about the sheets. So you have them. You've had them sewn in your coat the whole morning while I knelt at that hearth counting cinders."

She said nothing. Silence was free, and he had taught himself to hate it in other people's mouths.

"Then I'll do the sum," said Kunal. "There's one person in these hills who'd want them kept from Raghu and from me. One person who has your oath. You've carried that letter in your hem for a week and refused me twice, and now this." He pulled the sock over the ruined toes with the heel of his hand, and it was not gentle. "You'd let Dhanu die for your queen's map."

She took her hands out of her sleeves.

She held them up between them in the dusk, open, the way he had held his own at the fire before the Eye. The fingertips were pink. The nails were clear.

"They're not for the queen," said Ila. "Or Raghu. And they're not for trading you."

Her hands stayed warm.

It was a true answer, and it cost her nothing, and that was the trouble with it. He would have taken a lie. A lie he could have hated cleanly.

"Then what are they for?"

"That's free too," she said. "I won't say tonight."

"Dhanu is on that ice."

"I know where he is." Her voice shook once and steadied. "You asked once what I'd have said, if I'd put a figure to it. If you'd handed them over, I'd have said one in ten. Less. I'd have said it and watched you hate me."

"I might have been the one."

"Yes. You might." She looked at him. "I'd say it again."

He got his arm under her knees and his other behind her back, and stood, and the blisters split across his palms. He did not let it reach his face. A rope's length of cold air lay between him and the fold. He walked into it.

* * *

The shingle under the cliff was a grey strip, forty paces from the ice to the rock. He had known it since he was nine, when Dhanu had taught him to skip stones here. Now it had a lantern on it, shuttered to a slit, and men.

Two pairs stood on the cornice above, four dark shapes against the last of the sky, the rope between each pair hanging in a curve. One held something to his eye that caught the light. Spyglass. Kunal felt it settle on his chest like a thumb.

Raghu waited at the water's edge, bound at the wrist by eight spans of grey wool to a boy.

Kunal had met the captain twice, across the Garh Neshar hall, when Raghu wore the regent's bell on his shoulder and a courtier's smile. He wore no smile now. He was a lean man gone leaner, with ice in his beard and the careful stillness of someone who had not slept since the Snow Fair. The boy tied to him was perhaps twenty. Grey wool, a brass bell on the shoulder, a face too young for the cold on it. Pasang. Kunal knew the name from a Ranu story Meera had told, and could not now have said which story.

"Prince," said Raghu. "You've brought me a woman and no paper."

"I've brought you me."

"I asked for the sheets."

"They're ash." He said it flatly. He had decided on the walk that it was the one thing he could say with a steady face, because it was what Ila had said, and he would stand on her word if it was the last thing he stood on. Her breath was warm against his collar. "I'll give you every turn I know. I walked the Stair. I walked the Moon. I stood in the Eye, and I can walk it again. Take me. I'm the way you wanted. I don't need paper to open it."

"A man who can't produce a thing is an inconvenient man."

"Then I'm inconvenient."

Raghu studied him, as a clerk studies a coin. Kunal let his eyes go past the captain's shoulder, out onto the grey ice, and there they stopped.

Dhanu lay on the Drum, twenty paces out. He lay on his back with one arm flung wide, the lame foot turned in. A scrap of blue wool lay across his chest. He was entirely still.

Beside him, on her side with her knees drawn up, lay Gauri. A strip of cloth was knotted across her mouth. Her wrists were bound before her, and a cord ran from her belt to an iron peg driven into the ice. Her eyes were open. They met his, and they were as dry as stones.

"Dhanu," said Kunal.

The lake gave him back a low note.

"Dhanu."

"He's resting," said Raghu.

Kunal heard something in his own chest drop away from its footing. He had been ready to hear that Dhanu was hurt, or starved, or frightened. He had walked the whole shore with Ila's *one in ten* in his ear. He had not been ready for the plain flat fact of a man lying very still on ice.

"Captain," said Pasang, low. He was staring where Kunal stared. His voice cracked. "Sir. You told me the lame one was only..."

"The limping one went home this morning, Pasang," said Raghu, loudly, in the voice of a man reading a notice. "You saw him go. That is a farmer from the east fold. Keep your eyes on the prince."

The boy's mouth closed. He looked at the ice and then, quickly, away.

Ila's fingers dug into Kunal's shoulder.

"His hand," she breathed. "Raghu's. Look at his hand."

It was resting on the hilt of his sword, and the fingertips were white. They were white to the first joint, bloodless, like a row of peeled almonds.

Kunal looked at them and could make nothing of it. A lie, small, told to the boy at his wrist. The limping one went home. And Dhanu lay out there where anyone could see.

He could not do the sum. His head would not take it. Whatever Raghu was hiding from Pasang, it was not that a man was dead, because the whole shore could see that. It was something else, and the thing in front of him was Dhanu's open, frosted face.

"The sheets," said Raghu softly, "or the ice takes the woman. And I'll start with her, so you can watch."

He lifted two fingers to the cornice.

"Eleven paces left of the boom line," said Ila, clear and loud, into Kunal's ear, "then straight."

He ran.

* * *

Ila's soundings were in his legs before his mind caught up. He had stood at her shoulder on the Drum two days ago and watched her chip her holes with his own knife and the iron spike, tapping the haft, listening for the ring. She had called every figure aloud. *Eight fingers. Eleven. Seven here, don't.* He had laughed at her. He had said nobody sounded the Drum, because the Drum could be heard.

Now he ran with her weight crushing his blisters and his heart beating in the burst skin of his palms, and her voice came steady as a chain-man's tally.

"Boom line, there, the pale seam, it's on your right. Eleven paces. Count them. Don't look at it."

He counted. Behind him a shout went up, Raghu's, then the dry slap of boots on shingle and then on ice. He did not turn his head.

"Straight. Straight. The ice is fourteen here. It's good, Kunal. Hold the line."

Behind them a man yelled, "Cut across! Cut him off!" And a second answered, close, and the rhythm of the boots changed. They were coming on the chord, the short way, through the pale seam. The place where the Drum was thinnest was exactly where a man in a hurry would think it safest.

"Don't look," said Ila.

The ice spoke.

It was a single note, enormous, rolling up through the soles of his boots and into his teeth, and then it broke into a sound he had never heard from the Drum, a wet tearing like cloth ripped under water. A man screamed. Another screamed with him. The rope that tied them together dragged the second one under with the first.

Kunal slowed. He could not help it. Over his shoulder he saw two dark shapes in a black hole, white with spray, and a rope thrashing at the lip. Then the cornice men were shouting, and Raghu was shouting, and the whole shore was turned toward the break.

"Go on," said Ila, very quietly. "They're gone. Go on."

He did not go on. He veered.

Gauri lay twelve paces to his left, with the lantern's slit of light flung across her bound hands. She had pulled herself up on her elbows. Above the gag her eyes were wide and furious.

He dropped to one knee on the ice, Ila clamped against his chest. The knife was already out. He could not feel the handle. He pinned it with the heel of his dead hand, and sawed the cord at her belt, then the cloth between her wrists. The edge went through her skin as well as the wool, and she hissed. She tore the gag off herself.

"The huts," said Kunal. "Run. Don't stop."

She did not look at him. She looked at Dhanu, lying a few paces away with the blue wool on his chest. Her face was perfectly steady, and he had never seen anything so terrible.

"Gauri."

"I know," she said. It came out a rasp. She put one hand flat on Dhanu's coat, as a woman checks bread, and took it away. "Go."

She ran. She ran badly, bent over, wrists still tied, her legs driving at the shingle. Behind her the shore men were wheeling toward the hole in the ice, and not one of them looked at a woman running the other way.

He should have gone to Dhanu. He knew it. He should have knelt, and shut the eyes, and said something, anything. Frost lay in the lashes. A scrap of blue wool lay across the chest. He had known this man thirty years.

"Kunal," said Ila.

"I know."

"We can't."

"I *know*," said Kunal, and ran.

* * *

There was no word for the hour that came after. He ran along the shore until the shingle gave out, and walked when he could not run, and walked when he could not walk, with the rope dragging and the lantern shouts growing small behind. Ila gave him bearings in the dark. Once she said, "Put me down, you can't," and he said, "I can," and she did not say it again.

His hands had stopped being hands. They were two sheets of burning where something wet ran, and he held her with them anyway, because the alternative was to put her down.

They crossed the neck of rock between the Drum and the Eye by the blue stones, which he could see now, faint and steady, a thin chain pulling him on. Raghu's men were bound. They would see them too, if they came. He did not let himself think of it. He kept his eyes on the next cairn, and on the next.

The cleft opened out of the cliff like a shut mouth beginning to speak. He carried her in past the lip and the ice hollow and set her down on the dry gravel, against the wall. Then he stood and looked at what they had.

Nothing. His pack lay at the hut, with the flint and the lamp oil and the last of the bread. Hers was with it. The cleft held rock, and cold, and the sound of water far down, and no fuel. He had pushed a prince's name and nothing in his hands onto a frozen lake, and here was the whole of what that bought.

Ila had her bad foot drawn up under her. She was shaking so hard her teeth clicked. She did not complain, and she did not ask to be warm. She looked at him, and he saw her get ready to say something true.

"Don't," said Kunal.

"I only..."

"Don't say anything true to me tonight." He sat down against the opposite wall, with eight spans of wool between them and his blistered hands lying open on his knees like something he had borrowed. "I'm not sure what I'll do with it."

She shut her mouth. The rope lay slack across the gravel between them.

Behind them, on the ice, beneath a sky going black, Dhanu lay with his face turned up and nobody to cover it. Kunal sat in the dark and counted his own breathing, because it was all there was to count, and did not know yet whether he would hate her by morning, or only wish he could.
