# Chapter 9: Between the Cairns

They went into the Hollow Moon at moonset, eight spans of pale rope between them and no light but the stars.

The line here was nothing like the Meadow Cairn's. It showed only at the old cairns, waist-high heaps spaced a few hundred paces apart, each wearing a thin blue skin of glow like frost with a fever. Between them the snow lay black and blank. The mountain showed you where you could stand. It left you to cross the rest on your own wits.

Ila had two wits to cross it with. The steady star over the north rim gave her a bearing. Her paces gave her distance. Kunal had a third, and he muttered it under his breath as he walked, a shepherd's rhyme with the tune filed off:

*Fan on the left hand, cold ridge on the right,*
*count three hundred where the snow goes blue,*
*and a hundred more to light.*

"Three hundred and eight," said Ila, as the snow went blue under the starlight.

"The song says three hundred."

"The song is eight paces short."

"The song," said Kunal, "allows for shepherds."

"The song allows for sheep."

He made a sound in his chest that wasn't quite a laugh, because they had agreed on the cliff that nothing louder than a breath would cross the bowl tonight. The sound went into his collar and stayed there. She noted, without wanting to, how he had kept it.

On the west lip, a mile and more off at three-fifteen, a red coal sat on the rim.

The watchers' fire did not flicker. Somebody fed it. It was a steady, careless, comfortable fire, and that was what she disliked about it. A man who thought no one was coming could afford one. So could a man who wanted to be looked at.

"They've lit one," she said. "A trap would light two."

"Why two?"

"So you'd look at both and miss the dark between."

He turned his head a fraction. "That's a very sinister thing to know."

"It's what a surveyor does with a spare hour."

They kept to the east wall, where its shadow fell long across the snow and swallowed the pale rope. Cairn by cairn, the line led them north.

* * *

The slope came after the third cairn, and Ila knew it before she saw it.

She knew it by the sound under her boots. Ahead, the east wall let down a long white tongue of snow between two ribs of rock. The starlight lay on it smooth and fine, like a sheet pulled tight. The line ran straight across the middle.

"Stop."

She went forward the length of the rope and knelt. She laid her palm flat on the snow, then rapped it with her knuckles. Underneath came a faint, low *whump*, a drum with nothing in it.

"Thirty-eight degrees," said Ila. "Near enough. The wind's loaded it from the north-west, two days ago, maybe three. That's a slab on a rotten layer, and the layer is talking to me." She took her hand back. "One chance in five it goes if we cross high and quiet. More if either of us is clumsy."

"Is that a stated odds, or a comfort?"

"It's what I'd put in the field book."

Kunal came up beside her, as far as the rope let him. His face was a pale shape and his voice was perfectly level. "The lower third's no good either. Summer water runs under it. A spring comes out of the rock above and cuts a channel through the ground. The snow there's a lid on a hole." He pointed with his chin. "Under that bare patch, see the dimple? Last July I put a mule in it to the shoulder."

"Then low is out."

"And high is where it breaks."

"Yes." She looked at the slope. "Middle, then. Above your dimple and below the crown. It's the slowest way and the dullest. We step in my steps, one at a time, no talk. A weight is a weight, so we don't pile ours on the same ground."

"The rope's eight spans."

"Then I go to the end of it, and you wait here, and you come when I stop."

He did not argue. She saw him not arguing, the whole long effort of it, a man who had taken a hundred slopes on his own say-so. Then he bowed to her, a small dry bow in the dark.

"You read. I'll hold the other end of the rope and the dimple."

She went out onto the white.

It took a long time. She put each boot down as if the snow were a thing she owed money to. Halfway across, the slab spoke under her with a deep, soft *whump*, and a crack ran away from her left boot, bright as a hair, and went on a long way. Then it stopped.

She stood in it and did not breathe. She counted to forty. Nothing slid.

"Come," she said, to the air. Her voice came out almost normal. "In my steps. At the crack, step wide, don't jump."

He came. He was careful, he was quick, and at the crack he took her whole stride and not an inch more. Then he was beside her on the far rib of rock, and neither of them said anything for twenty breaths.

"One in five," said Kunal, finally.

"It was a good five."

"I will tell it at weddings. 'The crown surveyor said, one in five, and I believed her.'"

"It's a published figure."

That time the laugh was loud enough that she put a finger to her lips, and he doubled over to smother it, shaking, without a sound.

* * *

They went on. The line crept north in its dim blue beads. Between the fourth cairn and the fifth, Kunal said, "The next light is half a mile on."

"It's four hundred and ten paces."

"That's about half a mile."

"It's a third, and the third is mine to say." She did not look round. "A thing that's four hundred and ten paces off is not about anything."

He laughed again without sound, a long silent shake that she felt as a pull on the rope, and then he said, "Four hundred and ten. I am corrected. I'll try to be more certain of my own hills."

"You don't need to be certain. You need to say what you don't know."

"That is a great deal to ask of a prince."

"It's the whole job of a map."

At the fifth cairn the wall made a lee, a scoop of still air where the wind could not reach, and she let them stop. She took out the field book and her bearings pencil, and sighted back across the bowl to the red coal, and put the point to the page.

The lead snapped.

It was the third time since dusk. She swore under her breath, quite fluently, in the dialect of the Taravan survey office, and began picking at the case with fingers that did not want to work.

"It's the cold," said Kunal. "It makes them brittle."

"I know why."

"You said once," said Kunal, "on the Stair. To nobody. That they were breaking on you."

She had said it to the air, mid-climb, half a sentence of complaint, and gone on climbing. She had forgotten it entirely.

He held out his hand, palm up, a bare brown hand with the rope-scrape still healing across it. "Give me the rest of them. They'll keep in my coat."

It was a small thing. It was her pencils. She had carried her own instruments across eleven winters of hills, as she carried her own opinions, and the only person she had ever let touch the case was herself.

She held it a moment longer than she needed to. Then she tipped the leads into his palm.

He opened his coat. He put them inside, against his chest, over the shirt, and buttoned it again, and she watched him do it with a feeling she could not file. Then they waited in the lee for the cold to go out of her fingers, and neither of them said what they were waiting for.

"Give me one," said Ila, after a time.

He took one out, slowly, with two fingers. It was warm. It was warm as bread, as a hand just out of a pocket. She took it, and it did not snap.

* * *

"A name," said Ila. "You owe me tonight's."

"You have it. I gave it on the cliff, too freely." Kunal leaned his back against the cairn, which hummed under his shoulders with a low, dim, blue patience. "The Hollow Moon. The shepherds' summer way to Kuhl."

"You said that this morning, and I didn't write it."

"No. I noticed."

"You'd said it like a man who hadn't decided to." She uncapped the case. "I didn't want to take a name you hadn't meant to give."

He was quiet. Then, in a different voice, slower, he said it again, each word set down by itself. "The Hollow Moon. The shepherds' summer way to Kuhl."

She wrote it exactly, in the margin of the bowl, with the warm lead. The cairn hummed on, low and level.

"My question, then," he said.

"Ask."

"When you finish this map, do you give it to the queen alone?"

She had known it would come. She had not known how it would feel to be asked it by a man who had just handed her back her own name for his hills.

"To the queen," said Ila, "and the archive." She made herself go on. "I don't know who reads the archive. I asked, last autumn, for the file on the Shilak Cairn. The clerk told me the crown keeps it in a drawer I haven't a key to. Whoever has the key will read this, too."

"That's more than I asked."

"You asked who. That's the true size of the answer."

He looked at her hands in her lap. She did not know why. He looked, and then he looked at her face, and whatever he had been braced for was not what he found there.

"Thank you," said Kunal.

"Don't. It isn't a gift. It's a line."

"I know." His voice was rough. "That's why."

She turned to the page so she would not have to see him.

* * *

The bearing of the fire had come out the same from the cliff at dawn and from here, within three degrees. From her two stations and the baseline she had paced, she could fix it. She had the figures before she wanted them. A mile and an eighth, a little over, on the black shoulder of rock where the glass had winked. And in the glow around the fire, the long smear of shapes: two low humps, which were tents, and a third dark thing she thought was a horse line, which she would mark *unclear*.

She sat with the pencil an inch above the paper.

It was true. It was there. It was four, five men, someone's lamp trimmed, and a sheet that showed a camp on the Hollow Moon's west lip was a sheet that told the crown where to find them. That was a use of her line. It wasn't a use she had chosen.

She had never chosen, she'd told herself. She had only drawn.

She drew the fire as a small ring. She drew the humps and wrote *tents?* and the horse line and wrote *unclear*, and she put the date and the bearing beside it, and she looked up.

"I've put them on the sheet," said Ila. "The fire and what's round it. I'm telling you now so you don't find it later."

His face did a quick, complicated thing in the dark. "May I see?"

She turned the book. He bent over it, close, and the rope between them lay along his arm. He read the figures and then the margin. He read *unclear.*

"It's good," he said, as if it hurt. "It's very good. Every line I'd have drawn myself." He straightened. "That's the first mark on your whole sheet I'd have put there."

"That's chance. It's not a policy."

"I know. It's why I'm afraid of you."

"You said that at Ewe's Rest."

"It's more true tonight."

The rope between them hung quiet. Neither of them did anything about it. After a while she put the lead back in his hand, and he put it back inside his coat, and the buttons went down in the dark one by one.

* * *

Dawn came up grey out of the north rim, and with it came the end of the bowl.

The land tilted. The snow gave way to a stony shoulder, the first rock since the slope. At the top of it, where the route left the Hollow Moon for the high pastures of Kuhl, there should have been a keeper's cairn, a tall one, with a slab on top and a niche for a lamp. Dhanu had called it the door.

It lay on its side.

The stones had been torn apart and flung down the slope. The slab was cracked in two. The great flat stones, old, grey, lichened over with a hundred years of shepherds' thumbs, were scattered like bones across the scree. Ila walked among them with the rope trailing, and the hairs stood up along her arms.

On the face of the nearest, someone had cut a line of marks with a knife-point. Fresh, white, deep in the lichen. A bell. A round-shouldered brass bell with a crossed hatch for the clapper, drawn three times, the same bell, on three stones.

Behind her, Kunal made no sound at all.

"You know it," said Ila.

"I know it." He crouched and laid two fingers on the scratches as if he could feel the heat of the hand that made them. "It's Raghu's."

"When?"

"I can't say."

She looked at the snow around the base. It had been trodden, and trodden again, the prints softened and filled with blown grain, so that she could not tell whether it had been a day or two or three. She was a woman who read ground for her living. That was what she did not like.

The stones should have been lit. She had seen the blue on every cairn through the night. Now they lay dull in the grey light, and the line that should have gone on north beyond them, faint and patient, was nowhere she could see.

The rope between them hummed nothing at all.
