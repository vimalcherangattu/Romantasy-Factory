# Chapter 29: The Under-Cairn

The lamp died in the crawl. After that there was only the tick of meltwater on stone and Kunal's voice, counting.

"Thirty-one," he said, behind her. "Thirty-two."

Ila stopped, crutch planted, breathing. The black pressed against her open eyes like a hand. She had given up trying to see in it a hundred paces back.

"That's my pace you're counting," she said. "Not yours."

"Yes."

"Mine isn't a pace anymore. It's a lurch with a stick."

"I know. I'm counting it as one and a quarter spans, because that's what it measured on the gravel this morning." A pause. "You told me to carry a figure when I didn't have the true one. This is the figure."

She almost laughed. She had been a measurer for eleven winters, and her own stride had gone false under her. Two toes lay dead in her left boot. The right foot had swelled until the laces hurt. Kunal had cut the crutch at the lake huts from a juniper limb, in the grey light, with his knife in his bad hand and Gauri holding the other end. He had padded the fork with his own shirt hem. It dug at her armpit with every step, and she was grateful for it.

She went on. The rope between them lay eight spans slack along the rock and tugged her wrist whenever he lagged.

Behind them, the hollow was a day's grief away. The black water had turned out to be a ledge. With a full lamp and Ila's axe haft sounding ahead, they had found the blue cairn-stones running knee-deep along the left wall, a path half-drowned and invisible by half a lamp's light. At its end the water poured down a six-foot step into a crack, and the way went down with it. Kunal had lowered her on the rope, then climbed after her with his dead fingers hooked in the rock. Neither of them had spoken. After Dhanu there did not seem to be much left to say.

"Fifty," said Kunal. "Fifty-one. The roof's coming down. Duck."

She ducked. Her hair caught on stone. The crawl narrowed until she was on her knees, dragging the crutch, with Kunal's breath loud behind her. For a stretch there was nothing but rock and the cold taste of it.

Then her left hand, reaching, closed on nothing.

"Wait."

"What is it?"

"The wall's gone."

She felt for the edge. It was the end of the crawl. The air in front of her was wider and drier. It carried a smell she knew from Taravan's archive, stone and old dust, the smell of a thing kept a long time and not looked at.

And low down, at the edge of seeing, something was blue.

She thought at first she had imagined it. Kunal's hand found her shoulder. Then, as her eyes learned how little they were being asked to see, the colour came up out of the dark as a stain comes up through paper. Cairn-light. Faint, even, and far dimmer than the stones in the cleft. It lay in a ring on the floor, and she understood that she was looking at a wall of stacked stones, curved, perhaps twice her height at the crown, with no sky above it.

"Light the lamp," she said. "Not yet. Let me look first."

She crawled out onto level floor and stood, her crutch scraping, and let the blue show her what it would.

It was a hollow under a heap. That was the only way she could say it. The mountain had made a vault, and someone, long ago, had built flat stones up from the floor to meet the roof, so that the whole chamber was a cairn turned inside out. The blue came from between the stones, from the seams. It did not brighten when she looked at it.

"Under-Cairn," said Kunal, quietly, beside her. He had come out on his knees and stayed there. "I've said that word my whole life as if it were a place in a song."

"It's a place."

"Yes." He didn't get up. "It's a place."

* * *

He struck the lamp alight with the flint and the heel of his hand, and the chamber came up gold and small. Ila told him to turn the wick down. She wanted the oil, and she wanted to see how little she could make do with.

There was a door.

It stood at the far curve of the wall, a frame of three squared stones, two uprights and a lintel, set into the cairn-work like a gate into nothing. Behind it was only more dark. Across the lintel and down both uprights ran a carving, deep and clean, though the stone under it had gone the colour of old bone.

Three loops, nested one inside another.

Ila's breath went out of her.

"I know that," she said.

"It's a knot."

"It's *the* knot. Not any knot." She had hobbled to it before she knew she was moving. Her hand, in its glove, hovered over the groove and did not touch. "Meera drew it in the count. She showed me at the drafting table in Garh Neshar. A bundle in a dead juniper at the Rope-Hut, tied in this pattern, and beside her sketch she'd written *Same as cairn.* Asha's crew left it. Meera meant the Shilak Cairn. She couldn't have known it was the same as this."

Kunal said nothing. She could hear him thinking, and was oddly glad of it. She set the match in her mind, Asha's crew, Meera's sketch, this door, and put it away unanswered, the way she filed a bearing she could not yet close.

The carving did not stop at the knot. On the left upright, at the height of her shoulder, the loops ran into something else, and she lifted the lamp to it.

Two hands.

They were cut life-size, palm up, side by side, and one rope ran across both, looped once about each wrist, as it did at the Shilak Cairn and on the waystone and in every child's tale of the first crossing. She had never seen the cairn carving, only a dozen rubbings, all wrong. She had never seen it clean.

One hand was broad. It was a worker's hand, square across the knuckles and thick in the thumb. The other lay beside it, long and narrow, with fingers that tapered.

"Brothers in arms," said Kunal. His voice was odd. "That's the crown's line."

"Hold the lamp."

He took it. She dug in her coat for her pocket rule and the stub of pencil. She had no table, so she did what she had done at nineteen on an abandoned wall in Taravan. She laid her own bare hand over the narrow one, pale against the old stone, and marked with the pencil where her fingers ended.

"Longer than mine," she said. "By the nail's width. And thin through the palm. Compare."

He set his right hand, bandaged, against the broad one, with the lamp tilted. It fitted with room to spare.

"A man's," she said. "That one. The warden's. Saro." She lifted her own. "And I have long hands for a woman, Kunal. I get them from my mother. This is not a boy's hand. The wrist's wrong, the taper's wrong, the thumb sits too low. It's a woman's."

"Hemi."

"Hemi was a woman." It came out of her like a measurement, flat and certain. "Three hundred winters of *two brothers in arms,* and she's carved here in her own hand."

Neither of them moved. The hum of the stones was low. She could not have said if it was in her ears or in the rope.

Under the hands, in a single line, ran script. It was old and square-cut, with letters she had seen only in margins of route-songs.

"Can you read it?"

Kunal brought the lamp down. His lips moved.

"Some." He kept his eyes on the stone. "Pritam taught me the route-songs in this hand."

He stopped.

She watched it reach him. Pritam had drilled him in these letters as a boy, and called them songs. His grandfather, Udai, had stood in this chamber and read this line, perhaps aloud, with a warden beside him. The family had kept the alphabet alive for sixty winters and never said what it was for. Kunal's free hand went to the lamp's base and closed on it, as if the floor had tilted.

"Go on," she said, gently.

"It's the same alphabet," he said, after a moment, "with a thousand years of grandmothers' mistakes in it." He traced under the first word without touching, as she had. "'*Carried.*' That's plain. Then a word that's *till*, or *until*, one of the old forms. Then '*no.*'" He frowned. "Then the stone is worn. Something the shape of a hook, and a curl. Hands have been laid on this end more than any other part."

"Worn by what?"

"Fingers. Years of fingers." He lifted the lamp until the carved line glittered. "*Carried till no—* and then nothing I can say."

*Carried.* She thought of the Ladder's route-song, the old hill word for seen across by the hand. She thought of her grandfather's chronicle entry, *carried down at the thaw.* The same word. One song used it for the living and the post's book used it for the dead, and she had held both in her head since the Ladder without letting them mean the same man.

*Carried till no.*

"A promise," she said, almost to herself. "Not a death. A promise has a term. You carry someone till a thing comes. *Till no something.* What would it end at?"

"I don't know."

"Don't guess."

"I'm not." His hand had begun to shake, a little. He put the lamp on the floor to hide it. "I won't guess at that."

* * *

She took the rubbing with the paper from her field book, laid flat across the line and held with the heel of her glove. Her pencil leads snapped in the cold. Kunal had been carrying them against his chest since the Hollow Moon, and without being asked he took a fresh one out from inside his shirt, warm, and put it in her fingers. She said nothing. She went over the line with the side of the lead, slow, from the first letter to the worn hook. The words came up white out of the grey, and where the hook had been there was only a smear.

It was a poor rubbing at the end, and she knew it. She pressed harder. The paper tore a little. She stopped.

"Leave it," said Kunal. "We'll know the shape. We'll find someone who can read it."

"Who?"

"I don't know." He lifted the lamp again. "Come and see the rest. The roof is lower here."

There was more. The door had held her so fully that she hadn't seen what ran beside it, along the curve of the wall to the right. A column of scratched marks, from the height of her shoulder down to her knee.

She counted them, because counting was what her hands knew. Fourteen. Each had been cut by a different hand, in a different year, some deep and clean, some shallow and quick, as if the carver had been in a hurry or afraid. Most were bare strokes. A few had small signs beside them, a knot, a goat's head, a curl that might have been a wave. They went down the stone in the order of their ages. The lowest was a different sort of mark altogether.

It was a goat, cut neat and square. Beneath it, in the same careful letters as the door's line, was a name.

She had written it herself in the margin of her first field book, at nineteen, to see what it looked like.

*Harish.*

She stood in front of it a long time. The lamp made the letters swim. It was a stranger's hand, the cutting of a man who had been taught his letters properly and had not expected to use them like this. Her father had said, once, over the clock, that his own father used to sign his reports with a flourish on the H.

There was no flourish. The H was bare and straight.

"Ila," said Kunal.

"Don't." Her voice was steady, and she hated that. "I'm all right. I'm looking."

"I know you are."

Her glove touched the stone for the first time. It touched the goat, not the name. She had half expected warmth, or cold. It was only stone. She had come all this way to find it and it was only stone, and her grandfather had once stood where she stood and cut it into the wall with a knife.

"There's a shelf," said Kunal, behind her. "Here."

It was waist-high, a flat slab laid across two stacked piers, close beside the marks. It ran back into the dark of the vault. She turned to it with the lamp.

Gloves.

They had rotted. Wool and leather and a few scraps of felt, gone brown and black and the colour of earth, their shapes slumped but their order unmistakable. Each pair lay one glove folded over the other, palms together, the fingers set square to the edge of the slab. Then a gap, the width of a hand. Then another pair. Then another.

She counted them. She couldn't stop herself.

"Thirteen," she said.

"There are fourteen marks."

"Thirteen pairs." She walked the lamp along the slab to its end. Beside the last pair, level with Harish's mark on the wall, lay a clean place on the stone, a hand wide and empty. "Every payer folded his gloves here, as the story says. This is where his would be." She stared at the gap. "He came here, cut his mark, kept his gloves, and walked out alive. He folded them on a flat stone on the slope above the third bend, and he died there, and they found him at the thaw."

Kunal let out a slow breath. "Out alive."

"On his own legs. He came out of the mountain alive, and he died anyway." Her voice thinned. "*Carried.* The post's book says it for the dead. The hill word says it for the living. He was both."

The vault was quiet. Meltwater ticked on stone somewhere out of sight.

Kunal pinched the lamp out.

She almost cried out. The dark dropped on her like a coat. Only the cairn-light was left, faint and blue at the seams of the wall, hardly enough to show her where her own hand was.

"Oil," he said. "We have half a skin. I want us to see this by the only light it was ever meant to be seen by."

"That's not a reason."

"It's one reason." A breath. "Sit. Your foot."

He helped her down onto the cold floor, her back against the cairn-work, the crutch laid across her knees. He sat beside her, near enough that his shoulder pressed hers. The rope between their wrists lay slack and humming. She held still.

It was a peculiar thing to be so afraid and so unfrightened. She had known it on the ice, and now she knew it in the dark under the whole weight of the mountain, with a dead man's mark in front of her and a living one beside her.

"Ila."

"Yes."

"I love you."

She stayed still.

He said it like a distance, in the same level voice he had used on the Stair when he told her the stone was loose. And it came out of the dark where she could not see him, where she could not look at his hands.

That was the point, she understood slowly. He had put out the lamp. On the Mirror, at the Eye, even on the ice, there had been a place to look, a pair of fingers whitening or not. For a fortnight she had read this man by the colour of his hands, because it was the one honest instrument she had, and he knew it. He had taken the light, and with it the instrument.

But not every instrument. The room was a cairn. Its stones would ring if he lied, and the rope on her wrist was humming level even now. She could hold her breath and listen, and the stones would give her a verdict. She would be listening for one. That was what she would be doing, in the dark, to a man who had just said the plainest thing he owned.

*He's asking me to take it on his word.*

It was the most frightening thing anyone had ever done to her.

She put out her hand into the dark and found his.

Her gloves were on. She had left them on, deliberately, and his hands were wrapped, the burnt palms in strips of her own shirt, the dead tips of the right grey and wrong under the cloth. Wool over cloth over skin. Nothing could pass between them, not cold and not a lie. If he had been lying, she could not have felt it. She held his hands in her lap on top of the crutch and knew she was holding them blind.

"I've got my gloves on," she said. "You're wrapped to the wrist. Nothing could come across if it tried. I can't check, Kunal."

"I know."

"I could take a glove off. Unwind a strip." She meant it to come out firm. It came out less steady. "I'm not going to. It isn't to check. I'm holding them. That's all this is. I'm holding your hands."

His fingers closed on hers, as far as they could.

"I'm not counting," said Ila, before the stones could answer, and she did not listen for them.

He let out a breath. She felt it go through his whole arm.

They sat in the dark under the cairn, and the meltwater ticked, and the blue stayed even in its seams. She had no word yet for what she felt, and it would keep. She had a pencil in her coat and a rubbing with a torn end and a grandfather's name, and for the first time since the Hollow Moon she was not afraid of what came next.

Then the dark behind them changed.

A paleness showed at the mouth of the crawl, low down, the wrong colour, yellow and not blue. It moved. It brightened and slid along the wall of the passage they had come through, and she heard, far off and then not far at all, the scrape of a boot on stone.

Kunal's hands went rigid in hers.

Lamplight was coming up the crawl behind them, and somebody was carrying it.
