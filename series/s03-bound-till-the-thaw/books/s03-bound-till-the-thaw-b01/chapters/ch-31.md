# Chapter 31: The Winter Crossing Law

Nimmi came down from Ranu on the second grain cart the hill council let through, and she laid her kit on the guest-house table before she had unwound her shawl.

"Left hand," she said. "Ring finger and little finger. Grey to the root, as I told you at Ranu, and it has not improved by being ignored."

Ila had cleared the table and boiled the linen. She stood at the window with her measuring cord wound tight round one fist, as if she could survey her way out of the room. Rohan sat on a stool at Meera's right and took her right hand in both of his.

Her grip was already hard. "Don't talk," Meera said. "I'm going to count."

"Count what?"

"Everyone." She looked up at the ceiling beams. "One. Hakam's cousin, the winter I was nineteen. Two. His wife."

Nimmi bent over the left hand. The small bright knife moved. Rohan kept his eyes on Meera's face.

"Three. The tanner's boy." Her voice thinned and held. "Four."

By the ninth he was counting with her, saying only the numbers, half a beat behind. He had thought the count was a ledger. It was more like a rope she climbed, one hold after the next, and every number was a face. She needed no mark for any of them. Her knuckles ground his split ones together and he let them.

"Ninety-one," said Meera.

"Done," said Nimmi.

"I'm not stopping at ninety-one."

"Then count slower while I bind it. I have never lost a patient to arithmetic."

They went on, the two of them, up the long stair of strangers she had walked over the Saddle, while Nimmi wrapped the hand in clean linen and lamb fat. Somewhere past a hundred and forty Meera laughed, shakily, at a goatherd who had paid in cheese. At two hundred and six she stopped and breathed.

"And one," she said. She turned her head and looked at him. "Two hundred and seven."

He understood where he had been entered. He had no answer. His mouth opened and nothing came out of it.

"I'd have put you in anyway," said Meera. "I only wanted to watch you hear it."

"Head between your knees, Captain," said Nimmi, tying off. "Grey face, damp hands, a stare. I have seen it on fathers."

* * *

The law took six weeks to write, and when they finished it ran to eleven lines.

Hill council men came and went with their opinions and their tea. Kunal came twice, spoke with care, and left each time looking like a man who had given away less than he meant to and more than he expected. Nimmi stayed nine days before Ranu pulled her home, and she dictated the second clause as though listing symptoms. "Medicine passes. Not 'may pass.' Not 'passes on payment.' It passes, and the warden who stops it answers for the cough."

The first clause was Rohan's, and it cost him a morning. Licensed bound guides, crossing in winter, under a licence that carried two seals: the post's and the hill council's. He had held the only seal on that road for twelve winters. He cut it in half with his own pen and found the pen did not shake.

Ila corrected everything.

"A bound rope is not 'about twelve arm-spans,'" she said, striking a line. "Whose arm? I measured yours against the post door, and it runs a hair over the common. Write twelve spans of the guide's own arm and let the guide be the standard."

"The jogini refuse anything longer," Rohan said.

"Then somebody should measure the jogini." She laughed, loud and sudden, and a council man dropped his cup.

In the second week he set a parcel on the table beside the count. Meera unwrapped it with her right hand: a left glove of felted wool, lined with lambskin, cut for a thumb and three fingers.

"You measured me," she said.

"With a cord, while you slept. I should have asked." He kept his voice level. "The hand cannot tell you when it is cold. You check its colour every hour, the way other people look at the sky."

She drew it on. It fit. She flexed the three fingers and looked at the glove for longer than the glove deserved.

"Don't be kind in front of the council," she said.

"They are in the next room."

"Then be kind quietly."

He was. It took no effort at all.

The last clause he wrote on a grey afternoon with Kiran at the end of the table. Kiran had been told to rest and had asked, instead, to be in the room. It was the only thing he had asked for since the Fair, and he had not made a single joke. He sat wrapped in a blanket, his torn ear tucked under it, watching the pen.

Dilan's rope end lay by the ink. Rohan had put it there without deciding to.

*No person shall be hanged, nor held to blame at law, for a death upon a bound crossing.*

He wrote it in one stroke and sanded it. The scratch of the nib was very loud.

"You spelled 'crossing' right," said Kiran. His voice cracked on it. He pulled the blanket higher. "Good. It's good."

"It is for the next one," Rohan said. "Whoever it is."

Kiran nodded without looking up, and stayed where he was until the ink dried.

* * *

The news came in pieces, with the dispatch riders.

Varun lay in irons in the cellar of Garh Neshar and would go down to Taravan in chains when the passes opened, to answer in the queen's court. Pritam was held in his own grey seat, with the hill council signing for his grain stores. And the regent's general, said the assembly's finding, had been exiled across the border with his horse and one saddlebag.

"The paper says exiled," said Nimmi, who had not yet gone home. She had a cup of walnut tea and no patience. "The torches went down in my lane on the order of a man on a grey horse. I stood on my step. I watched him do it. Write what you like. Ranu will say otherwise, and it will go on saying it long after the paper rots."

Rohan thought of a sword belt laid on cold grass, and of a man who stood beside it with his hands empty. He wanted the name. He knew it was not his to ask for. He set down his pen, wrote nothing, and let the whisper stand.

* * *

On the last night, when the fair copy was done and sanded, Meera sat with it under the lamp and did not read it.

"It's paper," she said. "Good paper. Until her hand is on it, in spring, I'm still a name in the assembly's book with a hanging written beside it."

"The assembly has no gallows the queen has not lent it."

"Your wardens do."

"Yes." He did not look away from that. "A warden cannot pardon. Only the queen. But my writ is her own hand, and a warden may name his guide upon it for the crossing home."

She was quicker than he was. She always was. "The Closing Roll."

"You know it."

"I've known it since I was nineteen. A name on the Roll is a name that comes when it's called." She counted it on her good hand, one finger at a time. "For life. Winter service whenever summoned. And I can't say no to a warden."

"Mine, while I wear the grey. After me, whoever holds the post."

"That's the part I don't like."

"No," said Rohan. "I would think less of you if you did."

She was silent. The river ground under the window. He had spent his life being the one who said, *it is the law,* and he had never before offered someone the law and wished it were smaller.

"I'd be selling it to you," she said. "My freedom. To the man whose job was to take it."

"To the crown. I am one of its clerks." He tried the dry tone and it nearly worked. "I would not abuse the call."

"I know you wouldn't." She turned the fair copy so the lamp lit its first line. "I'll owe them winters. I'd give them anyway."

He had thought it would feel like a loss. It felt like watching her step off the bank onto ice she had tested herself.

"And the orders in your coat," she said. She did not make it a question.

"I will not hand you to anyone." The rope on his wrist hummed, low and level, and he felt her feel it. "How I make the paper say so, I have not finished. I will finish it. I give you that."

"Good," said Meera. "You can finish it at the cairn."

He gathered the pages to carry down to the clerk. "I will leave you to sleep."

"Stay."

He stopped with the papers against his chest.

She was not looking at the lamp or the law. She was looking at him, and her hand lay flat and still on the table.

"I've given all winter," she said. "Orders. The count. My hand. I've never in my life asked anyone for a thing that was only mine." Her fingers stayed warm; he could see them. "Stay. Tonight. I'm not finished asking after the thaw, but I'm starting with tonight."

He set the papers down. "Yes."

Ila had mended the latch at last. Meera crossed the room and shot the bolt, and the small iron sound told him more than the word had. She had left the door open once, to prove she could. This was the other thing, and it was hers.

He undid the new glove for her, one stall at a time. She watched his hands do it.

"Watch mine," she said, low, and drew his face down.

"Here," she told him against his mouth, plainly, as she gave orders on the Saddle. "Slower."

He did as she said. Being told was the best thing that had happened to him all winter. Her bandaged hand lay against his shoulder, careful and warm, and the lamp burned lower, and the river went on grinding under the window.

* * *

Afterwards she lay with her head under his chin and laughed, once, at nothing.

"What?"

"I want it again tomorrow." She said it to his collarbone. "I'm going to keep wanting things. I thought you should be warned."

"I am warned," said Rohan. "I will stay."

The rope tightened once against both their wrists, then eased, like a plucked string settling. A contract with the mountain. He had never made a promise that felt so little like a weight.

* * *

The thaw came early to the south slopes and late to the north, and by the time they left Garh Neshar the snow on the road had gone to grey porridge by noon.

Ila stayed behind with her cord and her unfinished map and a ridge she had not yet found a name for. She gripped her brother's shoulders at the gate and told him he walked a degree off true, and he told her he knew.

They crossed back as lawful as paper could make them. The writ ran, in Rohan's hand and the queen's, to the Captain of the Shilak post and his named guide, Meera of Kothi Shilak, and no one at the Neshar post asked a question after the first look at the seal. Meera carried the writ case in her coat. She said it weighed more than the redbark had.

They passed the Rope-Hut on the second morning. The nail above the door stood bare. Nobody had hung a new sprig there, and Rohan knew whose hands had used up the old one. The felled tree below it had put out green at the tips. Neither of them went in. He was glad to have seen it from outside.

They travelled in the dark hours and the early light, when the crust held. By day the whole mountain talked: water running under the snow, ice settling, the slow wet crack of cornices letting go somewhere out of sight. The winter line was fainter in the thaw, a pale blue thread in the grey, but it held. Rohan walked behind her and uphill, as she had taught him, and anchored the rope. She never once looked back to see if he was there.

On the fourth morning they came down off the Saddle to the waystone. Meera stopped. Three small stones sat on the shelf where she had left them for Jassi. She added a fourth, set it carefully, and stood a moment with her good hand flat on the pillar.

"For the goats," she said. "He'd say I'd forgotten the goats."

That afternoon they topped the last ridge, and below them lay Kothi Shilak, a tall old salt house with its goat pen and its thin smoke, and beyond it the small grey ring of the cairn. Goat bells carried up the slope, faint and wrong-pitched and entirely alive. Snow lay along the cairn's north side, heavy and white. On the south side, where the sun reached, it had begun to darken.

Meera stood very still. He knew she was counting. He did not need to ask what.

"Two nights," she said. "Maybe three."

"Yes."

The rope hung loose between them, eight spans of red wool gone soft with use. It had held him on a gully wall and in a lockhouse. It had told her every truth he had ever said. After the first drip at the cairn it would be only a rope.

He did not want it to end. He did not want it to hold them against their will, either. He saw, with the cold clarity of the pass, that he wanted both and had no right to either, and that the choice lay with the woman beside him and the mountain, not with him.

Meera put her mittened hand over his. "Come on, Rohan," she said. "Let's go down and find out how far we walk when nobody's measuring."
