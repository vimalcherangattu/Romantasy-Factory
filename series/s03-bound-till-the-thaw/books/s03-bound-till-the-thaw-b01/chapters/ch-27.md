# Chapter 27: Torches

Meera's counting stopped somewhere past four hundred.

Rohan had been listening to it for an hour. It was a whisper at the edge of hearing, the numbers falling further apart, like water from a failing roof. Four hundred and eleven. Then a long gap. He counted ten of his own and nothing came.

"Guide."

"Mm." It was the sound of someone answering from the bottom of a well.

He got up. The door was four paces off, inside the chain, and he went to it with the iron dragging at his belt and struck the planks with the flat of both cuffed hands.

"Sentry."

"What." The wet cough, thick and sour.

"The woman is dying of cold. Your lieutenant wants me breathing, and I am chained to her." All of it was true, and he said it in the voice he used for burial rolls. "Give me my coat and the brown pattu. If you're asked why, say a captain of wardens gave you an order and you chose to hear it."

A silence on the other side. A swallow from a flask. Then the bar scraped, the door opened a hand's width, and a bundle struck him in the chest. The door shut. The bar fell.

His coat. The borrowed pattu with its hole in the hem. And in the right pocket, where he had left them three days ago, his gloves.

He put them on with the cuffs in the way, working the leather over the iron one finger at a time. Then he went back across the oats and knelt behind her.

* * *

She was sitting against the wall with her chin on her chest and her good hand clamped over the bad one. He did not touch either.

"I am going to move you," he said. "I will not touch your skin. If I do by accident, say so and I will stop."

No answer. He took that for leave.

He laid the coat over her shoulders and drew the brown pattu across it. He tucked the folds under her knees, then slid in behind her, his back to the stone, and pulled her gently between his knees until she lay along his chest with her head under his chin. Layer on layer, wool and leather and his own body. His gloved hands closed over the front of the coat, and he kept his mouth well off the bare nape of her neck.

*Skin is the door,* he thought. *Keep the door shut.*

The iron band was the worst of it. It lay around her left forearm over a hand she could no longer feel, and the metal had gone colder than the night. He tore a strip from the hem of the pattu, where the hole already was, and packed it between the iron and her sleeve with the tips of two gloved fingers. The band sat looser. She made a small noise, not a word.

Fire did not warm lie-cold. He knew that as well as he knew the Closing Law. Only time or a hand could draw it off. But everything around the lie-cold could be kept away from her: the draught under the door, the stone at her back, the plain cold that would finish the work the rope had begun. If he kept that out, the rope's share might stay a share. It might not kill her.

It was the only arithmetic he had.

"I will talk," he said. "You need not answer. You need only stay on this side of my voice."

For a long time there was only her breath. It was shallow, and shallower than it had been.

Then, thin and almost cross: "Talk about what?"

*She asked.* He closed his eyes. "True things. They cost nothing tonight."

* * *

"Dilan laughed like a goat sneezing," said Rohan. "It came before the joke did. He'd be laughing at a sentence he hadn't finished, and then he'd have to apologise to us for ruining it. He laughed in the middle of the oath at the post, in front of the old captain. I gave him a week of latrine duty. He sang the whole week."

A small movement against his chest. He could not tell if it was a shiver.

"That hymn. You've heard me say. He sang it every watch, because he thought it was a love song. He could not carry a tune in a bucket. It went up where it should have gone down and the men used to cover their ears. And he'd look hurt, and then do it again." He stopped. His throat had closed on the last word and he waited for it to open. "I miss it. I did not know that I would. I expected to miss the boy. I did not think I would miss the noise."

Her right hand moved inside the pattu and found his gloved one, and held on to the leather. He let it.

"Ila measures," he said, after a while. "My sister. When she was seven she paced our house in her bare feet and told my father the kitchen was a hand shorter than the builder promised. She was right. He never forgave her or the builder. If she were in this room she'd have your chain at two spans and four fingers, and she'd say so aloud and laugh so loudly the sentries would think it was at their expense."

"Which it would be," said Meera. It was a thread of a voice, and it was entirely hers.

"Which it would be." He felt his mouth move. "She names the mountains for people she likes. There is a ridge on the survey sheet she calls Tamar's Shoulder, after a cook. I have asked what the cook did. She says, 'Fed me.'"

He told her about the old post room before it had a stove. He told her what his father said about the rope end, the one time he saw it: *Burn it, or carry it, but don't turn it into a kind of priest.* He told her he had carried it. He did not say *and now I have lost the man who stood at the other end of it,* because he did not know yet if that was true.

"My grandfather," he said, "went out in a storm to look to the cairns, for a party that was lost. They found him at the thaw. My father told it as a kind of prayer, and I never asked what it was like to find him." He breathed. "But he told me one thing once, when he'd been drinking. They found my grandfather's gloves lying on a flat stone beside him, one on the other, very neat. I've never worked out who he was tidying them for."

Silence. Then he heard it at the very bottom of the quiet, the way you hear a thread in a cloth: her breathing slowing.

"No," he said. "Meera. Stay."

He did the one thing he had not done since the Saddle. He hummed.

It was Dilan's hymn, and it came out badly. It rose where it should have fallen and cracked on the high note. He had no voice. He had never had one. But he knew the tune the way he knew his own boot size, and he went through the verse that began *keep me till the morning, love,* and into the next, flat, and getting flatter.

"That's," said Meera, against his collar, "flat."

"I know."

"It's a hymn." A pause, and a breath. "You can't help it. It's not even... it's not a love song, Rohan."

*Rohan.*

"He didn't know that," said Rohan, and went on humming.

* * *

It began in the ends of his fingers.

A pinch first, as when you carry cold iron too long. It was inside the gloves, in the pads of all ten fingers, a tightness that spoke of blood being asked to go somewhere it didn't want to go. He flexed them. The tips had a dull, wooden quality.

He had told no lie. He had touched no skin. This was neither of the two doors that the cold used.

He went through the third.

*I will not let you fall on this mountain.*

He had said it in the Rope-Hut on a night of storm, with her mouth still warm against his, and the rope had hummed low and level under it. A promise made while bound. If it broke, it would strike him as a great lie struck. And Meera was slipping, and the slipping was a fall, and the rope, which had no opinion on how far Ranu stood from the mountain, was beginning to count.

*The spirits' jurisdiction is wider than I'd like,* he thought, dry as dust. It was the nearest he came to a joke that night.

He could end it. He had only to shuck a glove and lay his bare hand on her wrist. The cold would run up his arm, and he would know her lie in one breath, and its size, and the whole thing would be borne and ended.

And he would know. Not the truth, but that the truth was large.

He sat with his hands knotted over her coat and his fingertips going white in the leather, and he did not take off the glove.

"Four hundred and twelve," said Meera, very faintly. Then, a long time after: "Thirteen."

"Fourteen," said Rohan.

* * *

The first light came through the slit window at some hour past midnight, and it was orange.

He had not meant to look. The shutter was a ruin of warped slats that let a stripe of the lane through, and he held very still, because she had gone limp against him again and he did not want to wake the thing in her that was holding on.

Torches.

They came down the lane in a slow file, soot and sparks, and spread along the marked houses like a string of beads being laid out. A man to each door. A man at each corner. He counted forty before he ran out of the stripe to count them in. Behind them came a handcart with straw on it, and a second, and men with their arms full of bundles, which they stacked against the planks, up to the sill, up to the lintel where the ash handprints showed pale.

Straw against the wall. Forty torches held steady. The red-moustached captain at the front with a paper in one hand, waiting for the hour.

Behind the walls, a child coughed. A woman said something low. Rohan could not hear the words and did not need to.

Seven houses. Forty-one sick. The ones who tended them. A boy named Kiran, on a pallet by a plank wall.

*Thirty-two new graves,* he thought, *and I signed the road shut that put them there.* He had not said it aloud yet. He had meant to say it, before the assembly, with the speech in his coat. It was a coat he was now sitting in, in a grain store, with a woman dying in his arms and a lane of torches, and nobody on earth to say it to.

In the middle of the lane, outside the third marked door, Nimmi stood.

She had come out with no lamp and no shawl. She stood on her own step with her arms at her sides, beside the lintel with its fresh grey hand, and she did not plead and she did not run. She looked at the line of torches the way she looked at a patient who would not be saved. She was counting them.

"Don't," he said, softly, to no one. "Go in."

She did not go in.

* * *

The horse came down from the upper road at a walk.

It was a tall grey, not hurried. Its rider wore a plain cloak the colour of old slate, with no bell stitched on the shoulder, and he sat the saddle like a man long used to it. He rode into the circle of torches and stopped between the captain and the first house. He had a grey beard cut close, and the hard, tired face of a man who has done a great many things that someone else wrote down.

"Captain," he said.

His voice was low. It carried the way a good officer's does, without being raised, across the whole frozen lane.

"Sir." The red moustache had straightened. "The order's under the regent's own hand, sir. Burn the cough. I have it here."

"I know whose hand." The grey beard did not look at the paper. He looked at the houses. He looked for a long time at the straw, and the lintels, and the small shape of a woman standing very still on a step. "How many are in them?"

"The sick, sir. And whoever's nursing."

"Children."

"Some, sir."

"Yes," said the rider. He spoke the single word as though he were setting it down on a table. Then, with no change at all: "Lower the torches."

A shuffle. The captain's mouth opened. "Sir, the order—"

"I will answer for the order. Lower them."

For a breath no one moved. Rohan had seen that breath at the edge of a hanging, the moment before a man steps up or does not.

The nearest soldier put his torch to the snow.

It hissed. Then the next, and the next, down the long file, a ragged hiss of fire going out, until the lane was full of grey smoke and the smell of wet pitch. Somewhere a man coughed. Somewhere another began dragging straw back into the carts, with the slow, grateful, ashamed motion of a man putting down a load he was glad not to have had to light.

The rider stayed on his horse. He did not look at the captain again. He looked once, directly, at the woman on the step. And Nimmi looked back at him with a long, level, memorizing look, as if she were getting a face by heart.

He turned the grey and walked it back up the lane, into the dark.

Nimmi watched the place where he had gone until the smoke covered it. Then she turned and went in, and the door closed.

* * *

Rohan found that his hands had closed hard on the coat.

It was not a pardon. It was a night. At dawn the captain might send for a different officer, or the paper might be carried to someone with a harder face, and the straw would come back out of the carts. He knew that. A stranger had spoken one word and a lane had put its fire out, and Rohan did not know his name.

*A man obeyed his conscience in the regent's grey,* he thought. *Here, in this valley. On this night.* It was a thing he had not thought was possible, or had not wanted to believe, and it undid something in his chest that he had not known was tied.

He had spent twelve years believing that if he bent the law once, nothing would hold. He had just watched a man bend a sealed order, and forty torches had held, and had gone out.

Meera lay against him with her breath a ribbon. The orange was gone from the slats. The lane was a smudge of smoke under a lid of stars.

He bent his head until his mouth was a finger's width from the wool of her hood, and not a hair nearer.

"Live, Guide," said Rohan. "I won't ask you anything else about that rope."

The rope on his wrist hummed. Low, and level, and steady as a held note.

He felt it all the way up his arm. He had made another contract with the mountain, and this one he knew he could keep. It cost him a man's name and a promise made over a rope end before eleven wardens. He felt his thumb find the clean-cut wool inside his coat and rest on it. He did not draw it out.

"Rohan," said Meera, from very far down.

"I'm here."

She said nothing more. But her good hand tightened on his glove, and the numbers came again, one by one, as faint as breath on glass, and by the time the stars paled behind the slats his fingertips had begun to ache with returning blood.

* * *

The blue came before dawn.

He had not slept. He heard the sentry's cough stop first, which was wrong, and then he heard nothing at all from the other side of the door. No boots. No flask.

A key slid into the bar's iron lock.

Rohan eased Meera against the wall, tucking the coat under her chin, and rose with the chain dragging at his belt. The bar lifted. The door swung out on the blue of the hour before the light, and the cold came in around the shape standing in it.

Asha. Onion-ochre canvas, her hair lank with the night, her face drawn and pale. A heavy iron key in one hand and a long knife in the other, the blade held low and loose.

She looked at Rohan. She looked past him at the shape in the corner, wrapped in a coat, and the sound of its breathing. Her eyes did not soften. They narrowed, as if she were adding up a column she did not want to add.

Behind him, he heard Meera's boot scrape the oats. She was trying to stand.

"Don't," said Asha, "do that, Guide. Not yet." She did not step inside. She held the key where Rohan could see it, and she held the knife where he could see that as well.

"Price first."
