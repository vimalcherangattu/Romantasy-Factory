# Chapter 24: Cut-Corner Silver

The wall ran along the bottom of the grove, dry-stone and chest high, and the red rope ran over it.

Meera walked until the line went taut at her left wrist, then took one more step so the wool bit. Eight arm-spans. On the far side of the stones Rohan sat in the snow with his back against them. She could not see him. She could feel him on the other end, still as a post.

Ahead, the walnut trees stood black under a rising moon. Beneath the largest sat a man on a folded blanket, as if he had come to watch a fair. A pale wool scarf covered his face from the nose down, and a hood hid the rest.

"Guide of Shilak," he said. "You came alone."

"I came with a wall. Don't ask what's behind it."

"Should I?"

His voice was low and unhurried, a voice taught in a good house. She stopped where the rope allowed, a long stride short of him, and kept her hands out of her pockets so he could see them.

"You pay the lower lane for medicine," she said. "For two winters. Why?"

"Why does anyone pay for medicine?"

"That's not an answer."

"Would you trust it if it were?"

She almost smiled. She had dealt with Taravan factors who talked in circles, but they circled toward money. This man circled toward nothing, and he did it politely, which was worse.

"Nine packets of redbark crossed Shilak in my pack," she said. "They're in Ranu. You'll want to know that."

"Am I owed them?"

"No. They were never for sale."

"Then what do you call it, when a man pays for what he was not sold?"

A light was bobbing in the trees behind her.

She turned, hand dropping toward the knife she did not wear. A clay lamp swung up through the walnut trunks at the height of her hip, held in two small mittens, and under it came a face she knew. Nimmi's grandson. Six years old, in his grandmother's second shawl, with the fringe dragging in the snow.

"Pinku," she whispered. "Go back."

"You went out," said Pinku. "Everybody said stay. Nobody said why."

The masked man was already on his feet. He did not hurry, and he did not loom. He folded down onto his heels in the snow so his hood sat below the boy's eyes.

"Does your grandmother know where you are?"

"She said stay."

"And you are a person of great independence." He took the lamp, gently, and held it where the wind would not reach the flame. "Then walk back in my footsteps, so the snow keeps your secret. Can you do that?"

Pinku considered. "Are you a robber?"

"Do I look like a robber?"

"You look like a scarf."

The man's shoulders shook. He put something in the boy's mitten, and when the fingers opened it was a walnut, whole and dark, with a ribbon of red thread knotted round the shell. "For the long walk. Open it when you are inside."

The boy went. The lamp's glow dwindled down the trees, and the buyer watched it all the way, his head turned so Meera saw only the line of his hood.

She had meant to dislike him. She put that down for later.

"Well," he said, rising. "Your silver."

He took a purse from his coat and tipped it into her glove. The coins were thin squares, heavier than lowland bits, and each had one corner clipped off clean, as though a mouse had bitten it. She turned one in the moonlight. The cut was neat. It had been made with a tool, by someone who made it forty times in the same place.

"What's wrong with them?"

"What would be wrong with silver?"

"Nothing is wrong with silver. Something is wrong with this." She counted. Nineteen. She closed her fist on them and, from habit, fixed the cut in her mind as she would fix the shape of a face. "I'll write it in the count. I write everything."

"Then write that it spends."

Next he lifted a canvas sack from behind the tree and set it between them. It was heavy and square, bound with tarred cord. On the cloth, inked in black, was a bell: a small brass assembly bell, the seal of a house with a brass bell in its hall.

She did not touch it yet.

"That is the regent's own," she said.

"Is it?"

"It's his store mark. The grain sacks in Ranu carry the same." Her throat had gone tight. "That's his redbark. From his cellar."

"Does a cellar care whom it feeds?" He pushed the sack an inch toward her with his boot. "Twenty-three go without in that village. I would rather they did not. There is enough here to finish them and some over, if your healer does not stretch it into water."

The wall behind her was silent. She could feel Rohan listening to nothing, doing exactly as he had sworn. It was worse than if he had spoken.

"There's more," said the masked man, and the lightness went out of his voice for the first time. "You won't like it."

"I don't like any of this."

"The captain's lieutenant. A tall man, handsome, polite about regulations. Did you think the mountain kept him?"

The cold went through her faster than the wind. "Varun."

"He crossed the Saddle eleven days after you, bound at the Shilak Cairn to a salt-road man who would speak his own name for the price. They came down through Garh Neshar. He sleeps in the regent's guest wing." The wool of the scarf moved with his breath. "A woman called Asha rides with him. The regent has put a warrant in his hand. It names a seal-thief and a traitor, and it names a captain of wardens. They leave Garh Neshar tonight with twenty men. They will reach Ranu at dawn."

She heard the number and could not make it mean anything. Then it did. The drift at the gully bend had turned the carter back, but it would not turn twenty men with shovels and a warrant. The lower road was reopening behind it, dug out for the crown's riders by the regent's own hands.

"Dawn," she said.

"Dawn."

"How do you know?"

"Do you think a regent's hall has thin walls?"

"Do you always do this?" she snapped. "Everything I ask, a question back. People are dying in that house."

"Yes," he said quietly. "That is why I came myself."

It was not an answer, and it silenced her better than one.

"Your name," she said.

He went still.

"I want it. I'll carry your sack into a sick village. I'll spend your silver. I'd like to put a name in the count beside it."

"Why does a smuggler want names?" He tilted his head. "Names are what the pegs in the deodar at your gate are for. You know that better than any woman living."

She did. Three hangings in a gate full of pegs, and every one begun with a name.

"A debt is a reason," she said.

"Is it?"

"Not for you, apparently."

"Then keep the debt," said the masked man. "It's easier to carry than a name." He bowed, the short bow of a man who had been taught it as a boy and had never found a way to stop. "Walk quickly, Guide. Mind your wall."

He was gone through the trunks before she had finished lifting the sack. She stood with nineteen clipped squares in her glove and a regent's bell on the cloth. Her left hand was pressed flat to her coat, over the brass badge she had lifted from under Kiran's pillow while he slept, and the hand was cold with plain night air and nothing else.

* * *

Rohan stood when she came over the wall. He had stood before she cleared it, so the rope would not drag her, and his hand was out to take the sack.

He did not ask.

She watched him not ask. It showed in his mouth, in how he held his weight, in how he turned the sack over and saw the bell and read it, and his eyes came up with the question plain in them and his lips shut around it. The promise lay on him like a coat he had chosen.

"You said you wouldn't ask," said Meera.

"I did."

"I'm going to tell you anyway." Her voice was steadier than she was. "That's allowed. It's the only part I get to choose."

She told it plainly, and in order. The questions, the silver, the bell. When she reached Varun, his face did not move. It was only the hand on the sack that tightened until the cloth crackled.

"Alive," he said.

"Twenty men. Dawn."

"Then the slide did not keep him." Rohan said it as a man reads a figure from a roll, flat and low. "I knew it in the Rope-Hut. I think I wanted it to be a thing I only knew in the hut."

"We could be gone in an hour." She had already counted the road. "Not the lower lane. There's a goat track over the shoulder above the walnut terraces, high and bad, and it comes out well away from the road they're digging open. Garh Neshar's a day and a half with you on foot. If we're off the slope before their horns, we've the long start—"

"And Kiran?"

She stopped.

"You mean to leave him behind," Rohan said. His voice was very gentle. "Under that warrant, he is a smuggler in a marked house, and he cannot stand at the woodpile without the wall to hold. He is too weak to flee. You know it. Twenty men will ride whichever road we take, Meera. They will not need to find us. They will find him."

"The soldiers will mark every door."

"Then we will do what is possible before they come." He set the sack down on the wall. "Nimmi can brew six cups to a pot. Four pots before light. Between us, we can carry them."

She looked at him. He had weighed it already, she saw, in the minutes it had taken her to climb a wall. He had counted, and the sums did not come out in his favour, and he had set them down anyway.

"If I stay, it may cost me the Fair," he said. "A warrant strips a writ. By noon I may be a prisoner with no paper to show a gate, and the meadow will go on without me. Hear the rest. The seal does not need me to reach the Fair. It needs hands that are not in irons. If it stays on my body, it is Varun's by noon and the mission dies with my arrest. If it is hidden, it outlives me."

Then he put two fingers to the nape of his coat.

The seam gave to a knife she had not known he kept. He worked the stitches loose without hurry, his good hand steady, and drew out a thing she had held once in a fever hut and had given back. Lapis, blue as a shut eye, the goat cut fine on its face. It hung from a cord tied off in the double hitch.

He held it out on his palm.

"Take it."

"Rohan—"

"Varun will search me. He knows exactly what to look for, because I told him." His mouth moved, bitterly. "I told him in the post room, and I thought it was loyalty. If it is on me, it is his. If it is on you, it may be hidden. Hide it where I can't lie about it."

"You can't lie to me."

"To anyone else I can, and will, and the rope will let me." He did not look away. "I would rather be searched honest. Do not tell me where. If I do not know, I cannot give it up, and I cannot be made to say."

She understood what he was putting in her hand. Not a stone. The whole Fair, every mile of it. He had carried it alone through an avalanche and a fever, and the one time she had held it, while he burned, she had sewn it back into his coat unasked. Now he gave it to the woman he had been ordered to deliver at the thaw, as simply as passing the salt.

She took it. It was cold from the night and warm from him.

The cord had come loose from long wearing. She slid the stone through her fingers, found the standing part, and tied it again, two turns round, a tuck, and the locking half-turn. She did not snug it as hard as she had in the hut, when she had been afraid and angry. She tied it as he did, with a little slack to let it breathe. Three children had learned it from him at midday on a kitchen bench.

He watched her hands.

"You've learned it," he said.

"I was shown by an expert."

His eyes creased, briefly. It did not stay.

Her left hand, the one that held the cord, pressed against her coat, and under the wool she felt the edge of the badge. She did not move it. The rope said nothing. He had not asked her a question, and nothing she had said was false.

But she knew exactly how much she was holding, and in whose hands.

"Now," said Rohan. He turned to the wall, set his forehead near the stones and shut his eyes. "I will count aloud. Go the whole length of the rope. I will not look."

"Rohan."

"One," he said. "Two."

She went. The rope paid out behind her, and he would feel its pull and know which way she had gone. A direction was not a place. At the end of the eight spans lay three choices, each ordinary and none safe: the house wall with its cracks, the goat shed with its loft, the woodpile with its hundred hiding gaps. She thought about who would turn out what first. A soldier checks a loft before he lifts logs. He checks a house before either. She thought about it the way she thought about bales on a salt-house floor.

"Forty," Rohan said behind her, to the wall, in the flat voice he used for burial rolls. "Forty-one."

She chose the woodpile behind the goat shed. She knelt at the third stack, where the split ends faced the wall, and pushed the lapis deep into the gap between two knotted deodar rounds. Then she pulled a curl of loose bark across the hole and set a billet of walnut over it. It took less time than she wanted and more than she could afford. When she came back along the rope her hands were empty and her face was set, and the count had reached a hundred and sixty.

He opened his eyes and did not look at her hands.

"Go and see to Kiran," Rohan said. "I will wake Nimmi and heat the water."

* * *

They brewed four pots before light.

Nimmi pinched the redbark out of the new sack with her lips pressed so thin they vanished, and said nothing about the bell on the cloth. Meera carried cups two at a time down the passage. Rohan carried the pots. By the third round the Dev boy's father had colour in his lips, and Kamla's girl had been spoken to sharply for sitting up.

Kiran drank his dose propped against the wall, his eyes on Rohan's back all the way across the room. Meera stood between them for a breath and then moved her body, so that the line ran through her.

"Didi," said Kiran, very low. "What's that noise?"

"Nothing yet."

"Is it a nothing that's coming?"

"Drink."

The sky in the high window had gone the grey of an old pot. Nimmi was chalking the last tick on the lintel when the sound came down the lane from the lower road, thin and far, rising through the snow-dull air.

A horn. Two notes, falling.

She knew it. She had heard it on the Saddle's lip, climbing out of the dark with a line of torches behind it. Rohan lifted his head at the same moment, and across the passage their eyes met, and neither of them said a word. The red rope between their wrists went taut and quiet.

More horns answered. Boots. The slap of harness on frozen air, and the bells on many shoulders, a thin brass jingle running up the lane like water.

Then a voice, carrying and cheerful, friendly as a neighbour's at a gate. It came from the lower road, and she had last heard it giving orders at a post-room table.

"Captain-ji! Captain-ji, are you in there? It's only me!"
