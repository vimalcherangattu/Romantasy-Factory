# Chapter 32: First Thaw

Eleven drips a minute fell from the snow cap on the top stone of the Shilak Cairn, and none of them counted.

Meera had been counting since dawn. The drips fell on the flat slab and spread into a dark coin, and the coin dried in the wind before the next one came. A drip was not meltwater running. The rule was exact. The bond ended when water first ran at the cairn, and the mountain had never been generous with its definitions.

"Twelve," she said. "Twelve a minute now."

"You said that a quarter hour ago," said Bindu.

"It was eleven a quarter hour ago."

Bindu hummed a knot-count under her breath and went on winding her spindle. She had come up from Kothi Shilak before light with half the village behind her. Hakam, the three-fingered old man, sat on a flat stone with his pattu over his knees. Roop and Chand stood tied wrist to wrist, their own bond waiting on the same first drop as hers. A dozen goatherds and their wives stood in the wet snow. They were pretending not to stare at the wardens, and the wardens were pretending not to stare at them.

Eight wardens in grey stood on the north side of the ring. Rohan stood with them and not among them, the red rope between his wrist and hers, and the rope was the only thing about the morning that was entirely clear.

Meera slid her good hand into her coat and brought out the bell.

It was the big bell, not the kid's. The small dented one lay under a mound at Ranu in the hands of a seven-year-old, where she had told it to lie. This was the brass drum Badami had worn through nine winters of crossings, green at the seams, with a tongue worn thin as a leaf. Bindu had fetched it from the goat pen that morning without being asked. "She's retired," Bindu had said. "She doesn't need it. He did."

Meera stepped to the cairn's inner face and hung the bell by its thong on a jut of stone beside the carved hands. The wind found it at once and gave it one note, flat and plain, and not the note the stones made.

"Jassi," she said. "Your sister has the orchard. I told her about the forty trees. I'll tell you now the goat is warm and fat and under your roof, which is the roof of a man who's looking after her." She stopped. "That's all. I counted you in the book the day you came to us, and I'm not taking you out."

Nobody spoke. Hakam took off his cap.

* * *

"Captain."

The senior warden did not say it loudly. He was a square man called Gopal, grey at the temples, who had walked behind Rohan for nine winters and who stood now at a correct distance with his hands at his sides.

"Gopal," said Rohan.

"I carry no orders of my own, sir. But I have read your writ." Gopal's eyes went to Meera and came back. "It says deliver the Salt Fox at the thaw. The ice is going. I'd be failing you if I let the hour come and said nothing."

Meera felt the rope go very still. She did not look at Rohan. She had told herself on the last ridge that she would not, and she had told herself in the night, and she had been right both times.

*Silence is free,* she thought. It had never felt so expensive.

The wardens stood in a half ring. Some looked at their boots. One young man looked at her left glove, with its empty stall, and then at the sky.

"You are not failing me," said Rohan. "You are doing your duty, and I want it done clearly, before the meltwater runs. So that nobody here, on either side, has to wonder what was said."

He turned so that he faced the whole cairn: the wardens, the crew, the goatherds with their children on their hips. His voice was the plain, slow one of the meadow. Meera had come to know it as the voice he used for bad news.

"The writ names the Salt Fox, and I know who she is. She is Meera of Kothi Shilak. She wrote her own name in the assembly's book before three hundred people, and the clerk wrote it again." The wind rang Badami's bell. "I am not arresting her. I am walking to Taravan beside her, with the law in her hand."

Meera had the fair copy in the writ case on her chest. The eleven lines weighed nothing and she felt each of them.

"The queen may say otherwise," said Gopal.

"She may. Then she will say it to my face, and I will stand where I stand now." Rohan's mouth moved, the dry corner of it. "If you wish to enter that in the post book, Gopal, please spell it right."

Gopal was silent for the length of a breath. Then he put his fist to his chest, an old salute, the post's own, and stepped back into the line. The young warden who had been looking at the sky was looking at Rohan.

"Sir," said Gopal. "It's entered."

Behind her Bindu began humming again, louder, and cut it off with a cough.

* * *

The drips came faster. Sixteen. Eighteen. She stopped being able to count them.

Then there was a small, ordinary sound at the foot of the cairn, like a spoon dragged through a cup. A thread of water the width of a barley stalk had found its way off the slab, down the south face and into the gap between two stones. It ran. It caught the light. It ran again.

The rope on her wrist went slack.

It did not fall. It just stopped being a thing that listened. The knot that had sat tight on her pulse since before midwinter was a knot and nothing more, a bit of red wool she had bought from a woman in Taravan.

She heard Rohan breathe in. She did not hear the stones.

"Well," Meera said. "Count that."

She untied her wrist. Her fingers did it without being told, the old flat reef with its half hitch. She laid the rope in Bindu's hands and turned and began to walk.

She counted aloud, because it was how she did things. "One. Two." Past the end of the eight spans, where the rope would once have drawn her up like a leashed goat. Her stride did not change. "Nine. Ten." The cold that had waited at that distance all winter, the cold she had felt for a mitten at fifteen, never came. The wind blew and it was only wind. Her own breath smoked and it was only breath.

She went on to the goat pen gate, and the creaking of it told her she had gone far enough. Twelve spans, fourteen, she did not know. She had never been this far from him by choice.

For a moment she stood there, the whole of the Shilak below her and the road to Taravan going away in mud, and she could walk on. Nothing held her. She could have gone down to the lowland and been nobody.

She turned around.

He had not moved. That was the first thing she saw: he stood exactly where she left him with his hands open, as he had stood on the meadow. He had not taken a step after her, and she knew what it had cost him. His fingertips were the colour of living skin.

She walked back. Every step of it she chose.

"Fifteen," she said, when she reached him. "I stopped counting after the gate. It's very odd."

"I counted," said Rohan. His voice was not steady. "Sixteen and a half."

"You never took your eyes off me."

"No."

"That," said Meera, "is the last time you get to check."

* * *

Bindu had the new rope ready. She had spun it herself over the winter, in the evenings when the others slept, undyed cream wool with the lanolin still in it, and she held it out in both hands as if it might be refused.

"It's not red," she said, going pink. "There isn't any red in it. I thought you'd not want the same one."

"It's short," said Meera.

"It's two spans. Nobody needs more than that if they're standing close."

The goatherds laughed. It was the first laugh of the morning and it went round the cairn like a drink.

Meera took one end and held it up. Rohan took the other. For two months and more they had touched no rope that wasn't tying them to a cliff or to each other, and neither of them knew quite what to do with one when no one had made them.

"The mountain's done," she said. "Nobody's asking us. There's no distance, and no cold, and I can lie to you all I like."

"You cannot," said Rohan.

"I could. It'd be free." She tied the knot on his wrist, the warden's double hitch he had tied on the seal's cord, two turns round the standing part, a tuck, a locking half-turn. She did it snug. She did it the way he snugged it, not the way she had. "There. Yours."

He looked at it. Then he took the other end and tied hers: the flat reef with a half hitch, Bindu's knot, taught to a five-year-old with a rope round her fist. His fingers were not quite steady, and he did it better than he had ever done anything.

"Say it to the stones," said Hakam, from his rock. "It's usual."

"The stones have heard enough from us," said Meera. She looked at the man in front of her. "Say it to me."

"My name is Rohan," he said. "My road is the one you walk. I choose it."

"Meera. And mine is the one you walk." She felt the thin wool settle on her pulse, a rope that held because two hands held it. "I choose it. And it's Rohan, not Captain. I've been saying it in the dark since the Saddle and I'd like it said in the day."

"Rohan," she said again, to the wardens, the goatherds, Jassi's bell, the dripping slab. It sounded like her own.

Somebody cheered. Chand, it was, a big shy man, who went red as soon as he did it. The cheer broke, and half the village was suddenly laughing and crying and wanting to touch her sleeve. Bindu wept openly into the spindle. Rohan stood in the middle of it with the cream rope on his wrist, looking at his hand, and at her, and at his hand again.

"We're betrothed, I think," he said, as if he were checking a column.

"Yes. I've done the sum."

"Then I should say that I love you."

"You've said it." She held up her bare right hand, and the left in its felted glove. "Look at me, Rohan. Look at my hands. Nothing's watching them now. Nothing's checking. You'll have to take my word."

"I do," said Rohan.

The rope had never let them have that: belief with nothing to prove it.

* * *

The wind dropped.

She felt it before she heard it. Everyone did. The laughter thinned from the outside in, a wave going out of a room. Bindu's spindle stopped. Even Badami's bell stopped, hanging from its thong with the tongue held still, as if a hand had closed on it.

The stones began to ring.

It started low in the south face, where the water ran, and climbed. She knew that note. She had heard it a hundred times, the stones answering a lie inside the ring. This was the same note and yet not the same. Where it once stopped at a liar, it rose and kept rising, filling the cairn to its rim like water in a jar, and the snow on the top stone shivered into the air.

"Nobody lied," said Hakam, very quiet, and put his hand on his chest.

The cold came into her palms.

This was no lie-cold. She would have known that anywhere, the dead white climbing from her fingertips. It came to both hands at once, to the five of her right and the three of her left, and to the two that were gone. It was clean and sharp as frost laid on a window, and where it lay it wrote.

Nothing had ever spoken to her through her skin. She felt it as speech all the same. There were no words, and there were words. They sat in the hand like a stone held in it, too heavy to mistake.

*The bargain is owed.*

All around her people were gasping. A goatherd's child began to cry. One of the young wardens had dropped to a knee in the slush with both hands pressed against his coat.

*It was made at this stone. It was never paid.*

Rohan's hand closed on hers. His was shaking. She held it back.

*When the fourth winter ends, the pass closes. Forever. Unless someone pays.*

The ring held for a long moment. Then the note fell, stone by stone, and the cold left her palms and the wind came back across the slab as though it had been waiting at the door. Badami's bell gave one flat clank.

Nobody said anything. Meera looked at her hands. They were pink. They were only hands.

"I felt it," said Gopal. He was grey. "Every word. Did we all?"

"Every word," said Bindu, in a small voice, and the whole crowd made a low sound, an animal one, of people who had known winters all their lives and had never been told one would be the last.

"Who is to pay?" said Chand. "What bargain? Nobody told us of a bargain."

Meera thought of Kiran at the drafting table in Garh Neshar, wrapped in his blanket, watching Rohan's pen. He had not made a joke since the Fair. He had once said, half laughing, that he owed a life and supposed his was the coin. She had let it pass as a joke. Her stomach turned over now, cold with a fear no mountain had put there.

"The chronicle says two brothers crossed bound," said Rohan slowly. He had not let go of her. "And that the pass has stood open to the faithful ever since."

"Ever since," said Hakam, "isn't forever."

Meera said nothing. She was looking at the carving.

She had seen it all her life. It was on the inner face of the ring, worn by three hundred winters of weather: two hands, one rope. She had touched it as a girl, for luck, as every child of the Shilak did. She had never looked at how the hands were made.

They were not the same size. One was a broad hand, thick at the knuckle, a worker's. The other was longer, narrower, a hand that held a thing carefully. The rope between them was tied in no knot she knew. And at the foot of the carving, where she had always thought a chip had broken off the stone, the rope simply ended. Cut. Or untied. Or unpaid.

*Hands. Not names.* A warden and a smuggler, said the crews. Two brothers in arms, said the chronicle. The chronicle had lied about something, and she had counted enough ledgers to know when a total did not come out.

"Rohan." She did not turn her head. "How long does a pass like this have to stand open before someone writes a law to shut it?"

"Sixty winters." His voice was thin. "My father said the law was written for my grandfather."

"I know what your father said."

He was silent. The wind turned Badami's bell. She felt his hand tighten on hers, the cream rope pulling a little at both their wrists. Somewhere in his coat, she knew, lay a pair of gloves in a story, laid one on the other on a flat stone.

"Ila stayed in Neshar to finish her map," Rohan said at last, very low, as if he were saying a different thing and could hear himself doing it. "Every pass, every cairn she could reach. The queen will want it. She will want it very much."

Meera heard what he did not say. She had the habit of it now. *She will want it, and I do not yet know why.*

"Then we'd better get to Taravan," she said. "Before somebody else decides what it is we owe."

She kept her eyes on the carved hands, and the broken rope, and the end that went nowhere. Behind her the thaw ran on, and somewhere under the mountain, in the dark, it ran toward a place she had never been.
