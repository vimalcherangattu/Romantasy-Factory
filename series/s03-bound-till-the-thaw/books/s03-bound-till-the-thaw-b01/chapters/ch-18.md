# Chapter 18: Twelve Packets

"Didi," said Kiran. "You brought a warden?"

He lay on a pallet by Nimmi's hearth with a quilt up to his chin, and he was the colour of old ash. His lips were cracked. The frostbitten ear she had last seen two winters ago, curled and puckered like a dried apricot, stood out against a face that had lost its flesh. His eyes were the same. They went from her to the grey pattu on Rohan's shoulder and lit with something rotten and delighted.

"Don't talk," said Meera.

"You've been gone ten days and you bring me a warden. I'd have settled for apricots." The laugh turned into a cough that bent him double. She was on her knees with her arm under his shoulders before it finished. His ribs moved under her hand like a bird's.

"Don't," she said again, and heard that she was begging.

"He's tied to you." Kiran's eyes had found the red rope. His voice cracked, then steadied. "Of course he is. Of course you did it the hard way." He turned his face into her sleeve. "Didi. I kept them going. Nimmi will tell you. I kept them going."

"I know. Hush."

Behind her, Rohan stood in the doorway, close enough that the rope hung slack. He said nothing. He was good at that, she had learned. It was the one silence she had ever been glad of.

* * *

Nimmi did the counting at her own table, with a kettle going and her sleeves pushed back.

"Forty sick. Nine under ten. Fever, all of them. Cough, wet in most, dry in the old. Hira the weaver has the blue lip. Bholu's mother lost her voice on the third day, which is bad, and her daughter has the rash, which is worse. Kamla's two have been sleeping since morning, and I don't like sleep." She ticked each one off on a thumb, and she sounded exactly like a woman reading out the damages. "The Dev boy won't eat. The Dev boy's father won't stop eating. Your brother is the only one who jokes, and it's wearing him thin."

"Nimmi."

"Open the pack."

Meera unrolled the oilcloth on the table. Three rows of four, palm-sized bundles of waxed paper tied with thread. Twelve. She had counted them at every halt since Kothi Shilak, and she counted them again now, touching each one.

Nimmi weighed the first in her palm. She didn't smile.

"A course is a pinch in hot walnut tea, morning and night, three days. Less for the little ones. A packet is two courses, three if I'm mean." She looked at the half-opened wrapper at the corner, the scoop taken out for the captain's lungs, and said nothing about it. "Twelve less what the road ate. I make it eighteen courses."

"For forty."

"For forty." Nimmi folded her arms. "You can do the sum. I did it on the way here, every night, and it came out the same."

Eighteen. Meera heard the number settle in her chest like a swallowed stone. She had carried twelve packets over the Long Saddle with the regent's hunters behind her, and Jassi dead in the gully, and the whole mountain wanting her to fail. She had thought of the hundreds of nights she had counted them. She had never once thought of the other number.

"Then we choose," said Nimmi, "and you write it down. You're the one with the book."

* * *

They did it in the back room, where the loom stood under a sheet. Nimmi sat on the weaver's bench. Meera sat on an upturned crate with the count open on her knee and the pen she kept for names.

She had told Rohan at the door to wait in the front room.

"I'll keep the rope slack."

"Eight arm-spans," said Rohan. "I am aware of the length. I will be at the edge of it."

"You'll be at the edge of it with your back turned."

He looked at her. It was the long, level look he gave a map he did not like, and she held it. "Meera, the choosing—"

"Is mine." She heard how hard it came out. "Nimmi's and mine. It isn't a warden's business."

"I have no wish to make it my business." He said it quietly, and it was true, because she had seen no lamp-oil in his hands. "I will turn my back."

He turned his back. But the back room was windowless and the lamp had burned down to a stub, and when Nimmi cursed and fumbled with the flint, a shape came through the doorway and a hand held out a fresh wick, already lit. Rohan had not turned around. His arm was simply there, steady, holding the light at her shoulder.

"I said—"

"I heard you. I am not looking." He faced the front room and the lamp stayed where it was. "You cannot write by a stub. If I put it down, you will have to get up and light another, and then you will be away from the rope."

She did not answer. She dipped the pen.

"Hira," said Nimmi. "Blue lip. Sixty if he's a day. A course won't turn him."

"Then he doesn't get one."

It was the first name, and she wrote it without a pause, in the column on the right. She made her hand move across the paper and form every letter. *Hira, weaver, no course.* She did not look up. The lamp did not shake.

"Kamla's two. Both sleeping. Girl of five, boy of three."

"Sleeping since this morning?"

"Since dawn."

"Gone too deep," said Meera. "Both of them. No."

"Meera—"

"I know. Write it, I'll write it. It's the same rule." She wrote it, and under it, *Kamla's boy, Kamla's girl*, and the pen scratched. The rule was a plain one: the course had to catch someone still on the near side of the worst. A fever that could still be turned. Someone who could still swallow the tea.

They went down the list. Bholu's mother, yes. Her daughter with the rash, yes, because Nimmi said the rash meant it had days left and not hours. Old Lalli, who'd had the cough three winters running and would likely outlast them all, no. The Dev boy, yes. His father, no, and Nimmi laughed once, short, and didn't look away from the paper.

Fourteen. Fifteen. When the pen reached Sunni, Meera's hand stopped.

She had seen Sunni already. Everyone in the front room had been settled on blankets between the loom and the hearth, and the girl was seven, with plaits as thin as pencils and a fever that made her cheeks look painted. She had not been asleep. She had been watching Meera's coat. When Meera crouched to put a cup in her hands, she had asked, in a voice like a cracked cup, whether the bell in her pocket was for a goat.

"For an old one," Meera had said. "She's called Badami. She's retired."

"Can I ring it?"

She had taken out the small, dented bell and put it in the girl's hand, and Sunni had shaken it once, and laughed. It had turned into the cough. She'd put the bell on her chest and kept her fist over it.

"Sunni," said Nimmi now. "She's still talking. Still eating, a little. Her breath's wet but it's clear at the bottom."

"So she's stronger than the others."

"She's stronger than Bholu's mother. Which means she can wait on the second water." Nimmi rubbed her eyes. "I can brew six cups at a time on this hearth. It's three trips. The strongest ones have the longest roads to travel, and they'll bear a delay. Sunni could take till dusk."

"You're sure."

"No." Nimmi dropped her hands. "I'm sure of nothing. That's what this is."

Meera looked at the page. Seventeen names with a course. Seventeen without. Sunni's was the last one on the right-hand side, the eighteenth. She wrote it. *Sunni, seven. Third water, dusk.* The ink sat there, black and wet and exact, and the whole page shone in the lamplight like a field.

Behind her, the lamp had not moved. Rohan had not spoken once. She saw the hem of his coat at the corner of her eye, and the hand at the end of the light, bare and still, with a split across its knuckles from the stone fold.

He did not touch her shoulder. He did not say it was not her fault, or that she had chosen well. He did not say anything at all.

She held out the pen to nobody, set it down, and found that she was glad he was there.

* * *

The water was Rohan's idea. At least, it began as his idea, and it ended as a thing he did with his whole body, as if there was nothing in him but arms.

There were four wells in Ranu and forty-one sick, and the nearest well to Nimmi's house was a hundred paces down a frozen path. At noon he took two buckets from the porch before anyone had asked. He went down the path, filled them, and came up with water slopping, and carried them in.

"Where?"

"Kettles," said Nimmi, without looking up. "And the next house. And the one after. Go."

He went. He was a warden of the queen in a pattu with a goat on the collar, and the whole village watched him go, with faces in doorways and shutters and the cracks of doors. They saw the red rope running from his wrist to Meera's. They saw him slow at every step, to stay inside the length of it. And Meera went where Nimmi needed her, house to house with the tea, and the rope drew him after like a string on a toy.

He could not go further than eight arm-spans from her. So he followed her to Hira's, and stood at the edge of the room with the bucket while she crouched by the weaver's pallet and did not say anything, because there was nothing to say. He followed her to the Dev house and filled the kettle from his own bucket, and when the Dev boy's father, still eating and weeping into his bread, took hold of his sleeve and said *please, please*, he stood and let the man hold it. He did not tell him the law. He did not tell him he was sorry.

He followed her out of the house, and the next one, and the next.

"You could go to the well and be back," she said at the fifth door. "Two minutes. Within the length."

"I know the length." He set the bucket down. "I find I would rather not be eight arm-spans away."

"It's a practical rope. It won't mind."

"It is not the rope." He said it flatly, to the wall, and carried the bucket in.

At the sixth house, a woman stood in the doorway with her arms crossed and her face shut. She looked at Rohan, at the goat on his collar, and spat on the boards at his feet. It was a careful spit, not at him but beside him. Meera opened her mouth.

"It is a fair price," said Rohan. He picked up the bucket again. "Please tell her I am carrying water, and that I would carry it if she spat on me."

The woman's mouth twisted. She stood aside.

By mid-afternoon the first two waters had gone out, and Meera's boots were soaked to the ankle, and the count lay open in her coat with seventeen names ticked. She stood in the lane between the Dev house and the next, with her cold hands pressed to her mouth.

"That's one hundred and forty steps from the well," said Rohan, behind her. "I counted. You always count."

"Don't make me laugh."

"I was not trying to." He set down the buckets. "I was giving you something to count that was not the other thing."

She laughed anyway, or something close, a hard cracked sound. When she turned, his face was grey with the ache of his ribs and his hands were raw, and he was looking at her as if she were an ordinary person to whom ordinary things might be said.

"Third water," she said. "Dusk. It's nearly dusk."

* * *

Sunni took the tea in both hands, and Meera held the cup beneath her chin, so the steam rose into her face.

The girl was hotter than she had been at noon. Her breath came short, and her plaits had come loose. But she smiled, a small cracked smile, when she saw the bell was still in her fist.

"Is Badami in the barn?"

"In the barn. Eating the roof, probably."

"Mama says goats can't eat roofs."

"That's because she hasn't met Badami." Meera tipped the cup. The girl swallowed, and swallowed again, and coughed, and swallowed. The pinch of redbark had made the tea bitter and brown, and she wrinkled her nose over it. "It's horrible, isn't it?"

"It's *so* horrible," said Sunni.

"Then it's working. Drink the rest."

She drank the rest. She slept, with the bell tucked under her chin and her mother's hand in her hair, and Meera stayed beside her for the length of a hundred slow breaths. She counted them. Then Rohan's shadow shifted at the door, and she got up and went out into the dark.

* * *

Kiran's cough was lower now, the tea in him. Nimmi had let her give him his own course at the second water, and he had been sleeping, off and on, for most of the day. Now he was awake, on his side, watching the fire with half-lidded eyes.

"You're writing it down," he said.

"What?"

"The count. You've got your book out." He tried to grin. "Who did you pick?"

"I picked eighteen."

"And you hate it."

"I hate it." She put the notebook away. It was an effort, and her hand shook, and she saw him see it. "Turn over. You're lying on your lung."

She went to ease the pillow out from under his head, to turn it. Something was under it. A small, hard weight, wrapped in a bit of wool. When she lifted the corner it slid out onto the quilt.

It was a badge. Brass gone brown, a stamped disc the width of her palm, with an edge worn smooth and a green bloom in the lettering. She knew it at once, because she had seen a hundred like it on a hundred coats: a goat's head, the queen's mark. A warden's badge. Dull, tarnished, old. A tiny dent bit the rim.

She sat very still.

"Kiran."

His hand came out of the quilt and closed over it, fast, faster than a sick man's should. He held it against his chest.

"It's nothing."

"It's a warden's badge. Under your pillow."

"It's mine."

"It isn't yours." She kept her voice low. In the doorway, the shape of Rohan's shoulder showed at the very edge of the light, turned away, watching the lane. Eight arm-spans. He could see the room if he turned his head. "Whose is it?"

"Didi."

"Whose?"

"I'll tell you." His eyes were very bright, and the fever was in them, but there was something under it that was not fever. It was fear, plain and old. "I will. I swear. Not now. Ask me when I can breathe."

"Kiran, if a warden—"

"Not *now*." His voice cracked on the last word, and the cough took him. When it was done he was crying a little, the way the sick do, without sound. "Please. I'll tell you. I'm so tired."

She looked at the badge in his white fist. She looked at the doorway, and the back of the man she was tied to, who hunted the one who had stood at the far end of a cut rope, and who carried that rope in his coat.

She had a free silence. It was the only gift the mountain gave you, and she had never wanted it less.

She tucked the quilt over Kiran's hand and the brass in it, and she moved so that her body stood between the bed and the door.

"Sleep," she said.

"You're not going to tell him."

"I'm going to sit here." She sat. "That's all I'm doing."

* * *

The third water went round at dusk and the fourth at full dark, and then there was nothing left to do but wait.

Rohan slept sitting up against the wall, with the lamp turned low and his hands open on his knees, and she watched them for a time out of habit. They were warm. They were only tired.

At the grey edge of dawn Sunni's mother began to make a noise.

Meera was on her feet before she knew it. She crossed the room. The girl lay as she had been, one hand curled over the bell, her plaits damp and her face turned to the wall. There was no cough. The room had become much quieter.

"She was asleep," said her mother. "She was asleep. I put my hand on her chest and it was going, and I closed my eyes. I only closed my eyes."

Nimmi was there. Nimmi put two fingers under the little jaw and held them, and held them, and then took them away. She did not say anything.

Meera knelt. She knelt where she had knelt the evening before, and the cup was still on the floor, the dregs gone cold and brown. She had given her the redbark at dusk, and a day too late, or six hours, or the length of the road between one house and the next. It did not matter which. She was seven.

The pen was in her hand. She did not remember taking it out. She opened the count to the page, to the last name on the right, and drew the line through, a plain horizontal line, straight as a rule. Then she wrote the date beside it and the hour, in the same hand that had written every other name.

Someone stood behind her. The lamp was unlit now, and grey light came through the shutter. A hand came down, not onto her shoulder but onto the floor beside her, flat on the boards, an arm's length away and no nearer. He had not reached for her. He had only put his palm where she could see it.

She did not look up.

"I wrote her down eighteenth," she said. "It was my sum."

"I know," said Rohan.

He said nothing else. He stayed where he was, on one knee in the cold, a hand's breadth from the small curled shape and the bell, and let her do the work.
