# Chapter 18: Twelve Packets

"Didi," said Kiran. "You brought a warden?"

He lay on a pallet by Nimmi's hearth with a quilt up to his chin, and he was the colour of old ash. His lips were split and dry. A wool cap was pulled down over both ears, hiding the frostbitten one, and under it his face had lost its flesh. One fist was closed under the quilt, gripping something, and it did not let go.

His eyes had gone to Rohan's face first. Meera saw it. Something in Kiran flinched, a small hard tightening, as if he had been struck. Then his gaze dropped to the grey pattu on Rohan's shoulder, and the joke came up over the top of it.

"Don't talk," said Meera.

"Ten days gone, and you bring me a warden. I'd have settled for apricots." The laugh turned into a cough that bent him double. She was on her knees with her arm under his shoulders before it finished. His ribs moved under her hand like a bird's.

Any smuggler flinched at that coat. She put it down to that.

"Don't," she said again, and heard that she was begging.

"He's tied to you." Kiran's eyes had found the red rope. His voice broke, then steadied. "Of course he is. Of course you did it the hard way." He turned his face into her sleeve. "Didi. I kept them going. Nimmi will tell you."

"I know. Hush."

Behind her, Rohan stood in the doorway, close enough that the rope hung slack. He said nothing. It was the one silence she had ever been glad of.

* * *

Nimmi did the counting at her own table, with a kettle going and her sleeves pushed back.

"Forty-one sick. Nine under ten. When your brother wrote his note it was forty-one, and twenty-five have been buried since, and twenty-five more took to their beds. The number doesn't move. The people do." She ticked each case off on a thumb, like a woman reading out damages. "Fever, all of them. Cough, wet in most, dry in the old. Hira the weaver has the blue lip. Bholu's mother lost her voice on the third day, which is bad, and her daughter has the rash, which is worse. Kamla's two have been sleeping since morning, and I don't like sleep. The Dev boy won't eat. The Dev boy's father won't stop eating. Your brother is the only one who jokes, and it's wearing him thin."

"Nimmi."

"Open the pack."

Meera unrolled the oilcloth on the table. Three rows of four, palm-sized bundles of waxed paper tied with thread. She had counted them at every halt since Kothi Shilak. She had opened only one, for the captain's lungs. Now she saw the wax on the end bundles, and grey paste in the oilcloth beneath.

"The Teeth," she said. "I had the pack against the rock all that day."

Nimmi turned the ruined bundles over with a fingertip. Two had split along the fold. The third had taken the damp and set like clay. She scraped what she could into a saucer, weighed it in her palm, and shook her head.

"Not enough to dose a cat." She pushed them aside. "Nine sound. A course is a pinch in hot walnut tea, morning and night, three days. A packet is two courses. Eight whole ones, sixteen. The one you opened is short two half-pinches, so call it a course." She looked at the twist Rohan had set on the table, thumb-long, the silk-sieve ring pressed in its wax. He had taken it from a hunter's purse. "And this. Crown-grade, from the men who wanted you dead. Another course. Eighteen."

"Could you thin it? Two pinches to a cup, a smaller—"

"Thin it and it turns nobody. I tried it three winters ago. I buried the proof." Nimmi folded her arms. "Eighteen courses, forty-one sick. You can do the sum. I did it on the way here, every night, and it came out the same."

Eighteen. The number lodged in Meera's chest like a stone she had swallowed whole. She had carried twelve packets over the Long Saddle with the regent's hunters behind her and Jassi dead in the gully. She had counted them until the count was a prayer. She had never once thought of the other number.

"Then we choose," said Nimmi, "and you write it down. You're the one with the book."

* * *

They did it in the back room, where the loom stood under a sheet. Nimmi sat on the weaver's bench. Meera took an upturned crate, with the count open on her knee and the pen she kept for names.

At the door she had told Rohan to wait in the front room.

"Eight arm-spans," said Rohan. "I am aware of the length. I will be at the edge of it."

"At the edge of it with your back turned."

He gave her the long, level look he gave a map he did not like, and she held it. "Meera, the choosing—"

"Is mine." It came out harder than she meant. "Nimmi's and mine. It isn't a warden's business."

"I have no wish to make it my business." His fingertips stayed brown on the doorframe. "I will turn my back."

He turned. But the back room was windowless and the lamp had burned to a stub, and when Nimmi cursed and fumbled with the flint, an arm came through the doorway holding out a fresh wick, already lit. Rohan had not turned around. His hand was simply there, steady, holding the light at her shoulder.

"I said—"

"I heard you. I am not looking." He faced the front room, and the lamp stayed where it was. "You cannot write by a stub. If I put it down, you must get up and light another, and then you will be away from the rope."

She did not answer. She dipped the pen.

"Hira," said Nimmi. "Blue lip. Sixty if he's a day. A course won't turn him."

"Then he doesn't get one."

It was the first name, and she wrote it without a pause, in the left column. She made her hand form every letter. *Hira, weaver, no course.* The lamp did not shake.

"Kamla's two. Both sleeping. Girl of five, boy of three."

"Since dawn?"

"Since dawn."

"Gone too deep," said Meera. "Both. No."

"Meera—"

"I know. I'll write it. It's the same rule." She wrote *Kamla's boy, Kamla's girl*, and the pen scratched. The rule was plain: the course had to catch someone still on the near side of the worst, a fever that could be turned, a mouth that could still swallow the tea.

They went down the list. Bholu's mother, yes. Her daughter with the rash, yes, because Nimmi said the rash meant days left and not hours. Old Lalli, who'd had the cough three winters running and would likely outlast them all, no. The Dev boy, yes. His father, no, and Nimmi laughed once, short, and didn't look up from the paper.

Sixteen. Seventeen. When the pen reached Sunni, Meera's hand stopped.

She had seen Sunni already. The girl was seven, with plaits as thin as pencils and a fever that painted her cheeks. She had been watching Meera's coat. When Meera crouched to put a cup in her hands, she had asked, in a voice like dry reeds, whether the bell in her pocket was for a goat.

"For an old one," Meera had said. "She's called Badami. She's retired."

"Can I ring it?"

Meera had taken out the small, dented bell and put it in the girl's hand. Sunni had shaken it once and laughed, and the laugh had turned into the cough. She'd laid the bell on her chest and kept her fist over it.

"Sunni," said Nimmi now. "Still talking. Still eating, a little. Her breath's wet but clear at the bottom."

"So she's stronger than the others."

"Stronger than Bholu's mother. Which means she can wait for the third water." Nimmi rubbed her eyes. "I can brew six cups at a time on this hearth. That's three waves. Sunni could take till dusk."

"You're sure."

"No." Nimmi dropped her hands. "I'm sure of nothing. That's what this is."

Meera looked at the page. Eighteen with a course, Sunni the last of them. Twenty-three without. She wrote it. *Sunni, seven. Third water, dusk.* The ink sat there, black and wet and exact.

Behind her the lamp had not moved. At the corner of her eye she saw the hem of his coat, and the hand at the end of the light, bare and still, split across the knuckles from the stone fold. He did not touch her shoulder. He did not tell her she had chosen well.

She set the pen down and found she was glad he was there.

* * *

The water began as Rohan's idea, and ended as a thing he did with his whole body, as if there was nothing in him but arms.

There were four wells in Ranu. The nearest to Nimmi's house was a hundred paces down a frozen path. At noon he took two buckets from the porch before anyone had asked, got to the end of the porch, and felt the rope go tight.

"Eight arm-spans," said Meera. "I'm coming."

"The tea—"

"Will wait a quarter hour." She pulled on her gloves. "It won't like it."

They went down together, a warden of the queen in a pattu with a goat on the collar and a smuggler with a kettle-burned sleeve, and the whole village watched from doorways and shutters. They saw the red rope between the wrists. They saw him slow at every step to keep inside its length. He filled the buckets. She took one so he need not carry both on his ribs, and they climbed back.

A hundred paces down, a hundred up. She counted it, because she counted everything. Every trip was a quarter hour, and every quarter hour came off the time between houses. The tea went late to the Dev house. It went late to Bholu's mother. Nimmi said nothing, but she looked at the sun.

Rohan followed her to Hira's and stood at the edge of the room with the bucket while she crouched by the weaver's pallet. He followed her to the Dev house and filled the kettle, and when the Dev boy's father, still eating and weeping into his bread, took hold of his sleeve and said *please, please*, he stood and let the man hold it. He did not tell him the law. He did not tell him he was sorry.

"You don't have to haul every bucket," she said at the fifth door. "Get a boy to do it. You'd be a quarter hour quicker, and I'd stop walking to the well."

"Which boy?"

She opened her mouth, and turned, and looked down the lane. In every doorway stood a woman with a cloth over her arm, or a grandfather holding a basin. Every pair of hands in Ranu was already full. The boy who had hauled for Nimmi till yesterday lay on a pallet in the third house, burning.

"Everyone who can walk is nursing," said Rohan, quietly. "I asked at the second door. Nobody has hands to spare." He lifted the bucket. "Without the rope you would be quicker. With it, you are not. I can do nothing about the rope. I can carry what it allows."

She had counted the trip. She had not counted why it had to be them.

At the sixth house a woman stood in the doorway with her arms crossed and her face shut. She looked at the goat on Rohan's collar and spat on the boards at his feet. It was a careful spit, not at him but beside him. Meera opened her mouth.

"It is a fair price," said Rohan. He picked up the bucket again. "Please tell her I am carrying water, and that I would carry it if she spat on me."

The woman's mouth twisted. She stood aside.

By late afternoon the second water had gone out, and Meera's boots were soaked to the ankle. She stood in the lane between the Dev house and the next, with her cold hands pressed to her mouth.

"One hundred and forty steps from the well to this door," said Rohan, behind her. "I counted. You always count."

"Don't make me laugh."

"I was not trying to." He set down the buckets. "I was giving you something to count that was not the other thing."

She laughed anyway, or something near it, a hard cracked sound. When she turned, his face was grey with the ache of his ribs and his hands were raw, and he was looking at her as if she were an ordinary person to whom ordinary things might be said.

"Third water," she said. "It's nearly dusk."

* * *

Sunni took the tea in both hands, and Meera held the cup beneath her chin so the steam rose into her face.

The girl was hotter than at noon. Her breath came short, and her plaits had come loose. But she smiled, thin and wobbling, when she saw the bell was still in her fist.

"Is Badami in the barn?"

"In the barn. Eating the roof, probably."

"Mama says goats can't eat roofs."

"That's because she hasn't met Badami." Meera tipped the cup. The girl swallowed, and coughed, and swallowed. The pinch of redbark had turned the tea bitter and brown, and she wrinkled her nose. "It's horrible, isn't it?"

"It's *so* horrible," said Sunni.

"Then it's working. Drink the rest."

She drank the rest. She slept, with the bell under her chin and her mother's hand in her hair, and Meera stayed for the length of a hundred slow breaths. She counted them. Then Rohan's shadow shifted at the door, and she got up and went out into the dark.

* * *

Kiran's cough was lower now, the tea in him. He was awake, on his side, watching the fire with half-lidded eyes.

"You're writing it down," he said.

"What?"

"The count. Your book's out." He tried to grin. "Who did you pick?"

"Eighteen."

"And you hate it."

"I hate it." She put the notebook away. It was an effort, and her hand shook, and she saw him see it. "Turn over. You're lying on your lung."

She went to ease the pillow from under his head. Something was beneath it, a small hard weight wrapped in wool. When she lifted the corner it slid out onto the quilt.

A badge. Brass gone brown, a stamped disc the width of her palm, with a green bloom in the lettering. She knew it at once, having seen a hundred like it on a hundred coats: a goat's head, the queen's mark. A warden's badge, with a tiny dent in its rim.

She sat very still.

"Kiran."

His hand came out of the quilt and closed over it, faster than a sick man's should. He held it against his chest. The same fist, she saw. It had been gripping this all day.

"It's nothing."

"It's a warden's badge. Under your pillow."

"It's mine."

"It isn't yours." She kept her voice low. In the doorway the edge of Rohan's shoulder showed at the margin of the light, turned away, watching the lane. Eight arm-spans. He could see the room if he turned his head. "Whose is it?"

"Didi."

"Whose?"

"I'll tell you." His eyes were very bright, and the fever was in them, but under it was something that was not fever. It was fear, plain and old, and she remembered how it had flickered across his face when he first saw the coat in the doorway. "I swear. Not now. Ask me when I can breathe."

"Kiran, if a warden—"

"Not *now*." His voice broke, and the cough took him. When it was done he was crying a little, the way the sick do, without sound. "Please. I'm so tired."

She looked at the badge in his white fist. She looked at the back of the man she was tied to, who had hunted a smuggler for a dead boy's sake for two winters.

Silence was free. It was the one gift the mountain gave you, and she had never wanted it less.

She tucked the quilt over Kiran's hand and the brass in it, and moved so that her body stood between the bed and the door.

"Sleep," she said.

"You're not going to tell him."

"I'm going to sit here." She sat. "That's all I'm doing."

* * *

The fourth water went round at full dark, and then there was nothing left to do but wait.

Rohan slept sitting against the wall, the lamp turned low and his hands open on his knees. Meera watched them for a time out of habit. They were warm. They were only tired.

At the grey edge of dawn, Sunni's mother began to make a noise.

Meera was on her feet before she knew it. The girl lay as she had been, one hand curled over the bell, her plaits damp and her face turned to the wall. There was no cough. The room had become much quieter.

"She was asleep," said her mother. "I put my hand on her chest and it was going, and I closed my eyes. I only closed my eyes."

Nimmi put two fingers under the little jaw and held them, and held them, and took them away. She said nothing.

Meera knelt where she had knelt the evening before. The cup was still on the floor, the dregs gone cold and brown. She had given the redbark at dusk. Too late by a day, or six hours, or the quarter hours spent walking to the well. It did not matter which. Sunni was seven.

The pen was in her hand. She did not remember taking it out. She opened the count to the last name on the right and drew a line through it, plain and horizontal, straight as a rule. Then she wrote the date beside it and the hour, in the same hand that had written every other name.

Grey light came through the shutter. A palm came down, not onto her shoulder but onto the floor beside her, flat on the boards, an arm's length off and no nearer. He had only put it where she could see it.

She did not look up.

"I wrote her down eighteenth," she said. "It was my sum."

"Yes," said Rohan. "And it was my water. Eleven trips to the well. I counted them, as you would." He did not move his hand. "I am not saying it to lessen yours. I am saying it is not yours alone."

She shut her eyes. Nothing in his voice asked her to feel better.

He stayed where he was, on one knee in the cold, a hand's breadth from the small curled shape and the bell, and let her do the work.
