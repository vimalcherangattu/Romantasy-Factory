# Chapter 1: The Count

Nine. Ten. Eleven.

Meera laid each packet on the oilcloth as she said its number, out loud, in the cold top room of the salt house. Her mother had taught her that a number spoken was a number kept. The one time Meera counted in her head, she carried eleven packets over the Long Saddle and called them twelve. Nobody in Ranu let her forget the missing one.

"Twelve."

Twelve packets of redbark, each the size of a palm, each tied with waxed thread. Enough for one village, if the healer was stingy and the sick were patient. Meera folded the oilcloth over them once, twice, three times, and tapped the roll against her knuckles to check the folds. Three. Good.

The crate sat open at her knee, and she did not like the lid.

A pale patch the size of her palm marked the place where the crown stamp should have been. Someone had scraped it down to bare deodar, then rubbed the wood with ash to dull the new colour. The other three lids were the same.

She ran her thumb over the patch. Careful work. It was the work of someone who wanted crown goods to look like nobody's goods.

Then she shrugged. Redbark came to her from the lowlands through a man she had never met, and she had never once asked whose cart it fell off. Questions cost coin. Children's lungs cost more. She pulled the waxed notebook into the lamplight and wrote with the bone stylus.

*Twelve packets redbark. Crate lids scraped, four of four.* Then the price, and the hour.

Kiran called it the most criminal thing about her: a woman who spat at the Closing Law and still kept books like a tax clerk. She called it arithmetic. The count didn't care what she thought of a thing. It only held it.

She turned back to the last delivery. Seven crates had waited at the mill below the village, left by a carter who had given her one instruction and one object. The object was a brass button. Whoever brought the next load would carry its twin, and no twin meant no crate. She had written that down too.

The button was small and domed and bright as a new coin, with a shank so stiff it had never been sewn to anything. It bore no crest. She threaded her needle and stitched it into the spine of the ledger, between the third and fourth gatherings, where only a person who unpicked the binding would find it. A button was a stupid thing to hide a drop in. That was the point.

She was biting off the thread when boots sounded on the outer stair. One set was heavy and one dragged, the gait of two people who had been roped together for days and disliked it.

Roop and Chand came in white to the eyebrows. They were the last pair Meera had sent across and the last to come back. Until the thaw they couldn't stand more than a rope's length apart, and they had opinions about that.

"Kiran's," Roop said, and held out a folded scrap of redbark sacking.

Charcoal, in her brother's slanting hand.

*Didi. Grey cough in Ranu since the new moon. Nimmi's counting: forty-one sick, nine of them under ten, six buried. We boil walnut leaf and it is about as much use as a prayer. Come before the children go.*

Under it, in Nimmi's square hand, a list. *Grey phlegm. Blue lips. Children first. No redbark left.*

And a last line, back in charcoal, because Kiran could not end anything on a bad word. *P.S. Nobody here will kiss me. I've decided it's the ear.*

Meera read it twice. The second time her thumb found the edge of the paper and pressed until the nail whitened.

The ear. Two winters ago he had come back from a crossing with his left ear black along the rim, the colour of burnt bread. He joked about it the same day. The mountain took a bite and spat it out, he said, no taste. He had told that joke forty times since. In all that time he had never said where the crossing was, or who had been on the rope, and when she asked he asked about the goats.

She hadn't pushed. Some things you let a person carry until they set them down.

"Is he well?" she asked.

"He's the one holding the lamp," Chand said. "He coughed once while we were there. Once."

Once was how it started.

Meera folded the note into her coat, then stood and rolled the oilcloth under her arm.

"Wardens come up the valley every tenth day," she said. "Today is the tenth. We're at the cairn by noon, and over the first ridge before dark."

"Roop," said Chand, "she's using the voice."

"Eat something," Meera told them. "Then go sit in separate corners, as far apart as the rope lets you. It'll improve your marriage."

* * *

The goat pen under the salt house smelled of warm wool and old dung, and it was the one room in Kothi Shilak that never smelled of salt.

Jassi stood in the middle of it with a hay fork in one hand and a goat's ear in the other, delivering a lecture.

"Pomegranate, I know you've got the cold shoulder, so does everyone. Auntie, don't look at me like that, you bit the last man. Sheriff, move your rump." He saw Meera on the ladder and did not stop. "Third run this month. My knees are as old as the Closing Law and they hold the same opinion of the crown."

"Then stay," Meera said.

"Stay? And who holds Sheriff on the Saddle? Magistrate won't walk beside him. Judge won't walk behind." He dropped the ear, slapped the nearest flank and began harnessing, still grumbling. "My sister keeps asking when I'll come to the orchard. I tell her, after this one. I've been telling her that for six winters."

"You volunteered."

"I did not."

"You were up before me. You've got the harness oiled."

Jassi looked at the harness in his hands as if someone else had put it there. He was forty, gruff as a gatepost, and he named every goat for a person he disliked. He also hadn't missed a crossing in ten years.

"Ten winters of this, you've done," he said, to change the subject. "Ten winters, and you still count your fingers on the stair like a clerk. Twenty-nine and no sense."

"Twenty-nine and paying for your orchard," Meera said. "Stop telling me about your knees."

From the far corner came a soft, tuneless chant. Bindu sat on a wool bale with the red rope across her lap, walking her hands along it and singing the knots under her breath.

*Two over, one under, the third one tells the truth.*

She was thirty-four, shy as a sparrow with people and loud as a drum with rope. Meera had never heard her speak to a stranger. She had heard her argue with a knot for an hour.

The rope was eight arm-spans of wool dyed madder-red. It had been spun in the house of a Taravan trader, Lata, who sold to them at a fair price and asked nothing. It was the right rope: hand-spun, no longer than the spirits would stand, and soft enough that it did not cut when it caught a wrist. Bindu measured it again, hand over hand.

"Eight," she said. "Still eight. It wanted to be nine."

"It can want," said Meera. She crouched beside her and took Bindu's hands, the knuckles cracked open from the wind. Without being asked, she dug a pot of goat fat from her coat and worked it into the splits. "It'll want a lot of things before the thaw."

Bindu looked at the fat, then at Meera, and turned pink to the ears. She went back to her knots.

That was the whole crew, nearly. A man who hated his knees, a woman who sang to rope, two bickering runners and a brother in a fever village. Meera did not think about the rest of what she carried, and she was good at not thinking about it.

* * *

The Taravan salt merchant was waiting in the weighing room with his boots on the salt scales, which she took as a mark against him.

He wore a good pattu and a better opinion of himself. He wanted a load of rock salt put over the pass to Neshar on the next run, off the Guild's books, and he was willing to pay for the privilege. He set a purse on the scale, then a second one beside it.

"Double your price," he said. "For the first place after this crossing."

Meera looked at the purses. She did not count them. She could see from the way they sat that the second had been added after he saw the stairs.

"Double buys you the place," she said. "It also buys the widow Puja's son a free crossing next run. His mother needs him across before spring. Yours is the coin that pays his rope."

"I wasn't aware I was a charity."

"You aren't. You're a customer who can afford to be."

He raised his eyebrows. "It would help the transaction, you know, if you said please."

"It would help my knees if I said please," said Meera. "It wouldn't help your salt."

He laughed, which was the right thing to do, and left the purses where they lay. She walked him out through the village herself, because she wanted to see the road.

Kothi Shilak had one gate, a gap in a stone wall that kept out nothing but goats. The Closing Law was nailed to the left post on a board gone grey with weather. She didn't need to read it. She had learned it before she learned her letters.

*Between first snow and first thaw, no person shall cross any high pass of Hemkot without the writ of the queen's hand. Whoever so crosses, and whoever shelters, feeds or warms him, shall be hanged.*

The hanging tree stood beyond the post, a single old deodar with a low arm. The wardens had hammered a peg into the trunk for every rope it had borne. The old pegs were grey and split. Two were pale and fresh, from last winter. The bark had not grown over the holes yet.

The merchant looked at the tree and then, very carefully, at the road.

"Good morning," he said, and went down the hill faster than he came up.

Meera stayed. She did not count the pegs. She knew the number.

The horns came from the lower road: three blasts, long, then one short. The sound hit the valley wall and came back doubled.

Not noon. Not the tenth day's lazy counting of goats. She was already running.

* * *

Bindu had the bale open when Meera came in. She never said a word and never needed to. The two of them worked as if they had rehearsed it, because they had, four winters running. The oilcloth roll went into the heart of the raw fleece. Greasy wool closed over it, then more wool, and Bindu sat on top with the red rope in her lap and her face as blank as a stone.

"Twelve," Meera said, once, under her breath, and stood back from the bale. She would not look at it again.

Jassi had a hay fork in his hands and the look of a man who had never wanted to be anywhere else. Roop and Chand had vanished upstairs to be married in a cupboard. The pen was quiet except for the bells.

Boots came up the path outside, and in the yard someone shouted an order. A goat bleated, then bleated again in a different key. Something thudded.

"Corporal."

The voice was not loud. It did not need to be. The yard stopped.

Meera went to the door and put her shoulder to the frame, as if she had only been passing.

A young warden in a grey coat had Sheriff by the harness and one boot drawn back. Another stood behind him with a spear he held as if he'd never asked it a question. Eight wardens in all, snow on their shoulders, breath smoking. At the centre of the yard stood a ninth man, in the same coat, and he was looking at the corporal's boot.

"It is a goat," said the young warden.

"It is a goat," the man agreed, "and it has not been charged with anything." He turned his head. "Put your foot down."

The boot went down.

That was Captain Rohan. She knew him by the grey of his coat and the stillness, which was worse than she had been told. Three winters she had heard about him: the hunter, the man who hanged two smugglers and seized her crew's loads twice, the one the lowlanders said never slept. She had pictured someone loud. A man who enjoyed the work, who strutted when he took a rope down off the tree.

He did not look like a man who enjoyed anything. He looked like a man who had read the law, checked the arithmetic, and found it came out as written. He was about thirty-five, lean through the face, with a stillness you could rest a ladder on. When he turned, it was all at once, without wasted motion, and he looked straight at her.

It was a short look. It took in her hands, her boots, the doorframe she leaned on, and the way she did not move. He gave her no time to arrange her face. His gaze stopped, very briefly, on the wax at the edge of her thumbnail. Then it moved on, and her breath had stopped with it.

She began, as she always did when she was afraid, to count. Eight wardens. One captain. Six paces from the yard gate to the door. She said the numbers into her teeth.

"I'm told," he said, in the measured voice of a man reading from a page, "that the guide Jassi keeps this house."

Jassi lifted the hay fork a fraction and put it down again. "I keep the goats," he said.

"Then you are the man I am looking for."

Jassi opened his mouth. Meera could see him searching for his sister's orchard and finding only a rope.

"He's got the cold in his chest," she said, and stepped out of the doorway. "He's deaf in the left ear and rude in the right. I answer for him."

The captain regarded her. Not the way wardens usually did, with the flat contempt of a man deciding whether a woman could be ignored. He looked at her the way she looked at a crate lid. Carefully. Waiting to see what had been scraped away.

"Then you will do," he said. "Guide."

Not girl. Not woman. Not mistress of the house. A title for the work, given as if it were a fact he had verified.

"What does the captain of the wardens want with a guide," Meera said, "in a month when the pass is shut by his own law?"

"A crossing," said Rohan. "Of the Long Saddle."

Behind her, in the dark of the salt house, a bale of wool sat very still with twelve packets in its heart. The goat Sheriff bleated once and was quiet.

Meera did not say please. She did not say anything at all.
