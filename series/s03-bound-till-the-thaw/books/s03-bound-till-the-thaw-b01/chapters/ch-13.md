# Chapter 13: Hunters' Fires

Rohan got to his feet on the second try and did not look at her while he did it.

The doorpost took most of his weight. The pattu slid off one shoulder, and his ribs closed round the breath like a fist round a coin. But he stood. The hut floor stayed where it belonged, and the rope between his wrist and hers hung in a slack curve and did not pull.

"Walk," said Meera.

"Where?"

"To the fold in the rock and back. Twenty paces. I'll count."

He walked. The snow outside was crusted and took his boots with a squeak. He kept his left arm pinned against his side and set each foot as if it might be asked to testify. Behind him she counted aloud in the flat voice she used for goats and packets, and he hated, with great precision, every number.

Yesterday she had hauled him across the floor like a sack. He had commanded forty men. He had stood before the queen's chancellor and read out a post's accounts without a stumble. And he had lain in a blanket while this woman laughed so hard she had to stop and untwist the rope.

"Twenty," said Meera. "Turn."

He turned. It cost him. "I apologise for the delay."

"No."

"I beg your pardon?"

"You've said sorry six times since the door. For the delay, for the weight, for the floor. I'm not charging for any of it." She came up beside him, close enough that the rope tightened and eased. "You can say it again if you like, but I've work for your hands and I'd rather they didn't waste breath."

"What work?"

"Rope."

* * *

She set him on the sill with the red rope across his knees and made him learn it.

It was his own trade turned inside out. A warden used rope to take things: a prisoner's wrists, a load off a mule, a body off a ledge. She used it to keep two people alive. She showed him how to pay it out hand over hand when she stepped off the line, so the pull never came as a jerk on a bound wrist. She showed him how to take in slack against a slide, how to sit back with his heel dug in, and how to read the tug of her weight by its rhythm. Three short pulls meant halt. One long one meant come.

"You'll do all of it with your right," she said. "The left stays in your coat."

"The left is perfectly—"

"The left lifts nothing above the shoulder. You told me so yourself on the pitch, when you thought you were lying."

He had not thought he was lying. He had simply not said. He closed his mouth and gave her his right hand.

The first bowline came out wrong. The second came out as something that would have slipped under a wet goat. "I am sorry," he said, "I have not tied that one-handed since—"

"Seven."

"You are counting apologies."

"I count everything. A bowline round a post, Captain. The rabbit comes up out of the hole."

The third held. The fourth held. By the ninth he had stopped talking. His fingers knew the work even where his pride did not, and she sat on her heels and watched them as she might watch a horse she was thinking of buying. When he drew the loop tight on the post, she tugged it hard enough to rock him, and it did not move.

"Good," said Meera.

That was all. No grin, no more. But the heat that had been sitting in his face since the doorway eased off, and he found he was looking at his own hand with something close to interest.

Under the collar of his coat, the seam at his nape lay flat and warm against his neck. He knew by touch the knot at the end of the cord. Two turns, a tuck, a locking half-turn. A warden's knot, tied by hands that had spent twenty years tying another. He had said nothing about it. She had said nothing. It sat between them like a line in a ledger that neither would sign.

"Again," she said, and he tied it again.

* * *

The afternoon went to walking. Twenty paces became sixty, then a hundred, to the shoulder of the rock rib where the slope fell away and the whole Neshar side lay open under a white sky. He needed the rest by then. He leaned on the stone with his breath sawing and did what he did best, which was read ground.

The Saddle's lip. The black thread of the Neshar road, switchbacking down. The third bend, half a day off, where a stone fold sat in the lee of a spur and a herdsmen's cut climbed behind it, steep and narrow, to a shrine no one had tended in his lifetime.

"They're lighting early," Meera said.

Smoke. A thread of it at the third bend, then another higher up. By dusk the threads had become fires. He counted them from the rock, as he had once counted torches along a ridge at night, because counting was a way of not feeling. Three at the bend. Two by the stone fold. Four more above it, strung out on the slope, and one small orange point right at the shrine.

Eleven. All of them climbing.

He frowned. It was wrong, and for a moment he could not say how.

Then he could. The road lay a mile to their left, broad and packed, an easy march for any column that wanted to reach the Saddle. A hunting party with fourteen torches and a horn would take it. They would take it because it was easier, and because they had no reason to be anywhere else.

The fires were not on the road. They were on the cut.

His mouth had gone dry.

The cut was a fallback. It was no part of the pass or of any map the post held. It was a line Varun had drawn at his direction, at a table in lamplight two nights before the crossing, in case the gully failed. Rohan had held the ruler and spoken the way aloud: from the winter line at the third bend up behind the fold, past the shrine, and round the spur to the Saddle's lip, where a pair on foot could come up unseen by anyone on the road. Varun had drawn it, in his neat hand, on the only copy.

Varun was under the gully. He had been under it for seven days. Rohan had stood on the slide in the dark and dug until his nails split.

So someone had been told. Someone had read the line off a sheet in the post room, or off a wall, or out of a man's head, and carried it down the mountain and sold it. And the cleanest answer, the one that sat there plain as a peg, was—

No.

He did not follow it. He put his hand flat on the stone and made himself look at the eleven fires as if they were eleven fires, and not a sum with only one possible answer. A dead man could not sell a road. Meera? He had named the cut to her himself, on the second night. He counted her, and he struck her out in the same breath: she had been tied to him every hour since, and a woman could not sell what she could not walk away to sell. A living clerk might have copied a sheet. A guard might have talked. It was a long list of other things.

Beside him Meera said nothing. She had put on the snow goggles she had cut from his writ case, and she was watching him through the slit.

"What?" she said.

"Not yet."

His hands stayed warm. It was not a lie, exactly. It was a refusal to say what he thought, which cost nothing, and she knew it as well as he did.

"That's your road," she said quietly.

"It is not a road."

"It's the one you named. The stone fold and the shrine. You said it on the second night."

"Yes."

"Then I'd say you've a bigger list of people who know it than you thought."

He looked at the shrine's small fire. She was on the list, and he had struck her off, and the list was still too long. "I would say the same," he said.

* * *

Dusk came down early and blue. In the hut the chulha ticked as it warmed, and Meera knelt at the shelf by the wall with the juniper bundle between her knees. The sticks were pale and dry and about the length of a forearm. She laid them on the shelf in a row and touched each one, moving her lips.

"Nineteen," she said. "Five for the night. That leaves fourteen."

"And eleven days to the Fair," said Rohan. "By my count."

"Mine too." She tied the bundle back. "Fourteen's under three nights. It's less if either of us has to ask something we don't want answering."

"We cannot stay here."

"No. We can't." She sat back. "Two days. The hunters are on your cut. By the day after tomorrow they're on the Saddle's lip, and we're standing in the one place they'll check. We go within two days, whatever the sky says."

"The sky is clear."

"It's clear now."

He did not argue. There was nothing to argue. He had a ruined lung, a left arm good for nothing, and a guide who had measured every risk since the gully and given him the odds out loud. One in four, one in three. He had learned to trust the figures because she had never once rounded them in her own favour.

She fed five sticks to the fire. The room warmed and filled with the clean bitter smell of juniper. He ate sattu from a wooden cup, and she ate it too, and neither of them touched the rest of Asha's bundle.

"My question," he said.

"Go on."

"The Salt Fox." He watched her face and kept his voice level. "You have crossed Shilak for ten winters. You know the crews. Do you know who leads it?"

It was the same question he had asked on the winter line, the night they were bound. He knew that, and so did she.

She did not answer. She sat with the cup in her lap, perfectly still, and the firelight lay along her cheek. Then she reached past him to the shelf, took a sixth stick, and laid it on the fire.

It caught with a crack.

"That's one," said Meera.

Thirteen. He did the sum without wanting to. A refusal cost one stick, and a stick was warmth, and warmth was the hours between them and the Saddle's lip. She would rather burn it than say a word.

There were two reasons a person did that. One was that the answer was no, and she did not care to be questioned. The other he did not follow, because he had refused one such thought already today and had no room for another. Instead he looked at the stick burning down, and at her bare hands round the cup, pink and steady in the heat. She had paid, and not for herself alone.

"I will not ask it again tonight," he said.

"You can ask whatever you like. It's on the account."

"I know." He set down the cup. "I would rather you were warm."

She glanced at him. Her thumb stopped on the rim of the cup and stayed there. She said, "My question, then."

"Yes."

"Why 'Guide'?"

He had expected it, some day. He had not expected it to feel like a hand on the back of the neck.

"You've called me that since the salt house," she said. "In the yard, in the gully, now. I've been called worse and I've been called better. A warden's got a dozen words for a woman on this road. Smuggler. Runner. Whatever's on the notice. You don't use any of them. I want to know why."

He was quiet a while. The truth was a plain thing. The difficulty lay in how much of it was his.

"Every other word for your trade is a word I write on a warrant," Rohan said. "Smuggler. Runner. The crew's name, the name the post gave it. They are names I have put on men before I hanged them. I do not mean to say them to you."

She did not move.

"It is not courtesy," he said. "I want that understood. I will do what the writ says at the thaw, and I will not pretend otherwise. But there is a name for what I may have to do to you, and a name for what you are on this mountain. 'Guide' is the second. You took a stranger with ribs like kindling across the Saddle, and you did not leave him. I will not call you by the other word while you are doing that."

The fire ticked.

He looked down at his own hands on his knees, and they stayed the colour of hands, and he understood that he had put them there to be looked at.

"It is true," he said, unnecessarily.

"I know," said Meera. "I watched."

* * *

The stove burned down to a red eye. Outside the wind had dropped to nothing, and the quiet had the ringing edge of deep cold.

He had not meant to move. It was the rope that did it. It lay slack between them across the packed dirt, the red wool dark in the dim light, and somewhere in the last hour his right hand had gone down and found it, as a man's hand will find a rail. It lay on the cord a hand's width from her wrist, where the knot sat, the one she had tied and he had tied back.

He felt where it was. He did not take it away.

She had seen it. He knew because her breathing changed, a small, even catch, and because she looked at the hand and then looked at the fire. Her own wrist lay on her knee, bare below the cuff of the glove. She did not draw it back.

Neither of them said anything. There was nothing to say that would have been free.

The cold at the door pressed in. The wool of the rope was dry and a little rough under his thumb, and the heat of her wrist reached him across that hand's width, faint, if he held still and noticed it, which he did. He thought: *this is the first thing she has allowed me.* He did not think anything after that.

Then she said, very low, "Listen."

He had heard it a moment before she spoke and had told himself it was the wind. But there was no wind. The sound came up from the dark slope below the hut, thin and carrying, the way sound carries in cold: a man's voice, young and hoarse with shouting.

"Left of the fold! Keep the cut on your right hand! There's a drop at the shrine, mind the drop!"

Meera had gone rigid. The hand beside the rope turned, very slowly, into a fist.

"Lalit," she said.

Rohan listened. Below the voice came the sound of boots on crust and the clink of iron, a whole line of men feeling their way upward in the dark. Then the calling again, patient and steady, picking the path as a man does who has walked it before.

*Keep the cut on your right hand.*

Asha's crew was not tracking the hunters. It was guiding them. And the road it was guiding them up was the one drawn on a single sheet of paper, in a neat hand, by a table in lamplight.

He did not move his hand from the rope. He found, to his lasting surprise, that she had not moved hers either.
