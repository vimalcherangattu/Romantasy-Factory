# Chapter 16: What the Storm Kept

For one stupid breath, Meera thought the rope tied her to the door.

Then the second stick slid past the sill, and her head cleared. The rope did not tie her to a doorway. It tied her to Rohan.

"Together!" she shouted into the roar. "Same pace, rope short! Stay on my left!"

They went out into it shoulder to shoulder. Snow came flat across the slope and filled her mouth. The cairn-light was out there, though, a blue thread under the white, and by its glow she saw the pale sticks skidding downhill toward the wind-felled juniper. She went down on her knees and swept her arm through the drift. One. Two. Rohan crouched beside her, working one-handed, his left arm clamped to his side. He pulled a stick from the snow and held it up like a man who had caught a fish.

"Four," she yelled. "Five!"

The sixth was wedged in the juniper's root plate. The seventh lay half buried under her own knee. After that there was nothing. She felt along the deadfall's trunk until the wind drove her off, and she saw the rest of them go. Four pale lines slid over the lip of the slope into the dark, and the dark took them.

"Seven," said Rohan in her ear. His breath rattled, but it was only the ribs. "Meera. Seven is what there is."

"I can count."

"Then count inside."

They went in. It took both of them to haul the oak slab back onto its hinges. She wedged the split bar against it, lashed the halves with Jassi's goat-hair cord, and jammed her pack against the foot. The wind still found the gaps. It whistled through the door seam like a kettle.

She sat down on the hearth stones and found she was shaking, and not from cold.

* * *

She laid the sticks across her knees where the snow couldn't get at them. Seven pale lengths of juniper, each as long as her forearm.

"Five for the night," she said. "That's what a storm night costs when the fire's honest and the door's bad. Two left for morning. After that, the deadfall. Wet wood, and no way to keep it lit."

"And tomorrow?"

"If it blows out by dawn, we find another line down. If it doesn't, we sit." She turned a stick over. "I can do all that arithmetic. There's one more sum, and it's the one that matters."

Rohan had set himself against the wall across from her with the pattu round his shoulders. The shape of him in the red light was the shape of a man bracing for weather.

"Say it."

"Fire doesn't warm lie-cold." She heard her voice go level, the way it did at a cornice. "Not a small one. Not a joke that isn't quite a joke. We have seven sticks, Captain. If either of us tells the other a lie tonight, it's a night of shaking and no way to put it out. So no lies. Nothing but the true thing, whatever it costs, and we spend what we have to."

"That is a harder rule than the firewood."

"It's the same rule. We just never said so."

He looked at the fire. He looked at her hands, lying open and ordinary in her lap. "You have wanted to say something," he said slowly, "since the hearth stone."

"Since the yard."

"Then say it. I will not hold the stick for you."

She had rehearsed it for ten days. In the rehearsals it had come out as a speech, with the crew's reasons ranked in order, and a dying brother at the end for weight. None of that came out.

"I am the Salt Fox," said Meera.

The fire ticked. Her hands lay on her knees in the light. She watched her own fingers, because that was where it would show, and they stayed pink and warm and bent a little at the knuckle. The wind howled at the door and nothing in the hut answered it.

"The Salt Fox isn't a crew," she said. "Not the way your post files it. It's me. I run it. I've run it ten winters. The packets in my pack are mine and the debt's mine, and every salt-house cellar you've seized was one of my doors." She did not stop. "That's the true thing. You can do what you want with it."

Rohan did not move. His face was very still.

"I knew," he said at last.

"You didn't."

"I did not know." He held up his right hand, and it was warm and still and the colour of hands. "I suspected. You said *you deliver me* in this room, and I have been trying very carefully not to hear it since." He lowered the hand. "I am obliged to be exact, now that exactness is free. I suspected and I chose not to know. That is not the same, but it is not far from it."

"Why?"

"Because if I knew, I would have to write it."

She breathed out slowly. It was not what she had braced for. She had braced for the warden, the iron voice. She had not braced for a man who had spent a week declining to open a letter.

"Now you," she said.

"Yes." He sat forward, wincing, and put both hands on his knees where she could see them. "My writ orders me to deliver the Salt Fox to the warden-judge at Taravan at the thaw. Whoever it is. Alive, if the mountain permits."

His fingers did not whiten. They lay in the firelight like hands at a table.

"Then that's both of us," said Meera. Her mouth was dry. "The two things that make us enemies, said aloud, and neither of us froze."

"It would appear so."

"Say it plainer."

"I am the man who must hand you to the rope," said Rohan. "And you are the woman I am to hand over. And I am sitting here warm."

* * *

The twist of redbark lay where she had dropped it, by the hearthstone, with one corner scorched by flying ash. She picked it up. Her hands were steady now, which she resented. They had no right to be.

"This," she said. "Tell me where you got this."

"The broad hunter's purse. Along with three silver and a pinch of salt."

"The same sieve ring as mine." She laid it beside her own oilcloth roll and set a packet next to it, the wax fold turned up, so the two rings lay side by side in the red light. "The crown's own guild stamps its sieve into every wrapper. I can read that ring in my sleep. It's why I thought the stuff was honest. Crown-grade. Cheap, and plenty of it."

"Plenty of it, and no seller you have met."

"A middleman. A lowland man who never comes to the salt house. I send a courier to the drop, and I leave a brass button as a token. The courier carries its twin, and without the twin, no crate." She stopped. "That's the bit I'm not proud of. It's a good arrangement, and I never once asked why it was so good."

Rohan went still in another way. "A brass button. Small, domed, plain."

"Why?"

"Varun has a new one on his coat. Brass, among pewter. He lost one on the stair at the post, he told me, and a pedlar sold him a replacement." He said it flatly. "I remember because he laughed about how long a pedlar had taken to find brass."

Neither of them said anything. The storm hit the door and went on.

"Let's do it in order," said Meera. "I'll go slow. You tell me where it's wrong."

"Proceed."

"The gully. The route you rode into at Kothi Shilak was Varun's. He picked the gully. I gave you the odds, one in four it slides, and he took the gully because it was the lesser risk. I thought that was a warden's sense." She held up a finger. "Two. The avalanche. I heard it before it came. A crack, a pause, and then another. A double crack. A snow slab slides on its own weight, and it makes one sound. Two means someone set the first and the mountain finished the rest."

"I heard only the roar."

"You were looking at me." She said it without any weight, and he accepted it without any. "Three. Varun was behind us. Twelve paces back, you said, on the rope-up. He lagged. He was always where the slide would stop short."

"I believed him buried."

"He may be. Nine days under it, he likely is." She did not soften it. "That doesn't change the order. Four, Lalit. Asha's tracker called the party a night ago. Three wardens and two goat-folk. You struck the third name at dawn, and the only paper that said three was the one Varun read out in the post room."

"I heard him read it. I stood next to him."

"Five. The hunters came up the Teeth, and they came up your fallback. The herdsmen's cut, behind the stone fold. Who drew that?"

He closed his eyes briefly. "I held the ruler. Varun drew it, in his own hand, on a single copy. Only the two of us saw it."

"And you told no one."

"I told you." He opened them. "On the second night. And you, I had ruled out, because you were tied to me every hour since."

"Six," said Meera. "The thing they'd pay a lordship for. You wouldn't name it."

"The queen's seal." He said it quite simply. "A lapis stone cut with a goat. You have held it. I carry it to the Snow Fair to revoke Pritam's claim. I told Varun I carried it, and the queen, and no one else alive."

"Then the hunters were never after you for the Closing Law. They're after what's sewn in your collar. And someone who sat at your table told them where it would be."

"And the redbark in the hunter's purse," said Rohan, "is the same redbark in your pack, from the same hand."

"Crown stores," she said. "Stolen by a crown officer, sieve ring and all. The crates came with the stamp scraped off, and I let myself think that was the middleman being careful."

She set the two twists side by side on the stone.

"It's Varun," said Rohan. "It was always Varun."

* * *

She had known it would come, once the order was laid out. She had not known where it would land.

"Ten winters I've paid that button," she said. She hadn't meant to speak. "Every season, silver for the drop. Good silver. Honest silver, most of it from the rich merchants who pay double, so the poor cross free. It all went to the middleman. To Varun. And Varun, we say, belongs to the regent."

"Meera."

"My lifeline was your enemy's purse." The words came out in her own flat counting voice. "The medicine that's keeping Ranu alive, and Kiran, was bought with money that went into Pritam's coat. I've been funding the coup you're riding to stop. Every winter. For the price of a village's cough."

"You did not know."

"Jassi didn't know either. And he's dead." She pressed the heel of her hand against one eye. "That's the sum, Captain. Don't tell me it's different."

"I do not say it is different." His voice was low. "Will you hear mine?"

She nodded.

"I have known Varun ten years. I stood at his wedding. He was the first man at my shoulder when Dilan died, and the last to leave the hall after I swore my oath over that rope." He turned the cuff of his coat, and she saw the clean-cut end of Dilan's rope poke from inside it, grey and neat. "I gave him my route. I gave him the seal's whereabouts. I told him, before any other man living, the one fact that would hang me. I trusted the man who killed Jassi, and I would have trusted him tonight. I would have called him dead, and grieved."

"You didn't know either."

"I do not say it for pardon." He looked at her squarely. "I say it because we have each been feeding the same hand. You with your silver and I with my faith. That is no small thing to share."

She laughed once, a cracked sound. It did not feel like a joke. It felt like a latch letting go.

"Then it's all different," she said. "Everything in the last ten days. The way he leaned on the gatepost in the yard. The way you wouldn't turn your back on him in the crevasse, and I thought it was soldier's habit. Every look I gave you across that fire, I thought I knew what I was looking at."

"And I you." He nodded at the rope on the dirt between them. "The mountain has been telling me the whole time that you were honest, and I kept filing it under something else."

Sparks lifted from the fire. The wind eased for a moment and rose again.

She had two choices, then. She could go on sitting on her side of the hearth with the whole truth between them like a table. Or she could stop.

* * *

She did not remember deciding it.

She was on her knees on the hearth stones, and he was a hand's breadth away, and neither of them was pretending the distance was an accident. She had refused him a night ago. She had said *I don't kiss my noose*, and she'd meant it, and the reason had been new and had been good.

It was worse now. She knew precisely what he was, and what she was, and what she would be at the thaw. She knew it with no hope of a second reading.

"Tell me why I shouldn't," she said.

"You have the reasons," said Rohan. "I have given you every one. At the thaw I must take you to Taravan. You would be kissing the man who carries your rope." His voice dropped. "I would be kissing the Salt Fox, and I would be a warden who knew it."

"That's all of it?"

"That is all of it, and it is enough to stop me." His hands lay on his knees, bare to the fire. "I will not move. You will tell me which."

She put her hand against his jaw, as she had the night before. This time she did not take it away.

He kissed like a man reading a document he had waited a long time to sign. Slow, and careful, and entirely there, with his right hand coming up to cradle the back of her head as if it might break. She tasted smoke and the thin bitterness of redbark on his breath. Her fingers dug into the pattu at his collar. The kiss deepened, and for a while the wind was a long way off.

When they parted, neither of them went far. Her forehead rested against his. She heard his breathing, the catch at the ribs, steady and not quite even.

"That," said Meera, "was free."

"I know," he said. "I watched your hands."

She laughed against his mouth. Then the laughter stopped, because his face had changed. He had gone still in the way that came before a hard thing.

"Meera. Listen."

"I'm listening."

"I will not let you fall on this mountain." He said it clearly, with his hands raised where she could see them. "Not the Teeth, nor the Saddle, nor any ledge between here and Ranu. If the ground goes, I will be under you."

The rope hummed.

She felt it first through her wrist, a low, level note, rising out of the red wool as if the whole length of it had been struck. The hut had no stones to ring. The sound ran up her arm and into her teeth, and it did not climb or crack. It stayed even and held, like a hand laid flat on a table.

Rohan's fingers were warm. They had stayed warm all the way through it.

"You shouldn't have," she said. Her voice shook. "A promise made while bound is a statement of intent. If you break it—"

"I know the rule."

"Then you know what you've done."

"I have made a promise I mean to keep," said Rohan. "I would not have made it otherwise."

She sat back on her heels and looked at him by the light of the dying fire. She had a way of reading a mountain, counting what a slope cost and what it gave back. She was reading him that way now, and the answer came without any help from her.

If she fell, he must catch her. If she slipped on the Teeth or the Saddle or the black ice of the Neshar road, he was bound by the mountain to be under her, whatever it cost his hands. Until the thaw, his life was tied to her footing.

And the thaw was the day he had to hand her to the rope.

She did not say so. There was nothing left that was free to say. She put her hand over his on his knee, and under the wind and the humming wool, she counted: five sticks for the night, two for morning, and a man who had just promised her the one thing he was ordered to take away.
