# Chapter 29: The Cut End

Kiran did not look at his sister, or at Asha's four standing around the fire like people pretending not to listen. He looked at the lapis stone on Rohan's chest, because it was easier than looking at his face.

"I went up the Saddle alone that night," he said. "Four loads of salt for the Dev people. I knew the line. Then I didn't."

Rohan said nothing. He had learned in twelve years of questioning men that a silence left open does more than a question.

"I lost it inside the hour. I turned back for the waystone with my hands gone, and your warden was there." Kiran swallowed. "He'd followed my tracks. Alone, in that. He had his rope out already. He looked at me and I thought, this is where I hang. And he said, 'Wrists, then. Names.'"

The fire popped. Somebody shifted a boot.

"He bound us at the stone. Both names, the third bend for the destination. He knew what that was, a warden roped to a smuggler. He said, 'I'll hand you to the road and we'll never have met.'" Kiran's mouth twisted. "Then he walked me down the line, and he sang."

Rohan's hands were very still at his sides.

"All the way. Wrong, every note. I told him to shut up. He said it was keeping us off the ice." Kiran laughed, one cracked breath of it, and stopped. "The shelf above the third bend went. It took us both down onto the road ice. The weight of it was dragging him toward the drop, and the rope was dragging me after. I saw him take out the knife. I screamed at him not to."

"What did he say?" said Rohan. His voice came out level, and he did not know where he had found it.

"'Sorry about the hymn.'" Kiran said it flat, the way a man says a thing he has said to himself a thousand times in the dark. "Then the rope went slack in my hands, and he wasn't on the end of it. I lay on that road till light. My ear was frozen to the glaze. In the morning I found this by my hand."

He held out his own palm, toward Meera, without turning.

She was already drawing it from inside her coat. The brass had gone brown with age and wear, palm-wide, the queen's goat stamped on it with a dent in the rim. She laid it in her brother's hand. Kiran closed his fingers once, hard, and then crossed the last arm's length of snow and put it in Rohan's.

It was warm from two bodies. Rohan turned it over. On the back, scratched with a nail point, three letters. *DIL.*

He did not say the name. He found he could not, and neither had Kiran, in all that telling. *Your warden. Him.* It was the only way the boy could have held it up without dropping it.

* * *

Rohan opened his coat.

The rope end lay where it had lain for two winters, rolled and tied with a scrap of its own cord. He undid the tie with his thumb. One end was whipped in black thread, small and neat. Dilan's work, the only knot he had ever done well.

He turned the other end to the light.

A rope that parts under strain frays like a broom. It leaves a beard of fibre a hand long. This had no beard. The strands lay flat and slanted, sliced through in one stroke by a good blade, from the near side.

He had read it the first night. He had read it every night since. And every morning he had told himself that ice could do this, and rock could do this, and a smuggler could have done it to run. Because if a smuggler had cut it, Dilan was abandoned, and Rohan knew whom to hang. If Dilan had cut it himself, there was no one to hang at all.

He had sworn before eleven wardens, with this in his fist, to find the man at the far end. He had found him. He was a boy on a borrowed coat with half an ear, who had lain on the ice until dawn, and who had carried a dead man's badge against his skin for two winters because he had nowhere to put it down.

"He cut it," said Rohan. "I knew that."

Kiran's face went white. "Captain—"

"I have known it since the first night, and I have never once let myself say it." He closed his hand around the wool. "It is a clean cut. Nothing else on this mountain cuts like that. He chose it. He chose you, and I have spent two winters hunting you for his sake and calling it loyalty."

Nobody breathed. Then he turned his head.

Meera had not moved from the flat stone. Her face was grey with the day's walking, her right hand open on the count, her left held against her ribs inside the coat. She was watching him the way she had watched the ice shelf on the Saddle, to see where it would give.

*Was Kiran on Dilan's rope?*

*No.*

He looked at the coat sleeve, at the way it hung. He knew the size of that lie. He had watched Nimmi name it by lamplight, two fingers, grey-blue at the root, and he had done the sum standing in a doorway and come up with nothing to say. A lie that large was paid for only by a truth that would have changed what he did.

She had stood in a lockhouse with the cold climbing her arm and refused him this, to the bone, so that he would never stand where he stood now with a rope end in his hand and a boy in front of him.

"Meera," he said.

"Yes." She did not look away. "I lied to you."

The rope on his wrist stayed warm and even. It was a true answer, and the mountain agreed.

* * *

"Well," said Asha, from the fire, "the Captain's face is doing something."

She had her knife out, but only to cut a strip of smoked goat. Lalit had put down his spyglass. The girl from the second rope was watching Rohan with her mouth a little open.

Kiran drew himself up. He looked like a man walking to a post. "Do it, then," he said. "I was on the rope. I crossed the closed pass. It's all true and it's all mine. Not her. She said no to you for me, so put it on me."

*There,* thought Rohan, with great clarity. *There is the law.*

He knew it to the letter. A known crosser, taken or not. A warden who let one walk was a harbourer, and hanged by the same rope. For twelve years he had believed that if he bent it once, nothing would hold. He had watched a stranger on a grey horse bend a sealed order, and forty torches had gone out. The law had not fallen down in that lane. A night had held.

He put the rope end and the badge into his coat, side by side, and he closed the flap over them.

"No," he said.

"Captain, the—"

"I know what it says. I have it by heart." He did not raise his voice. He turned so that Asha and her four could see his face. "You are witnesses, all of you. I know of an unlawful crossing, and I do not take the man who made it. By the letter, that makes me a harbourer. Let it be said so. If anyone asks who let him walk, it was me."

His hands, he noticed, hung open at his sides. He did not know when he had unclenched them.

Asha chewed her goat. "I'll have that cut on a tooth," she said at last. "Nobody will ask. Who'd dare?"

Kiran stood very straight, and then not at all. He sat down hard in the snow, as though the string had been cut, and put his face in his hands. His shoulders shook. No sound came out.

Asha stepped over, gripped him by the collar, and hauled him up. "Fire. Eat. You look like a bad candle." She steered him off with her shoulder under his arm. He went, and at the edge of the light he looked back.

"I'm sorry," Kiran said. "It isn't enough."

"It isn't meant to be." Rohan found the words were already in his mouth. "He decided you were worth it. I would thank you not to argue the point with him."

* * *

They walked a little way along the ridge to where the rock made a seat, eight spans from the fire, as far as the rope allowed. The stars were coming out over the second spur. The cold was the clean kind.

Meera sat. She did not speak. For once she was not counting.

"I did not reach," said Rohan.

Her head came round.

"In the lockhouse. You said no, and the cold took your hand, and I sat there with a glove between us and told myself it was courtesy." He kept his eyes on the dark ridge. "It was not courtesy. I could have taken your hand and learned that you lied. I did not, because I was afraid of what I would have to do if I knew. I have a law and a writ and a sworn word, and I could see exactly where the knowing would lead me. So I chose not to see. You paid for it, and I let you."

"Rohan."

"You lied to protect your brother from me." He said it plainly, and it was not an accusation. "A reasonable person would. I had hunted that rope end for two winters. I had a name for the man at the far end of it, and none for what I meant to do to him. That is on my law, not your hand."

She was quiet for the space of several breaths.

"It's my hand all the same," she said. "It lied." Her good fingers worked at the edge of the count. "I decided what you'd do with the truth before I gave you the chance to do it. I'd have been wrong, and I didn't give you the chance to prove me wrong. That's mine."

"Then both are true."

"Both." She opened the count on her knee. By the last light she wrote, in her small exact hand, and tilted the page so he could read it. *R. did not reach. M. lied. Both stand.*

He read it twice. It was the most serious document he had ever been shown.

"May I?" he said, and nodded at her left sleeve.

She hesitated. Then she drew the gloved hand out of her coat and laid it across his palm. It was loose, and weighed nothing, and it did not feel him. He held it the way you hold something that has been carried a long way.

"The Fair," he said, after a time, because there were things that could wait and things that could not. "At first light we reach the dry side of Garh Neshar. By midmorning the assembly is on the meadow, and the herald asks if any contest the regent's claim. I do. I press the seal."

"And I stand up after you," said Meera, "and give them the count. Ten winters of drops. The crate marks, the scrape of the spoon. The button." Her voice was steady. "Asha says what she saw at Varun's table."

"It will have your name on it."

"I know what it will have on it."

"They will hear it from your mouth in front of the bell." He turned the dead hand over, gently, in his. "I will not move."

"I know." She almost smiled. "I've watched you not move. You're very good at it."

"I will also speak," said Rohan. "Of the road. Thirty-one new graves above Ranu, and the blockade that put them there. I signed it. I have the speech in my coat and not the line it needs, and I think I have it now."

Her eyes went to the coat, to the place where his writ lay, orders folded in cream paper. The thaw, and the Salt Fox, and a delivery. Neither of them said so. Silence was free, and tonight it was all either of them could afford.

"Tomorrow," Meera said instead.

"Tomorrow," said Rohan, and did not let go of her hand.

* * *

They walked the last of the night by the faint blue of the winter line, which ran beside the salt road as if the mountain had decided to be polite. Kiran slept on the pony. Asha's four went ahead and behind in a loose ring.

The dawn came up gold on the far peaks and grey in the valley. They crested the second spur as the light reached the bottom of the world, and there lay Garh Neshar below them, a heap of grey stone with a smoke of cookfires about it, and the white loop of the frozen river beyond. A long pale meadow lay along the ice. Small figures were already moving on it. Drums began, very far down, a slow pulse.

Day twenty. He had ridden into Kothi Shilak with a warrant and a ruler, and a plan that had no place in it for a woman with a red rope.

Somewhere in that yard, his sister was pacing the north wall and calling it two spans out of true. He found that thought warmer than the sun.

Then the bell spoke.

It rolled up the valley, a long brass note that did not so much sound as arrive. He felt it in his back teeth first, and then in the bones of his jaw, and then in the stones under his boots. Asha swore, in an oath he did not recognise. The pony flicked its ears. The note ran on and on, until it seemed the whole mountain hummed with it.

"That's the call to assembly," said Lalit quietly.

"Yes," said Rohan.

Meera had come up beside him. Her hair was stiff with frost and her left hand was tucked in her coat, and she was looking down at the meadow as she looked at every crossing, counting the ways it could go wrong. He knew the look now. He would have known it in a crowd of a thousand.

"Rohan," she said. "How many do you suppose?"

"Several hundred. Fewer who will listen."

"Enough," said Meera.

He took the first step downhill. The rope drew taut between them, eight spans of red wool, and she came with him.
