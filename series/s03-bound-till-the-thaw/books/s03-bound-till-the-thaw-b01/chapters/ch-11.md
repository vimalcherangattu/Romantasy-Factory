# Chapter 11: The Lapis Goat

The coat came off him in three pulls, and he never knew it.

Meera dragged the left sleeve over the bound ribs as gently as she could manage, which was not very. Rohan made a sound in his throat and rolled his head against the wall. The heat of him came through his shirt like a stove through a door. She laid the coat on the shelf, unwrapped Jassi's goat-hair cord from his chest, and looked at what lay under it.

Bruise, yellow-green and black, wide as her two hands. And over it, all down the left side, the skin glistened.

"Right," she said. "Counting."

She said it aloud because the hut was too quiet. The breath dragged in and out of him with that wet sound. She fetched the pot, packed it with snow, and set it on the chulha. Then she took the juniper sprig from the nail above the door, the one the crew hung for luck, and stripped it into the water. The needles hissed. The smell came up sharp and clean, like a cold room with the window open.

Four sticks to bring it to a boil. She fed the fire and did not look at the bundle.

She tore a strip from the hem of her own shirt, soaked it in the steaming water, wrung it until her palms stung, and laid it across his ribs. He flinched, then sighed. She did it again. Steep, wring, lay. Steep, wring, lay. The cloth cooled in a minute against the fire in him, so she kept three going and rotated them like a woman minding bread.

It was not enough. She knew that while her hands were still moving.

Juniper opened a chest. It did not break a fever. For that she had one thing, and it was not hers.

She pulled her pack across the dirt and unrolled the oilcloth at the bottom. Twelve packets lay in three rows of four, palm-sized, wrapped in waxed paper and tied with thread. She had counted them at the salt house and on the Saddle and every night since. She touched the first with one finger and stopped.

Nimmi measured redbark by the pinch. A pinch a day for a child, two for a grown man, three at the worst. Forty-one sick in the note. She had done that sum on the pass a hundred times, and it had never once come out right.

"Half," she said.

She slit the corner of the first packet with her knife. The powder was the colour of dried blood and smelled of wet bark and iron. She took half a pinch on the knife tip, tipped it into a cup of the hot juniper water, and stirred it with a twig until it clouded. Then she raised his head onto her arm.

"Drink. Captain. Drink it."

He swallowed because she pinched his jaw and made him. Most of it ran down his chin. She wiped his chin and tried again, and the second cup went down better. It was less than a man his size should have. She did not tell herself otherwise.

She folded the packet's corner over and tied it with its own thread. Then she took the waxed notebook out of her coat, found the page, and wrote with the stub of lead.

*Redbark, half a pinch. One captain, lung fever. Ranu is one dose poorer.*

She looked at the line. It was written in the same flat hand as *two hundred and six people* and *eighteen silver*. Nothing in it showed what it cost to put down.

She shut the book.

* * *

By the time the pot had gone black on the bottom, the shirt strips were steaming on his ribs and the sweat had come out on him in earnest. That was either good or very bad. She sat back on her heels and watched his chest move, one breath, one more, and counted them.

His coat was steaming too, where it lay on the shelf by the fire. It was soaked with a day of fever. She picked it up to turn it, and her fingers closed on something that was not wool.

Hard. Small. Under the collar, in the seam.

She went still.

Then she turned the coat inside out on her knee and ran her thumb along the lining. A stitched pocket, no wider than her palm, in the seam at the nape, where no hand would go in a search. The thread was a warden's grey. The stitches were close and even. Someone had done this carefully, and not at the post.

*Sewn in*, Asha had said.

She had her knife out before she decided to. She unpicked six stitches, each as neat as a seamstress's, and worked two fingers into the pocket. Her fingertips found a cord first, then the weight at the end of it.

She drew it out into the firelight.

It was a stone no longer than her thumb joint. Lapis, deep as water under ice, flecked with gold dust like a frozen sky. On its face, cut so finely she had to tilt it to see, stood a mountain goat. Horns swept back. Forefoot lifted. One eye, a single pinprick, looking out.

She knew what it was. Every child in Hemkot knew that goat from the coins. It had never been on a coin like this.

The queen's seal.

Someone had threaded the cord through a hole in the stone's shoulder and tied it off with a double hitch, two turns round the standing part, a tuck, and a locking half-turn. It was the knot every warden tied. It held the notice on the Kothi gatepost, the lanterns on the post wall, and the noose peg on the deodar. She had seen it ten thousand times and never once held one in her hand.

Her mouth had gone dry.

"Meera."

She jerked. But his eyes were closed and his head rolled on the wall, and he was talking to somebody else.

"No," said Rohan, quite reasonably. "No, not the grain. The bell. He will have the bell rung for it."

She sat very still, with the stone cold in her fist.

"Twenty days." The words came in a rush and a drag, as if he were wading. "Twenty days from the post. Fifteen, now. Sixteen. I have not—it must be pressed before the assembly, before they—" A cough broke him in two. He rode it out with his eyes still shut. "Pritam. He will say she is dying. He will have the Snow Fair say it. They will look at the regent and see a man who feeds them, and—"

*Pritam.* The regent, in that voice. She'd heard it said with contempt all her life, but never with the ache of a man who'd carried it a long way.

"Dilan," said Rohan. His hand lifted a little off the dirt. "Mind the thread. Dilan, the thread." And then, very low, "No."

She said nothing. She put the seal down on her knee, picked up a wet strip, and laid it on his ribs. She kept her hand there until he quieted.

Then she sat and put it together, as she would a ledger line by line.

A regent who wanted Neshar. A queen too sick to ride to a midwinter fair. A fair where the whole assembly gathered below Garh Neshar to hear a man declare the crown's heir unfit, and to see him invested before they thought about it. And a warden captain who climbed a closed pass in the dead of winter, against his own law, with a stone in his collar that said otherwise.

*Queen's will.* He had said it, and his hands had stayed warm. Not a testament. The queen's hand, in the shape of a goat. A seal pressed in front of the assembly would undo whatever Pritam had built.

And that was why Asha wanted the coat. Never the man. Never the redbark. Only this: a stone the length of a thumb joint, and the clerk who would pay a lordship for it, and a pardon, and the end of Pritam's trouble.

She turned it over on her palm. The goat watched her out of one gold eye.

* * *

She held it a long time.

The fire sank and she did not feed it. Outside, the wind worked at the slates. Rohan's breath rasped on.

*Two hundred in silver for the skin, and a hundred more for quiet.* That was her own price. The other price was the list, and it was longer. Bindu and her songs. Roop and Chand somewhere on the road. Every name in the salt house, written into a clerk's ledger as pardoned. No more nailed notices. No more pegs. Not one more of her people hanged on the deodar.

And Kiran, with a space beside his name in the regent's book.

There would be silver in it too. Enough for the road to Ranu to stay open, for more redbark than twelve packets, for a lowland physician to climb to the walnut slope and tell Nimmi her count was wrong. The queen's seal was not just a stone. It was the one thing in Hemkot that could buy what she'd have sold her right hand for since she was nineteen.

It was in her hand now. He was lying at her feet with a fever she could let run.

She did not have to cut his throat. That was the clean part of it. She only had to keep the redbark in the oilcloth, and let the juniper go cold, and in the morning walk up the slope with the stone and tell Asha to name her price.

She looked at the man on the dirt.

She thought, *he hanged two*. She knew it as plainly as the weight of the stone. Hanged two lowland men in three winters, and had her loads twice, and had hunted her up and down this pass for a dead boy's sake and would hunt her still if he got up. He had told her so himself, without cushioning it. When the thaw came he meant to hand her to the queen with a writ and a clean conscience.

*He told me. All of that, in his own voice, with his hands warm.*

She could not make it stand for nothing. That was the trouble with a rope that took lies out of your mouth. It put a man's words into your count whether you wanted them or not.

And what would Pritam do with a winter? She knew what he did with grain. He sold it at famine prices to villages like Ranu. A regent invested at the Snow Fair, with no seal to stop him, would own every valley behind that pass. Nimmi's count would be the least of it.

That was a reason. It was a good one. But she made herself look at it, because she knew her own mind and did not trust a good reason that arrived so conveniently. It was not why her hands would not close on the stone.

Her hands would not close because he had lain awake with a cut rope in his fist and she had turned her face to the wall.

She had chosen that, and it had cost her nothing the cold would count. He had known it. She'd felt him know it. And he had not paid her back in coin or thanks. He had asked the right question the next night, a plain one, about her mother.

*I do not know what I am doing,* she thought. *I do not know what this is.*

She did know what the count said.

On one side: the crew, Kiran, the road, a hundred names in a clerk's hand. On the other: one captain with a fever, and a stone that would stop a coup. Which of them was she, in the end? A woman who sold what she carried to the highest bidder, or a woman who carried?

She had told a yard full of people once that her mother died because the medicine was on the wrong side of the law. She had meant every word. She had also meant the bargain under it, which she'd never said aloud: she would never be the one who put it there.

"Right," said Meera, to nobody.

* * *

She did it by the dying fire, fast, so there would be no time to think.

She threaded the cord back through the hole in the stone. It had slipped half out when she unpicked it, and she had to find the way it ran. She slid it back to the shoulder, drew it snug, and put the lapis goat-side down in its pocket, deep in the seam. Her needle was in her coat. She stitched it in with the grey thread, small and close, six stitches to match the six she'd cut. She knotted off and bit the thread.

The cord still hung loose out of the pocket's mouth, a hand's length of it. It needed tying off.

She tied it. Her fingers made the knot before she'd had a thought. A flat reef with a half hitch, the crew's knot, the one Bindu had taught her at five.

She looked at it.

It was a Kothi Shilak knot, and it sat in the seam of a warden's coat like a signature. If he woke and put his hand to his collar, he would know it in a breath. A man who could read a pass by its pegs could read a knot.

She picked it out with her thumbnail.

Then she thought back. She had seen the old tie before she touched it, in the firelight, with the stone lying across her knee. Two turns round the standing part. A tuck. The half-turn that locked it, drawn down tight against the hole. She laid the cord over her fingers and did it slowly, as she would copy a line from a ledger. Two turns. The tuck. The locking half-turn. Her fingers wanted to go the other way, the way they had gone for twenty years, and she made them go against it.

It looked right. It looked exactly right. She tightened it with a small, hard tug, and the stones in the wall made their low note and held it.

True. She had said nothing, so it was only the hum of the hut.

She looked up.

His eyes were open.

They were slits, bright and sunk, glazed with fever, and they were on her hands. She did not know how long. Her hands were still on the cord, one thumb pressing the knot flat, the coat across her knees with the open collar in the light.

He looked at the knot. Then he looked at her face.

She did not speak. She had a dozen things she could have said. *You were burning. I used half a pinch. I found it.* She said none of them. The silence sat between them across the chulha and the cold pot, the third party at the hearing.

His lips moved. No sound came. His gaze went back to the cord, and rested there. Then his eyelids sank slowly, like a man putting down something heavy, and his head rolled gently against the wall.

His breath evened out. Not much, but it evened.

She sat with the coat on her knee and her thumb on the knot until her hand ached. Then she folded the coat over his legs, lifted the strip from his ribs, wet it, and laid it down again.

She took out the notebook. The fire gave her enough to see by. She found the page where she'd written *Ranu is one dose poorer* and held the stub of lead over the line beneath.

She thought about two hundred in silver. She thought about a space beside Kiran's name.

She wrote:

*One captain. Price: none.*

Then she looked at it, and shut the book, and did not know whether he had seen enough.
