# Chapter 22: The Badge

Kiran made it forty paces.

Meera ran the lane with the rope snapping out behind her, and Rohan's footsteps came fast and uneven at the other end of it. She found her brother where the lane ended, behind Nimmi's goat shed, folded over the woodpile in his stockings. He was clinging to the split logs with both arms, as if the ground were tilting.

Ten paces back, the storehouse door stood open. The rope ran from her wrist to the doorway and no further. Eight arm-spans. She felt the line go hard at her left wrist and then stop.

"Stay in there," she called. "Rohan. Shut the door."

A pause. Then the door scraped to, leaving a gap for the rope and no more. He did not argue. He had heard what was in her voice, and he stayed on his side of the plank wall as a man holds still beside a trap.

She knelt in the frozen mud and pulled her pattu off her shoulders and put it round her brother. He was shaking in long hard waves. His cap had slid back. The left ear, the bad one, was a lump of puckered grey-pink, its rim eaten away.

"You'll lose toes," she said. "Put your feet in my lap."

"Didi."

"Feet."

He did it. She took them in her hands, the wool stiff with ice, and rubbed. It gave her something to do with her face.

"What did he say?" Her voice came out flat. "At the end. *If you know whose rope.* I only got the last of it. Whose rope, Kiran?"

He did not answer. He put a hand inside his shirt, slowly, as he had under the pillow, and drew out the badge. It was brass gone brown, palm-wide, the queen's goat head stamped on it, a small dent in the rim. He had worn it against his skin on a string. The string had left a pink line on his neck.

He held it out in his fist, and it shook.

"Take it."

"Whose is it?"

"Take it, Didi."

She took it. It was warm from his body. On the back, scratched with a nail point by a hand that had not been steady, were three letters, and she read them with her thumb before her eyes.

*DIL.*

She knew the name. Rohan had given it to her in the hut, and the rest with it. A warden who sang a hymn at every watch and had no tune. A rope end in a coat, cut clean. *I swore before eleven wardens.*

"Say it," she said, very quietly. "Say who he was."

Kiran's mouth moved. Nothing came. She watched him try, as she had watched him at nine try to say the word for the thing that had frightened him in the dark. His jaw worked. He looked at the sky over the goat shed, then at the frozen ground, then at the badge in her hand. His eyes filled.

"I can't," he said. "I've never. I tried at the cairn last winter, on my own, and it wouldn't come out. It's like a stone in the throat."

"Then don't. Tell it without."

He nodded. He drew his knees up under her pattu, and she thought for a moment that he would be sick, and then he began, in a voice with all the quickness gone out of it.

"It was the storm night. The one two winters back, the one I said was a bad crossing. I had four loads of salt for the Dev people, and I went up alone. I know. I *know*. I lost the line inside an hour, just like they say you will, and I turned back the way I'd come and found the waystone by feel." He swallowed. "There was a warden there. He'd been following my tracks. He was young, Didi. Not much older than me. He had no business out in that, and he came right at me, and I thought, that's it, that's the rope. And he didn't even draw. He said, *You're going to die up here, you idiot. Come down with me and be hanged in the morning like a sensible person.*"

A sound came out of her that was nearly a laugh. It hurt.

"He bound us," Kiran said. "Right there, at the stones. His rope, grey wool. Knot on my wrist, knot on his. We said our names to the waystone, and he said the third bend for where we were going, the nearest road with a roof on it. He said it was so I couldn't run. I felt it take. The blue came up on the stones, and I thought, I'll live." His voice cracked. "We were on the line, Didi. We were safe from the mountain. It was the ice that wasn't. We got to the shelf above the third bend and it went. Not a big slide. Just the ground under his feet, and him going, and the rope coming tight, and I went flat and started to slide after him on the glaze. My axe was gone. I could see the lip. I could see him past it, hanging, with the whole weight of me coming down the ice. And he looked up at me, and he said—"

He stopped. His breath came in short bursts.

"He said something," Meera prompted.

"He said *sorry about the hymn.* I don't know what that means. I've never known what it means." Kiran put his fist against his mouth. "Then he had his knife. He cut his own rope above his waist, and it went slack, and I stopped. Right at the lip. I stopped with the cut end in my hands. And he was gone."

The goat shed ticked in the wind. In the storehouse, on the other side of a plank wall, a boot shifted on a floor.

"We'd slid clear down onto the road ice, below the bend," said Kiran. "That's why I'm here. The line was gone, with him. I was off the pass, on a plain road, and a man alone can live on a road. I lay there. I couldn't make my arms move. The ice had my ear. It froze to the glaze while I lay on it, and when it got light I had to tear it loose. That's the bad crossing. I told you a bad crossing. You never asked for the rest."

"I should have."

"I'd have told you a lie. I was very good at it by then." He laughed, a dry rattle with no joy in it. "There was a brass thing in the snow by my hand. His badge. The string had broken. I took it. I couldn't leave it on the ice with him down there. And I walked home with it, and I haven't spoken his name since, and I haven't put it down."

She sat with her brother's feet in her lap. The badge was a small cold weight on her palm.

She knew the rest already, and for a moment she let herself not know it. The ice shelf. The hymn. The warden who died with a rope cut clean. Rohan, in the lamplight, retying and untying the same rope end. *To find the one who stood at its far end.* Eleven wardens, a vow, two winters of searching, and the far end of the rope had been in the next room of her own house, joking about wardens' laughter.

It had been in her ledger all along. Her brother, beside her, an arm's length from the man who was hunting him.

"He's in there," said Kiran. His voice had gone small. "The captain. I knew when I woke and saw the sleeves. I knew who he was before he opened his mouth, and I thought, if I don't look at him, if I just keep talking about the broth. Then he said the name, and said it so gently. He said *I swore.*" His hand closed on her wrist, hard. "Didi. He doesn't know. He asked me if I knew whose rope."

"I heard."

"A death on an unlawful crossing hangs. The warden died chasing a smuggler in the closed season. That's the law. It doesn't matter that I never touched him. It doesn't matter that he cut it himself. They'll say I led him up there. They'll say I ran." He was weeping now, without sound. "I'm not afraid of dying. I'm only afraid of the deodar at the gate. You know the pegs. I used to count them, going past."

"I know."

"Don't tell him. Please. Didi, I've never asked you for one thing."

She did not say yes.

Her whole body wanted to. She could feel the word sitting behind her teeth, warm and easy. But she was on the rope. The rope did not distinguish between a promise made to a brother in a woodpile and one made to a lover in a hut. A promise was a contract with the mountain. If she gave it and one day broke it, she would pay for it as for a great lie, and the cold would not ask her reasons.

"I can't promise," she said. "Listen to me. I can't, and you know why. If I swear and it goes wrong, it takes my hands."

"Then—"

"But I can choose, and I'm choosing now." She took his chin and turned his face to hers. "I'm not going in there tonight to tell him. I'm not saying I never will. That's all I can give you, and I'm giving it with my eyes open."

Kiran's wet eyes searched her face. "That's not *never*."

"No."

"You picked it careful."

"I did." She had. She had built the sentence as she would build a handhold on rotten rock, so the mountain would find nothing in it to take hold of. Tonight was a thing she could do. Never was a thing the cold could hold her to. "Quiet is free. It always has been. And if he asks, then it'll be a different day. Don't make me have that one now."

He let his head fall against her shoulder. He was thin as a fence-post under the pattu. She held him, and in her other hand the dead boy's badge dug into her palm.

Rohan would have to be told, someday. That was plain, and she made herself look at it. He was a man who signed the blockade and said so. A man like that, told the truth, could do one of two things. He could report Kiran, as the law demanded and as he had demanded of himself for two winters, and see the boy hanged at the peg beside the notice. Or he could break his oath before eleven men, and the law with it, and everything he had built his face around.

*If I bend once, nothing holds.* He had said it in the hut, and the rope had agreed.

She could force him to choose, tonight, with Kiran barely mended and the Fair two days off. Or she could say nothing and let him walk on toward the seal and his sister. He had a thing he must do before the Fair. He did not need this on top.

It was not a choice between telling and lying. She would not lie. It was a choice between speaking and keeping quiet, and for as long as he never asked, the quiet cost her nothing.

That was the part that frightened her. It cost her nothing at all.

* * *

She got Kiran back on his feet. His cap stayed in the mud by the woodpile. They went in by the back of Nimmi's house, her arm round him, his stockings leaving wet prints on the boards. The passage ran by the storehouse, and the storehouse door opened before she reached it.

Rohan stood there with one hand on the frame. He had not slept, she could tell, or had not wanted to. The rope lay slack across his boots.

Kiran kept his head down and shuffled past, and the bare ear went by at the height of Rohan's chest.

Rohan's eyes followed it.

Meera saw them go. They went to the puckered rim, the missing curl of it, and stayed one breath too long for chance. He had a warden's report in his head, she knew. Blood on the ice, and a curl of frozen cartilage no longer than a fingernail. Had the two come together behind his face? She could not tell. His face gave her nothing but a tired man looking at a sick boy. She was afraid they had.

Then Nimmi came out of the sickroom, took one look and took the boy, and said nothing, which for Nimmi was a speech. The door shut. Rohan's eyes came back to Meera's.

"Is he warm?" he said.

"He's warm. He'll keep his toes."

"Good."

That was all. She waited for the rest. He stood there with a question close to the surface of his face, and it did not come. She saw him lay something down. It was like watching a man set a heavy sack on a shelf, with care, and step back from it. He had seen something. Whether he knew what it was, she could not say. He had chosen not to ask.

"He ran from me," said Rohan. "I know why a smuggler runs from a warden. I should have known better than to speak of crews in his hearing, while he was ill. I put him in fear, in a bed he could not leave." He looked at her. "Whatever it was he heard, it is his. If he wants to say it, he will. I will not go in and ask him."

"Rohan—"

"You do not have to tell me anything." His voice was low. "You made that rule in the hut. I keep it. I would be a poor sort of man if I only kept it when it suited me."

She had braced for probing. She had not braced for this. It would have been easier to hate.

She stepped in and put her forehead against his chest, on the good side. His hand came up and rested on her back, light, as if she were a thing he had decided not to break.

"Thank you," she said into his coat. It was true. Her hands, against his ribs, were warm. That was what shamed her.

* * *

The dusk dose went round at the proper hour. Meera carried the cups herself, steadily, two at a time. Rohan walked at her shoulder in the borrowed brown pattu and took the pot when it grew heavy. Neither of them spoke much. Nimmi counted the two marked cases through, one by one, with her lips moving, and wrote the number in chalk on the lintel beside the ash.

Kiran slept through it, or let them think he did. The badge was cold against her ribs.

Later they lay on the blankets in the storehouse with the lamp out and the rope tied between their wrists. Rohan had settled on his right side with his back to the wall and his arm over her waist. His breathing slowed. After a while it stopped trying to be even, and she knew he was asleep.

She lay awake and looked at the dark.

Eight inches from her lay a man who had told her his sins in plain words with his hands open, and carried her water, and said *I do not know what I will do*. Every true thing he had given her, he had given while exposing his own throat. And she, who had asked him for them, now held in her shirt the one thing that was entirely about his wound.

Seven nights ago she had thought honesty was the whole of the rope.

She understood now that it had a shadow. Silence was free, but free was not the same as clean, and this silence would sit between them every hour that he reached for her. He would be tender, and she would feel the badge, and she would know what he did not. Or what he might. She did not know which was worse. Somewhere ahead was a day when he would stop being kind and become a captain again.

She went through the possible questions as she went through a cornice, looking for the one that would take the ground.

He could ask what the crew did. He could ask about Asha. Either she could meet with a held tongue and a burned stick.

Only one had no way round it.

*Was Kiran on Dilan's rope?*

If she said yes, her brother hanged. If she said no, it was a great lie, about the one thing that would change every choice he had left to make, and she knew what a great lie did. She had seen Hakam's hand in the yard of Kothi Shilak, three fingers where there should have been five. And if she said nothing, and burned a stick, and looked at him, he would know that, too.

He did not have the name yet. When he got it, it would be the only thing he needed to ask.

He slept beside her. His thumb moved once against her hip in his sleep, a small, trusting motion, and went still.

Outside the wind came down off the Saddle and rattled the ash-marked lintel. Somewhere beyond the wall, her brother was breathing, slowly, like a man walking.

She lay with her eyes open and counted the one thing she had left to count: how many days there were until he asked.
