# Chapter 25: Captain-ji

The soldiers were fanning out before the horns had finished, and Rohan counted the doors they were walking toward.

Seven marked houses on the lane. Forty-one sick, eighteen of them mid-course, and every one of them lying behind a wall of planks. He had seen what a search did to a sickroom. Boots on the floor. A blanket lifted off a child with the grey cough, to see what lay beneath.

"Meera."

"I heard." She was beside him at the storehouse door, her wrist level with his on the rope. "Goat track. We could still—"

"No. Listen." He kept his voice low and even, the one he used on a burial detail. "You are a guide I hired at Kothi Shilak. You are not a smuggler, you are not the Salt Fox, and you know nothing of Varun. You came because the rope took you. Every word of that is a lie to Varun, and lies to him are free. Say them as if you were bored."

Her mouth twisted. "I've never been bored in my life."

"Then borrow mine."

He stepped out into the lane and raised both hands to the height of his shoulders. The left one would not go higher. He let them see that too.

The lane was full of grey wool and small brass bells. Twenty men, perhaps more, with the red-moustached captain from the first patrol standing at the front. Behind them, at the edge of the trampled snow, stood a knot of people in onion-ochre canvas. Lalit with his spyglass. Two roped pairs. And Asha, with her arms folded and her face turned half away, like a woman waiting for a ferry she had not paid for.

In the middle of them all, on a tall bay horse, sat Varun.

He looked well. That was the first hard thing. He had a new coat, the grey of the regent's house, with a brass button at the throat that caught the light. He had slept in a guest wing. He swung down with the ease of a man stepping out of a carriage and came across the snow with both arms spread.

"Captain-ji!" He seemed truly glad. "Gods, you look dreadful. Sleeves too short. Is that a woman's pattu?"

"Lieutenant."

"Not anymore, I'm afraid." Varun drew a paper from inside his coat. The wax was red and stamped with a small bell. "I have a duty, and you'll forgive me. I'd rather be reading it to you in the post room over tea."

He read it aloud, in the clear voice of a man who had been praised for reading. The warrant named Rohan, captain of the Shilak wardens, as a thief of the queen's seal and a traitor to the regency of Neshar. It commanded him taken, searched and held for judgment at the Fair.

Rohan looked at the doors. At the soldiers waiting at them with their hands on the latches.

"I surrender," he said.

Varun's eyebrows rose. "I haven't finished the last line."

"I know it. It says all houses harbouring the accused may be searched." Rohan kept his hands where they were. "I am the accused. Search me. Keep your men out of the sick rooms and I will not give you an hour's trouble. You have my word as a captain of wardens, for what it is now worth to you."

"It's worth a great deal." Varun said it kindly. Then he turned his head and raised one finger, and the soldiers at the doors stepped back, and Rohan let out a breath that was only breath.

It had cost him nothing yet. He knew that. It was the first coin of a long account.

* * *

They searched him in the lane, in front of everyone.

Two soldiers stripped him of his borrowed brown pattu and his coat and ran their hands down every seam. They found the speech on the back of Nimmi's tally sheet, the folded writ and a flat tin of wax. Varun read the speech, smiled, glanced at the rest as a man glances at a lock without its key, and slid it all back into the lining. They found his knife. They slit the nape of the coat where the stitches had already been picked loose, found the empty pocket, and held it up.

"Nothing, sir."

Varun took the coat. He turned it over and put his thumb in the pocket and felt about, slowly, as if it might be shy. Then he found the last thing, folded in the lining: the forearm's length of grey wool, rolled and tied with its own cord, one end whipped in neat black thread.

"Ah," he said softly.

Rohan did not move. He did not let his face move. But the wool in Varun's hand was Dilan's, and Varun knew it, because Varun had stood in the warden hall with ten others and watched him swear on it.

"I remember this," Varun said. "You wept, a little. You tried to hide it." He studied the clean-cut end. Then he held it out, almost tenderly, and Rohan took it from him with a hand that did not shake. "Keep it. I'd never take that. I thought it was the stone, Captain-ji, truly. I thought you'd be the sort to hide it in the thing you love."

And then Varun smiled.

It was not the smile of a man who had lost. Rohan watched it spread and knew it for what it was, as a surveyor knows the true shape of a hill once the mist lifts. Varun had not wanted to find the stone on him.

"It's not on you," Varun said. "That's rather lovely. If it were, I'd hang you at noon on the nearest tree and be back in Garh Neshar for supper. But you've put it somewhere." He tapped the empty pocket. "The regent pays for the stone. Not for you. A lordship, you know. The Walnut Terraces, and the deed's already drawn, to be signed when the thing is in his hand. So I need you breathing, and near, and worried." He glanced at the lane, at the doors, at the grey faces behind the shutters. "And I need this village to stay exactly where it is, with all its little hiding places."

*Every house in Ranu is a hole in the ground,* Rohan thought. *And he has just told me he will turn out every one.*

He did not know where the stone was. He had made sure of that. It was the only comfort he had, and it was cold.

"Jassi's second," said Varun. "You roped up the gully ahead of me. What are you doing here?"

Meera had come to the storehouse door and stopped at the end of the rope, her face as empty as a plate.

"A guide. Hired at Kothi Shilak."

"Hired, and tied to you by wool?" Varun's gaze dropped to the red rope, running from Rohan's wrist to hers, and he whistled. "A jogini binding! On the Shilak! I'd heard of it, never seen one." He walked the length of the cord with his eyes. "And it holds? She can't leave you?"

"Eight spans," said Rohan. It was true, and free to tell. "Past that we both freeze."

"Then she's no use to me loose, and no use to you gone." Varun pondered it as a man studies a convenient shelf. "Chain her to your belt, then. Two spans of iron. The rope can stay, I wouldn't dare cut it. Hired guide." He tipped his head to Meera. "No hard feelings, my dear. You're the best-informed witness in the valley, and I'd hate to lose you to a cold night."

"Whatever you pay," said Meera, in a voice so bored that Rohan nearly believed her.

Varun's eyes passed over her without a flicker. He was looking at Jassi's second, a woman in a stained pattu with a hole in the hem, and nothing more. He did not know what she was, and Rohan understood in that moment how thin the single thread of her safety was, and that it ran through him.

The irons were cold. A soldier with a wet cough locked one cuff around Rohan's left wrist, then the right, and a short chain to his belt. Meera got a single band around her left forearm, pulled over her glove, and a length of chain that ended at his waist. She did not look at it. She looked at the middle distance, with the same flat patience she gave a bad rope.

* * *

"Now," said Varun. "Everyone's out, I think. Everyone who can stand."

They had come to the doors. They always did, in the end, the sick-house people, the nursing ones with grey faces and knotted shawls. Nimmi stood in her own doorway under the fresh-chalked tally. Pinku was at her skirts, mouth open. Behind her, along the lane, a dozen more.

Varun mounted the lowest step of the nearest house and raised his voice, so it would carry.

"Good people of Ranu! I am your regent's officer, and I have come to protect you. I'm sorry for the soldiers. A man in this valley has stolen what belongs to the crown, and he's stood in your kitchens and eaten at your tables." He swept a hand toward Rohan. "That's one. But there's another thing, and I think you ought to hear it, because it concerns your dead."

The lane went still.

"There is a runner of Kothi Shilak," said Varun. "Kiran. His crew sells to a middleman I know well, and I've had many a long winter of their talk, over many a pot. There was a night two winters ago when a young warden died on the road ice below the third bend." He turned to Rohan, and his face softened in the way of a man handing over a hard gift. "Dilan. You remember him, Captain-ji. You loved him. The men I buy from told me the runner who was on his rope that night was Kiran. A boy of twenty-two, with his ear frozen to the shelf, who walked away alive and left your Dilan dead."

Nobody breathed.

"He's in that house." Varun pointed, with the open palm of an honest man. Nimmi's house. The third marked door. "He has been sleeping in your sickhouse, Rohan, on your side of the wall. The smuggler who killed your boy."

Rohan heard the word *Kiran* and the world did a very slow thing.

He saw an ear with its rim gone. He saw a cartilage curl in a warden's report, no longer than a fingernail. He saw a young man's eyes go to his face on the first day and flinch, and a boy asleep with a fist closed on something. He saw a woman's flat hand pressed to her coat all through a snowed-in morning, as over a bruise she would not name, and the notion he had had about it, the one he had not picked up.

He picked it up now. It was heavy.

He turned his head. He could not help it.

Meera stood a chain's length away with her eyes on nothing. Not the ground. Not the sky. She was looking at a spot in the air between a soldier's shoulder and a roof post, a place with nothing in it, and her face had the stillness of a woman holding a full cup on a staircase.

She did not look at him. That was the answer, or it was not. He did not know. He could not tell.

A soldier shifted. A child began to cry. At the end of the lane Nimmi said, very clearly, "He's a sick man in my care, and you'll not lay a finger on him," and Varun, with a small bow, said that of course nobody would. A man too ill to run did not need to be chained. A guard on the door would do, and Nimmi could keep her ward.

"For now," he added, lightly, and stepped down.

* * *

Rohan had thought he would be furious.

He was not. He stood in the trampled snow with Dilan's rope in his hand, and a kind of silence came up in him, the silence of the morning after a burial. There was a name in it now, and a face attached to the name, and the face had been asleep in a room on the other side of a plank wall while he laughed at a bench with three children.

*He chose not to ask,* he thought. *In the passage. He saw the ear and chose not to ask. Why did he choose?*

*Because he did not want the answer.*

He made himself look at the rest of the lane, because it was what he did with things he could not carry. The soldiers had brought buckets of ash. Three of them were working along the row of marked houses, wetting the old handprints with a rag and pressing new ones over them, plate-wide, grey, fresh. The patrol's rule made sense of it. A week's work, done in an hour, for the pleasure of seeing it done.

Nimmi's door took the last print. The soldier smoothed the fingers with his thumb.

Asha stood ten paces off, watching.

She had not said a word since the warrant. She stood with her thumbs hooked in her belt, at the edge of the ochre canvas, and he saw her eyes go to the ash. Then to the lintel, where a child had scratched a lopsided goat into the wood. Then to Varun's back.

The brash set of her mouth slackened, and she went quiet, in the way people go quiet who have just done sums and don't like the total.

She had been paid to track a stone, he thought. She had not been paid to stand in a lane of marked doors.

Her eyes met his, briefly. They were hard and pale and unreadable. She looked away first.

* * *

The lockhouse was a squat stone store at the foot of the village, built for grain. It had one door, barred outside, and one slit of a window. Someone had swept it for the occasion. The floor smelled of damp oats.

A soldier shoved the door shut and the bar fell, and then it was dark, and then it was not, because Meera had found the slit and stood in the thin grey light with her back to him.

The chain ran from his belt to her arm. It was two spans, and slack, and it lay between them on the oats.

She sat, slowly. He sat. Neither spoke.

He had given his word at the grove that he would not ask what had been said there. He had kept it, to the letter, all night. He turned that over now as a man turns a coin he is afraid is bad. The promise concerned the grove. It did not concern Dilan. It did not concern her brother's ear. It left him free to put a question, and the freedom was no kindness, because he did not know what came after.

He knew the rope. He knew its price. A lie would take her hand.

A truth would take her brother.

He could see her breath in the light. She had pulled the left hand inside her pattu, as she had all morning, and was holding it there against her ribs.

"Meera."

"Don't." It was barely a sound. "Not here. They'll hear."

"Then I will keep it low." He moved a little nearer, until the chain lay across his knee and he could speak into the dark beside her ear and no one on the other side of the door could hear a word. He had spent his life asking precise questions, and he made this one the most precise of all, because he found that he could not say it any other way.

"Was Kiran on Dilan's rope?"
