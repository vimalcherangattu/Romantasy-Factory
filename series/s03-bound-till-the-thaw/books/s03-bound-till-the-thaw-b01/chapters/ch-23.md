# Chapter 23: Knots for Children

Meera had spoken six words since waking, and Rohan, who counted what mattered, had counted every one.

*Warm enough. No. Later. Yes. Eat.* The last had been for Kiran, through a wall.

The snow had come in the night and lay knee-deep in the lane. They had meant to be gone at first light. Nimmi came in stamping her boots and said the lower road had drifted shut at the gully bend, that the carter had turned back before dawn, and that no sound man would walk it until tomorrow. Rohan did the sums twice, the way he did them for a burial roll. He told Meera what the delay cost: a day he did not have, spent. She nodded and went on rolling blankets.

She was holding something inside her coat. He had watched her do it all morning, a flat hand pressed to her ribs, as a person holds a bruise they have decided not to mention. She had not shown him what it was. He had a notion about it, and he did not pick the notion up.

He could have asked. The rope would have let him, and she would have answered true or burned a stick for it. That was the rule she had made in the hut, and it was the one thing in this house that was entirely hers. So he handed her the water jug, left his questions on the shelf, and found that holding still cost more than the climb had.

Nimmi laid the last of the redbark on the table.

It was not much to look at: the bottom of one packet and the creased waxed paper of another. "Three doses, what Sunni never finished," she said. "The Dev boy's father. Kamla's two, who came up out of that sleep on walnut leaf and spite. After that I brew walnut leaf and I swear at it."

"Twenty-three went without," said Meera.

"Twenty-three would have gone without if you'd never come. Don't." Nimmi folded her arms. "There's a buyer. Masked. Pays the lower-lane runners for any medicine that crosses, last winter and the one before. Silver, no haggling. Some of it found its way up my back road. I never met him."

Rohan's mind wrote the charge down before he could stop it: *trafficking*, then *harbouring*, in a neat clerk's hand. He watched his own fingers not reach for a pen.

"Does the lane still carry for him?" Meera asked.

"The lane carries for anyone who pays. Kiran's lot tie a cord on my latch when they want a meeting. Two knots, plain, no tail. It means the grove behind the house, by night." Nimmi looked from Meera's face to Rohan's. "I can get word down the lane. I won't promise what comes back."

"Do it," said Meera.

* * *

At midday Nimmi let the three well enough to sit up into the kitchen, where the chulha was, and told Rohan to keep them off the floor and out of the cold. He had no talent for it. He sat on the bench with a coil of Nimmi's spare cord and the red rope running out of the door at his wrist, and three pairs of very serious eyes watched it.

Kamla's girl was five and had lost her front teeth. "Is that for hanging?"

"It is for walking."

"It's tied to a lady."

"It is tied to a guide," said Rohan. "She is in charge of me. This is called being in the care of an expert."

The Dev boy, who was eight and had the look of someone who had decided to be unimpressed, said, "Wardens tie people up. Everybody knows."

"Some of us tie knots. Some of us are tied by them." Rohan took the cord and made a bight. "Recruits at the Shilak post learn a game before they learn the real work. One of you ties me. The rest watch for the trick. If I can slip it, I win. If I cannot, you do."

It was not a game anyone had asked for. But the girl came off the bench and put her small wet hands on his wrist, and he showed her the turn and the tuck, and she tied him with the fierce care of a child wrapping a gift.

He slipped it in four breaths.

"*Again*," said the Dev boy.

They tied him four more times. He taught them the half-turn that looks locked and isn't, and the one that holds against any pull, which was the double hitch he tied on everything he valued. The Dev boy got it on his second try and turned it over with an air of grim satisfaction. Kamla's smaller boy, who had said nothing and eaten the whole bowl of broth, wound the rest of the cord about Rohan's good wrist and the bench leg and sat on the knot.

"You're arrested," said the Dev boy.

"On what charge?"

The boy thought about it. "Sleeves."

Rohan looked down at the borrowed brown pattu, where the cuffs stopped a hand's width above his wrists. "I admit it," he said. "I plead guilty."

Someone laughed in the doorway.

It was not a polite laugh. It came out rough and surprised, and it bent her over the frame with a hand on her stomach. Meera stood there in the cold with a pot against her hip, laughing until she had to wipe her eyes on her wrist, looking at a captain of the queen's pass-wardens pinned to a bench by a child of three.

He had not heard it since Sunni. He had not known he was waiting to.

"Don't," said Rohan. "I am a prisoner. It is undignified to be mocked."

"Plead guilty," she gasped. "To the *sleeves*."

"It is a serious offence."

The children shrieked. The little one bounced on the knot. And Rohan sat there, tied by a child, and let himself look at her face with the colour in it. It lasted perhaps as long as a held breath. Then he saw her catch herself, a hand coming flat against her coat again, the laugh going out of her.

She put the pot down. "I'll see to the water."

He freed himself. It took one pull, because the child had tied it as he had taught her to, and it held.

* * *

They packed in the storehouse in the grey afternoon. It took no time at all. There was little enough to carry: the count, the dry sticks of a fire they no longer needed, two sacks of Nimmi's walnut leaf for the lower road. His grey pattu came up from the bottom of its sack smelling of camphor, and he rolled it at his belt.

Meera knelt on the blankets with the red rope across her knees, checking it from end to end for wear. She checked it every night. This time she checked it twice.

"Meera."

She looked up. The distance was already in her eyes, and he saw it. It was not hostility, only a care in how she held herself, a person walking over a floor she knew was thin.

"May I say a thing that is not a promise?"

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. "Those are the only kind that are free."

"I intend to petition the queen to strike the clause from my writ that names the Salt Fox." He said it slowly, so it would all be there. "I will put my name to the petition. It will say the writ was drawn on a false report, and that the Guide of Shilak carried my life across the Saddle and my seal to the Fair. If the queen is in a mood to be offended, it will cost me the post. Perhaps more. I cannot tell you she will grant it. I cannot tell you I will be alive to ask." He held his hands open on his knees. "So I do not promise. I would rather give you a sentence that is true than a larger one that is not."

The rope lay across her knees and made no sound. A promise would have hummed. This did not, and he saw her feel the difference, the small stillness in her wrist as she listened to nothing.

He had weighed the word for an hour that morning, over the dull task of packing. There was a promise riding on this rope already, made in a hut in a storm, to not let her fall. He was a careful man about adding to it. A man who swears what he cannot guarantee has the cold for a bride, and he had seen how that went. He had watched her hand go white on the Saddle over a lie the size of a pebble.

"You said *I intend*," said Meera.

"Yes."

"You could have made it prettier. You could have said *I swear it* and let me hold it." Her voice was low, and not as steady as she meant it to be. "You said it so I could stand on it."

"I thought you would prefer something that would bear weight."

"I do." Her thumb moved along the wool. "Rohan. Your career. The writ is the only thing you've got besides the seal, and you've just—"

"I know what I have just done."

She put out her hand and laid it over his wrist, the bare skin below the short sleeve. Her fingers were warm. He noticed that, as he had noticed on the pitch above the waystone, when she had taken his cold, and what it had cost her.

"Thank you," she said.

It was the right word. It was said with her whole face. And somewhere beneath it, he heard a door that had been open a hand's width close to a finger's. She looked at his coat, where the cord of the seal and the end of a dead man's rope lay folded together, and then away. She drew her hand back.

He did not reach after it. It was hers to take back.

* * *

Night came early. Meera sat with Kiran through the wall, the rope threading the gap in the boards between them, and Rohan took the lamp and the back of a tally sheet and wrote to his sister.

He did not know how it would reach her. The carter was gone and the regent's men held the road. He had written to the families of seven buried wardens on much worse terms than these. A letter was a way of putting a thing in order.

*Ila,* he wrote. *I am alive. I am coming. The snow has taken the day I held in hand, so I will reach Garh Neshar only on the evening before the Fair. If the road takes another, I will push through the night and come to the meadow on the morning itself. Say nothing in the yard about me to anyone who sits at his table. You will want to correct someone. Do not.*

*I carry what you know the name of. I have been wrong about a number of things this winter, and I am learning to say so aloud, which is harder than the climbing. There is a woman here who has taught me some of it. I will tell you about her when you have sat down.*

*I will tell you about Dilan's rope as well. I think you would have liked him. He could not carry a tune.*

He read it over. It was not a report. He did not know what it was. He struck out *a woman here* and wrote it again, and left it.

*Mind the north wall. It offends me to think of it two spans out of true.*

He folded it into his coat, beside the speech he could not finish. On the other side of the boards, Meera said something low to her brother, and Kiran answered, and Rohan caught the rhythm of it without the words. Siblings, talking low in a dark room. It was a sound he had gone without for a long time.

* * *

The knock came as he was banking the brazier.

Nimmi stood in the passage with her shawl over her head and snow on the shoulders of it. In her hand was a hand's length of undyed wool cord with three hard knots near one end and a long tail ending in a loop.

"On my latch," she said. "A moment ago. No tracks."

Meera was through the door before the rope drew taut. She took the cord from Nimmi's fingers and ran her thumb along it, counting the knots as she counted everything.

"Three," she said. "And a tail." She frowned. "Kiran's crew stop at two, Nimmi, and cut the end square. I've tied it that way myself. This isn't ours."

Rohan knew the two-knot cord. It was in a file at the Shilak post, among the seized things of three winters: *crew signal, two knots, meeting by night.* He knew this one too, from a lesser page: *unknown signal, three-and-tail, twice found on lower-lane latches, never answered.* He had written *seen* beside it more than once. He had never found anyone at the other end.

"Then it's the grove all the same," said Nimmi. "The loop points that way. The runners have a pouch tied just so when the silver comes, and not one of them will say who from." She looked at the cord. "It's him."

"The buyer."

"Nobody pays for medicine and asks nothing," said Nimmi. "Nobody ever has."

"No." Meera's hand closed over the cord. "He doesn't send for me for free. Moonrise."

She tucked it into her coat, beside the thing she had been holding there all day. Rohan watched it go. Then she looked at him across the lamp, and he saw her wait for him to say the thing a captain would say.

"Then we go to the grove," he said.

It had come out too fast, and he made himself slow down. The cord had hung on lower-lane latches for three winters and no one had answered it. That was what made it a fine thing to leave, if a man wished to learn who would come. The regent's people were hunting a man in grey, and they could hardly ask for better bait than a sick village's need, with the one woman on the mountain who would walk toward it.

"It may be set for me," he said. "Nobody has ever answered it. I would like you to hear that said aloud."

"I heard it the moment Nimmi did." Her mouth was thin. "He still has the medicine."

"Yes."

So he went on, because the next thing was worse. In the hut he had sworn he would not let her fall on this mountain, and the rope had hummed. If he now stood behind a wall with his tongue held while she met a stranger in the dark, those two vows could pull against each other. If they did, one would break and strike him as a great lie strikes. He knew which one he would break. He knew what it would cost him, and he judged the price was his to carry, not hers.

"I will keep behind the wall, at the full length of the rope."

"You know the length."

"Eight spans. I have carried it fifteen days." He did not look away. "I will not interrupt you, and I will not ask afterwards what was said."

The rope hummed.

It was low and level, and it ran up the wool into her wrist. He saw her feel it. Her hand went to her coat and stayed there, flat over the place where the cord had gone, and her face closed, quietly, like a shutter drawn across a lit room. He could not read what lay behind it. He could read only that his promise had weighed more than he had meant it to.

It was a long moment before she nodded, and her nod held less than the one before.

He took the lamp and put it out. In the dark, the red rope drew taut between them and held, and he could not tell whether it was she who pulled.
