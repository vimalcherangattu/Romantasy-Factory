# Chapter 14: Died on the Pass

Lalit's voice stopped, and the silence after it was worse.

Meera stood in the doorway with her fist still shut and listened to eleven fires climb the cut. Below the hut the cairn-light lay faint on the slope, a blue thread laid over the dark. Far down, a rope of orange points crept along the herdsmen's way. They were not hunting. They were being led.

"She said the light," she said. "She said she'd give me the day and the next until the light went."

"She is not bound to you," said Rohan behind her. "Her word cost her nothing."

"I know what her word cost." She turned back into the hut. "Lalit's calling the road like a man who's walked it. Somebody gave her your fallback, and she sold it on. That's all I've got, and I'll thank you not to ask me what I think of it."

"I did not intend to."

"Good. Come to the fire. I need your eyes and your opinion, in that order."

She knelt at the chulha, tore a clean leaf from the back of the count, and drew by the red light. The hut at the rock rib. The Saddle's lip. The cut, with its fires. The road, broad and obvious and, by morning, full.

"They come up the cut tonight and tomorrow," she said. "By the day after, they hold the lip and the road both. We can't go by the road. We can't go by the cut. That leaves the Teeth."

"The Teeth."

She drew a row of small jagged marks along the south-east shoulder, where the Saddle broke away into stone. "A spine of rock, steep as a roof. It drops two thousand feet to the Neshar side and comes out below the third bend, past the hunters' backs. Crews call it the Goat's Teeth because a goat could do it and nothing else could. Jassi named it. He hated it more than any place on earth."

The last word caught. She laid her palm flat on her coat, over the small dented bell buttoned there beside Kiran's note, and went on.

"It's three ledges, two ropes' worth of down-climbing, and a chimney with ice in it. A pair who knew it could do it in a day and a half. A pair with one good arm will want two."

"A day lost."

"A day lost, or the hunters. I'll take the day."

He studied the drawing for a long time. He did not argue it. He asked about the ledges, how wide and how long, and where she would stand to belay him. She told him. He asked what she would do if he came off.

"Sit down hard and swear," said Meera. "You've been practising the sitting."

His mouth moved, barely. It was a smaller thing than a smile.

She got up and went to the door, because there was something she had not liked since noon. The stars had been thick and cold an hour ago. Now they were going out in the west, not in clouds but in a slow greasy smear, as if someone had breathed on a window. Over the Long Saddle the sky had the colour of a bruise three days old.

"What?" said Rohan.

"The sky's turning." She counted the missing stars under her breath. Eleven. Twelve. "I've seen that smear before a storm. It might hold off a day. It might be on us by morning."

"Then we go before it."

"We go before it, and we pray the Teeth are dry."

"I do not pray."

"No," said Meera. "I don't suppose the post allows it."

She shut the door and set the bar, and sat down across the chulha from him with the plan between them. The fire ticked. Neither of them had said it yet, and it sat there in the warm: they were leaving the one roof that had held them. Tomorrow they would be two people on a rope, and the night belonged to them still.

* * *

She banked the coals rather than feed them, and then, because it was the last night under a roof, she asked.

"My question."

"You asked mine already tonight. The matter of the name."

"That was the day's. This is the night's. I'm tired of rules that cost me sleep." She leaned her back against the shelf. "Why keep the law, Captain?"

He did not answer at once. He had the pattu round his shoulders and the red rope across his knee, and his right hand had found the knot in it again.

"You have an answer to that," he said. "Everyone does."

"I've got mine. Mine's ten winters old and has a grave in it. I want yours."

"Then you shall have it, though you will not like it." He looked at the fire. "My grandfather died on the pass."

She said nothing.

"It was sixty winters ago, the winter before the Closing Law. He was a warden of the Shilak post, before the post had a hall. I never knew him. My father told it as a short story, the way men tell what hurts. There was a storm, and he went out at night to check the cairns for a lost party, and a whiteout took him. They found him at the thaw. The law was written the next spring." He turned the rope a quarter turn. "My father said it was for him. So that no one else would go out on that mountain in a winter storm to bring in a fool who had no business there. I believe it was an accident. I think it very likely that it was a stupid one. He was a good man doing a thing he need not have done."

"So no one dies that way," she said.

"No one on my watch. Not a warden. Not a fool. Not—" He stopped, and she saw him decide to finish. "Not a boy like Dilan, chasing a runner onto the ice in the dark, because a runner was on the ice at all."

"The runner was on the ice because a sick village was on the other side."

"I know that."

"Do you?"

"I know it now," said Rohan. "I did not know it as I know it this week. I said the law keeps the strong from the passes. It does. Pritam would have the Shilak open to his grain wagons by midwinter if it did not. I said it keeps my wardens off the mountain. It does, a little. I have buried seven. I know the tally of both."

"My mother died of the grey cough in a closed winter," Meera said. "Ten winters ago. The medicine was in a lowland shop three days off, and the law said no one crossed, and no one did. That's my tally. One."

"One is enough."

"For me. It's not enough for you, though, is it? You'd weigh her against seven wardens and the grain wagons." She heard her own voice go flat, which meant it was close to shaking. "You'd do the sum, and she'd come out light."

"I would not do the sum with her in it."

"You'd have to."

"Yes," said Rohan. He looked up and met her eyes, and he did not look away. "That is what I am telling you. I would have to. And I have done it, and signed it, and hanged a boy by it. I do not say the sum is kind. I say it is the only sum I have, and that I begin to think the sum is wrong, and I do not know yet what to put in its place."

The banked fire settled. The wind had come up outside, and the roof beam creaked under it.

She had come to the question with a place ready to put his answer. A cruel man would have fit it. A stupid one would have fit it. It did not fit a man who had stood on a mountain with a dead man's rope in his coat and said *begin to think*.

She was keeping the law's hand and the law's victim in the same ledger, and she had no column for a man like this.

"Your turn," she said, and her voice came out rough. "Ask."

He thought about it. She watched him pick the question up and put it down again, and pick up another.

"If the law were gone," said Rohan. "Tomorrow. Struck off the roll. No peg, no notice, no warden on the Saddle. What would you do?"

She had thought about that more than once in ten winters and never said the answer. She said it now.

"Sleep."

He blinked.

"That's it?"

"That's the whole thing." She laughed, short and surprised, and it hurt in her chest. "I'd carry the medicine over in daylight, on the road, with a mule. I'd get home before dark. I wouldn't count anyone's fingers. I wouldn't hide a packet in a wool bale or go to bed with a boot on in case the salt house door got kicked. I'd put my head down and sleep through a night." She rubbed her eyes with the heel of her glove. "Ten winters. I can't remember it."

"Sleep," said Rohan, as if testing a word in another language.

"Don't."

"I am not laughing."

"You are. Your mouth is."

"It is a very small laugh." He ducked his head, and for the first time she saw the line of his throat move without a cough behind it. It was not an official face. She did not think anyone had seen it in a long while.

She put it in the count in her head, where the other things went.

* * *

She let the quiet sit for a while. Then, because it was her last chance under a roof, she asked the thing she had carried since the yard.

"There's a widow in Kothi Shilak," she said. "Puja. Her husband had a lean-to behind the house, and the patrol found salt in it the autumn he died. They fined her eighteen silver, and she hadn't eighteen coppers. Next day it was paid, and the post clerk wouldn't say by whom." She kept her eyes on the fire. "She told me in the yard that it was the captain's man who brought it. That it came out of the captain's wage."

He did not move.

"And there was Ketan's mother. You said it yourself. And I'd lay money there are others." She waited. "Do you pay the fines of the poor families, Captain?"

Rohan looked at the rope across his knee. He looked at it as a man looks at a thing he has been asked to carry in front of a crowd.

He did not answer.

She knew what he would do; she had learned it over ten days. He leaned and took a stick from the shelf. It was one of the pale, dry ones, and he held it a moment, and then he laid it on the coals without a word. It caught and burned.

"That's twelve," said Meera.

"Yes."

His hands lay in the light, easy, warm and entirely the colour of hands. There was no lie in them, because he had not told one. He had only chosen to spend a stick of juniper rather than say a thing aloud, and she had known men who would have spent a good deal more than that to be thanked.

He did not want thanks. He wanted it not said. That was a different animal altogether.

She had hated him for three winters on the strength of a notice nailed to a gatepost and two pale pegs on a tree. She had no wish to stop. It would have been easier. But the hatred had a hole in it now, the exact size of a man who paid fines in the dark, and every honest thing she had learned this week fit into it.

"You don't have to say," she said quietly. "I'm not asking you to."

"I know."

"I know too, then."

He did look at her at that. She had not meant it as an answer, and he took it as one, and something eased in the line of his shoulders as if a rope had been paid out an inch.

"Thank you," said Rohan, "for not saying it for me."

* * *

It was the fire's fault, she decided afterward. A low fire in a small room did strange things to the distance between two people.

At some point she had moved. Or he had. She was sitting on her heels at the edge of the hearth stones and he was beside her, and the rope between their wrists had gone slack and curled on the dirt like a sleeping thing. She could see the pulse in his neck. She could smell camphor from the pattu and juniper smoke and, underneath, the clean tired smell of a man who had been ill and was not now.

She had noticed his hands in the gully. She had noticed them at the fold, when he took the bowline for the ninth time. She had noticed that he had held his hand an inch from hers on the cord and had not let it fall, and had told herself it was nothing.

Now he was looking at her mouth.

He did not lean. That was what undid her. He sat still as he had sat for the Saddle's wind, giving her the whole of the choice. There was a breath's width between them, and he was not going to cross it.

She lifted her hand and laid it along his jaw. The stubble rasped against her glove. His eyes closed, briefly, as if he had been struck on a bruise and found it was only a touch.

"Guide," he said, very low.

She was close enough to feel it. She could have done it. Her heart was going in her throat like a goat on a ledge, and there was a kind of hunger in it she had not given room to in years, because it was a luxury and she did not keep luxuries.

*Say something true,* she thought. *It'll be free.*

She took her hand away.

"No," said Meera.

He opened his eyes. He did not look hurt. He looked, if anything, as if he had expected it and wanted to be told the reason.

"I'm not saying it's hate," she said, and made herself hold his gaze, because anything less would be a small lie in its own right. "It was, a week ago. It isn't now. I'd like you to know that, since you like things written down." She drew a breath. "But at the thaw you deliver me. It's in your writ. You told me so with your own mouth and I watched your hands stay warm. I'll walk beside you down this mountain, and I'll hold your rope, and I'll tell you the truth when I can afford it. But I don't kiss my noose."

The fire cracked.

Rohan did not speak at first. He took the refusal as he'd taken the first hanged man's curse, without flinching, and let it settle where it belonged.

"That is a better reason than the last one," he said at last.

"I didn't give you a last one."

"You gave me a week of them. They were all about what I am." He lowered his head. "This is about what I will do. It is a fair reason, and I cannot answer it, because it is true."

"You could promise," she said. It slipped out. "You could say you won't."

"I could. And the mountain would hold me to it." His voice was steady, and gentle, and it was the gentleness that made her throat tight. "I will not promise what I do not know I can keep, Meera. Not to you. Not when it would cost you my hands."

It was her name, plain, with no *Guide* in front. He had said it as he had said it once before, at the end of a night she had shaken through. He did not seem to notice. She noticed for both of them.

"Sleep," she said, a little hoarsely. "We go before light."

"Yes."

"I'll take the shelf. You take the floor."

"That is the second time you have given me the floor."

"It's a good floor. It's very flat."

"It has found every rib I own," said Rohan, and she nearly laughed, and had to turn her back to lie down.

* * *

She woke in the dark with the rope tugging at her wrist, one long pull, *come*, and he was at the door with the bar already lifted.

He did not speak. He only stood aside.

The wind had gone. That was the first wrong thing. After a night of rising wind, the Saddle lay in a silence so complete that the snow seemed to be holding its breath. The cairn-light on the slope had thinned to a single blue thread.

The second wrong thing was the west.

It stood over the Long Saddle like a wall, black at the base and bruise-yellow along the crest, and it did not look like cloud. It looked like a cliff that had decided to walk. The top of it caught a colourless light from somewhere, and below that the whole range had vanished, stripped out, as if a hand had wiped a slate. A single low sound came out of it, the groan of a very large thing shifting its weight.

Far down the slope, thin and small, a two-note horn answered it. Falling, falling.

"Twelve sticks," Meera said, because her mouth needed something to count. "A day and a half to the Teeth, if the ledges hold."

"And the wall?"

"Hours." She took the pack from beside the door and slung it on, and the bell shifted against her ribs and rang once, soft and dented. "Maybe less. Rope up, Captain. We're going down into it."
