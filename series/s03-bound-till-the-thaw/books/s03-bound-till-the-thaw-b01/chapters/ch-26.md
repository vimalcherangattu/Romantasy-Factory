# Chapter 26: No

The question sat in the dark between them, and Meera did the sum.

She did it the way she did every sum, fast and in a column. Three lines. Silence, truth, lie.

Silence first, because silence was free. She had leaned on it for sixteen days. But silence given to this question, by this man, in this room, was not silence. It was a yes with the sound cut off. Rohan read a quiet the way he read a ledger. He would hear it and know. Then he would carry it, because he could carry nothing else. He would stand before the assembly with the queen's stone in his hand, and when they asked him what he knew, he would say it. He had never learned how to do anything else with a fact.

The truth was shorter. *Yes.* One word, and a boy who could not stand at a woodpile without a wall would hang from a deodar with a peg hammered in for him. Rohan had sworn on a rope end before eleven wardens to find the man at the far end of it. Varun had already pointed at the house.

Then the third line.

A lie about this would be a great one. She knew the scale, because she had taught it to children at the Shilak Cairn for ten winters. A great lie killed flesh. The cold would go to her hands first.

She had two hands, and she was a guide.

*Two fingers,* she thought. *Three at the worst. I can still hold a rope with the right.*

It was not courage. It was bookkeeping. Kiran's life stood on one side of the ledger, and a part of her body stood on the other, and the numbers were not close.

"No," said Meera.

The word came out flat and ordinary, and she hated how well it sounded.

For a heartbeat nothing happened. Then the rope on her wrist went to ice.

It did not feel like cold. Cold she knew, the plain honest kind that came off the Saddle and could be argued with. This came from inside the wool, from the knot, and ran down her left hand in a single pull, as if someone had drawn a wet thread through her bones. Her palm went numb. Her middle finger stung and let go. The ring finger and the little finger did not sting at all. They simply stopped being hers.

She did not look. She did not need to. Inside the glove, under the iron band they had clamped over it, two fingers had gone white and hard as candle wax, and she could feel exactly where her hand ended.

She made her breath go in through the nose. In, two, three. Out.

She drew the hand off her knee and into her lap, under the fold of her pattu, and laid the good one over it like a lid.

* * *

Rohan did not move.

She could not see his face. The slit of window threw its grey light along the wall, not across it, and he sat on the dark side of the chain with his shoulders square. He had gone so still that she heard oats shift under the iron.

She waited for the sound. Something would say it, the wind in a stone, a note climbing. It was what the cairns did. But there was no cairn in a grain store, only the rope, and the rope was only wool, and it lay between them as quiet as a snake in winter. He had not asked for a promise. There was nothing to hum.

*Say something,* she thought at him. *Say you don't believe me. Say anything.*

Her shoulders had begun to shake.

She had not given them leave. It came up through her ribs in small hard jerks, the shaking of a body that has been told a great lie and is trying to throw it off, and she clamped her teeth to keep the noise in. The good hand pressed down on the bad one. Under the pattu she felt nothing at all through the glove, and that was the worst of it. Not pain. A gap, where a hand had been.

In the dark, the chain moved.

It was a small sound, iron on oats. She turned her head. Rohan's right hand had come up off his knee, slowly, the cuff dragging, and it hung in the air in front of her pattu with the fingers a little open.

She knew what it was. She had done it herself, in a hollow under the Saddle's lip, for a man with a bad rib and a worse story. He only had to find her bare skin. A wrist, the soft place under the thumb. He would take the cold off her and into himself, and he would know the whole shape of it in one breath: *she lied, and it was great.* Not what the truth was. He would not need to learn it. The size of the lie would tell him.

She did not pull her hand away. She was too tired and too cold, and some part of her wanted to be known by him more than it wanted to be safe.

His hand stayed where it was for the length of three breaths.

Then it went down. It lay flat on the oats beside his knee, palm to the floor, as if he had put it somewhere to keep it out of trouble.

"Thank you for answering," said Rohan.

His voice was low and perfectly level. She would have known nothing from it if she had not listened to him lie to himself for three weeks.

"Don't," she said. It scraped. "I didn't do it for you."

"No." A pause. "That I believe."

It was true. She'd said it clean and the rope took it clean, and she was grateful for the one small thing that was free.

He did not say more. He moved his weight a hand's width away on the oats, no further than the chain allowed, and sat looking at the wall. It was the courtesy of a man leaving a room without leaving it.

She could not tell what it was. She turned it over, and over, and it would not settle. Either he had decided she was telling the truth, and had spared her the shame of being tested, or he had known she wasn't, and had let her keep the lie because the alternative was a rope end and a boy with half an ear. She had no way to find out. Asking would cost her another lie, or another truth. And he would not say, because he would not say what he did not mean.

*Mercy,* she thought, *or belief.*

*Both of them look the same from here.*

* * *

The light moved.

It came slowly up the wall, a long grey bar, and stopped on the lintel, and sat there. The lockhouse smelled of damp oats and iron and, faintly, of his coat, which he no longer wore. They had taken that. He was in his shirt and a borrowed felt jerkin, and he had not said he was cold.

She did not say she was.

Her left hand had stopped being a thing she could picture. The numbness had climbed past the knuckles and was working in at the wrist, under the iron band. The metal was colder than her skin now, which was a strange thing to know. She pressed her elbow against her ribs and felt the brass badge under the wool, Dilan's, with the nail-scratched letters on its back. She pressed it with her arm because she could no longer feel it with the hand.

She hoped the dead boy had understood her. She had never asked him anything.

Outside, boots crossed the frozen snow. Two men, by the sound, coming to stand at the door and settle. One had a wet cough she knew. He was the soldier who had locked the cuff on Rohan.

"Gods, I'm froze," said the cough, and spat.

"Then stamp. You'll be warm soon enough."

"Tonight, they're saying?"

"Pasang says tonight." There was a pause. She heard a stopper pulled, a swallow. "Not the lieutenant. The captain's got the other paper, the sealed one, from the hall. Lieutenant wants it all held till the stone turns up. Captain says the paper doesn't care what the lieutenant wants."

"What's the paper say?"

"Burn the cough."

Meera's good hand went still on the bad one.

"Burn it how?"

"How d'you think? Houses are marked for a reason. Ash on every lintel, so we know which to light and which to leave." The man laughed, short and without much in it. "Pasang says it's the cleanest way. A fire takes the sick and the sickness both. And whoever's been sitting up with them. Healer, the woman, the hired hands. Anyone who's seen what the regent's grain sacks look like in a house where the children are dying."

"Seen what?"

"Ask the regent."

"I'd sooner not."

"Aye. Me neither."

They stopped talking. She heard the cough go, and the stopper again.

Seven houses, she thought. Forty-one sick in the lane, and the ones who tended them. Kiran, in the third marked house, on a pallet by the plank wall with a guard on the door and a chalk tally overhead that Nimmi had made herself. Pinku, six, with a clay lamp and a walnut tied up in red thread. A woodpile behind a goat shed, and in the third stack, between two deodar rounds, a stone as blue as a shut eye.

Fire would not burn lapis. But a cord could burn. A shed could come down across a woodpile. A soldier with a torch had no reason to be gentle with logs.

She had lied to save a boy from a rope. Tonight they would put him in a furnace, and she had spent two fingers.

*That's not how the count goes,* she told herself. *You don't get to say it was for nothing. Not until the night's out.*

Beside her, Rohan said in his burial-roll voice, "Seven houses."

"I heard."

"Forty-one in the lane. Nimmi has eight she would not have moved at all." The chain made a small sound. "There is no way to move them."

"Then we don't move them."

"Meera—"

"I'm thinking." It was a poor answer, and she heard how it sounded. Her breath fogged in the slit of light. It went up pale and thin and hung there.

He turned his head toward her. In the dark she felt the look land and did not meet it, and then she did.

He was looking at her face, not at her hand. His eyes were dry, very steady, the colour of slate at dusk. There was a question in them, the same one, but different now. He had asked it once and had his answer. This one was of another kind. It asked whether she was cold.

"I'm well," said Meera.

Nothing happened. No flare of white, no climb in the air. A small thing. It was true by the measure of the rope, though she was a long way from well, and she supposed it was true in the narrow sense that she was breathing. The mountain had no jurisdiction over *well*.

His mouth moved. It was not a smile. "You have a gift," said Rohan, "for the kind of truth the cold can't catch."

"I've had practice."

"Yes." He studied the oats between his boots. "So have I."

It was the nearest he came. He did not say *I know you lied.* He did not say *I believe you.* She waited for it, hungry as she was, and it stayed unsaid, and he went back to looking at the wall.

* * *

Time went strangely in the lockhouse.

The bar of light slid across the lintel and down the far wall. Somewhere a bell rang once, a goat's, tinny and unconcerned. Nimmi's voice rose in the lane, sharp, and was silenced. A cart rolled past with something heavy in it. She heard the soldiers grunt and counted twelve strokes of a mallet, a hammering sound she could not place until she did. They were nailing planks across Nimmi's goat shed door. They were sealing it for the night.

At some point the wrist went.

She noticed it the way she noticed a crossing going bad, by the thing that was missing. The iron band had been cold against her skin, and then it was no colder than the skin. She tried to flex the hand inside the glove. The thumb moved. The first finger moved. The middle, slowly. The other two lay as if they belonged to a stranger sleeping.

*Ring and little,* she thought. *Left.*

She'd tied her last knots with the right. Hadn't she? She'd drawn a knife in the Rope-Hut one-handed, swung an adze on the Teeth, written the count. She'd been right-handed for twenty-nine winters. It would be a nuisance. It would be less than a nuisance. Hakam in Kothi Shilak had three fingers and ran a goat string with a quickness that made young crossers swear.

*Hakam's were the mountain's doing,* said a small cold voice. *Yours are your own.*

She shut her eyes and put the voice in a column. Not now.

The shaking had stopped. That, she knew from experience, was not good. The body threw the cold off in shudders as long as it could afford them. When the shudders stopped, it was because the heat for them had run out.

Fire does not warm lie-cold. She'd told that to a hundred crossers. She'd told them as one tells children not to lick the iron post, a small clean fact with a lesson in it. Only time or the loophole would do it, and time was a thing she did not have, and the loophole was a hand lying flat on the oats an arm's length away.

She did not ask for it.

Rohan had not offered. She would remember that, whatever it meant. Twice in one day he had stood at the edge of a thing and turned his own hand back. Each time he had given her the dignity of keeping her own skin. It might be love. It might be a man declining to open a door he did not want to see behind. It might be the pure cold courtesy of a warden who knew the law and would not lay hands where he'd been told not to.

*You could ask him,* she thought.

*And he'd say he doesn't know. And that would be true too.*

Her head had begun to swim. She put her back against the stone and made herself look at the slit again. The grey had gone to blue at the edges. Dusk, then. How many hours since the question? Six? She had thought it was three.

*Count.*

It was what she did. She'd counted goats and sacks and fingers and graves. She counted breaths now, because they were the one thing nobody had taken.

One. The oats smelled of mouse and damp.

Two. Iron lay cold against her forearm.

Three. A boot scraped. The sentry shifted on the other side of the door.

Four. Somewhere past the walls a child coughed, a long wet rattle, and was hushed.

Five.

She breathed out, and the fog of it was thinner than before. She watched it go.

Six. The brass badge pressed her ribs where she could still feel it.

Seven. There was a seam of warmth along her right side where Rohan's shoulder was, an arm's width off, not touching.

Eight.

Nine. They would come with torches after full dark. She tried to think what Nimmi would do, and could not; her thoughts slid like wet wool on a slope.

Ten.

*Keep counting,* said the guide in her, the one who had walked a hundred people over the Saddle in a whiteout, who knew that the thing that killed you was not the mountain but the moment you stopped naming steps. *Name them. Don't let go of the number.*

Eleven.

Twelve.

The light in the slit went from blue to black, and Meera counted on.
