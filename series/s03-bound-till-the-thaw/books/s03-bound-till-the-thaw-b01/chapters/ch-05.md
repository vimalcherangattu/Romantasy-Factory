# Chapter 5: Two Brothers in Arms

The carving was on the waystone's east face, and Meera found it because she was looking for somewhere to put her hand.

Her knees had gone loose after the third stone of Jassi's cairn. She reached out to the pillar to stand, and her glove slid over a groove, then another. She wiped the lichen with her sleeve. The dawn was grey and dull, with no blue left in it.

Two hands. Worn nearly smooth by a thousand winters of wind, but she knew them. One rope went around both wrists in a figure of eight, and the knot sat between them like a third thing.

She had seen the same carving on the inner face of the Shilak Cairn since she was a child. She had never seen it on a waystone.

"Captain," she said. "Come here and tell me I'm tired."

He came, slowly, a hand at his side. He stood over her shoulder and she heard him breathe in short, careful sips. He was silent so long she looked up.

"'Two brothers in arms,'" said Rohan, in the voice he used for texts. "'They crossed the closed pass bound by one rope, and the pass has stood open to the faithful ever since.' It is in the first chronicle of the Shilak post. The older wardens learn it as children."

"Brothers."

"In arms. A phrase of the time. It means comrades of the sword."

"Then the crews have it wrong." She sat back on her heels. "We tell it as a warden and a smuggler. One of each, tied together because neither of them could do it alone. My grandmother told it that way. Bindu sings it that way."

"A smuggler's song."

"A warden's book." She waved her glove at the carving. "Both of them written by people who weren't there."

"Mine was written at the time."

"By the crown, which wanted two brothers and a sword. Mine was kept by the people who needed it to be true." She heard herself and nearly smiled. "You've no more proof than I have."

Rohan crouched, an inch at a time, and studied the rope in the stone. "The hands are the same size," he said. "No rank. No sword." He moved his glove along the lower hand. "This one has a cut across the knuckles. Or the stone does."

"Or the carver did it for a reason."

"Or the weather did."

"Or it's a smuggler's scar," she said, "and a warden's book left it out."

"A fine theory, with no evidence."

"And yours has a chronicle and no hands." She looked at the carving, and then, to her annoyance, at him. "If it were your book, would you want the first crossing to be a crime?"

He did not answer at once. Silence was free, and he had made good use of it all night.

"No," he said at last. "I would not."

"Then we're both telling it the way we need it." She got up. "Neither of us can prove a thing. I'll take that over a lie."

* * *

"The pitch," said Rohan, when she was looking for a lee. "Before we sleep I should like to see the first of the climb. If it is scoured, we move at dusk. If it is corniced, we must plan."

"It's scoured. Wind takes this slope first."

"You spoke of the rock band. The pitch above it is the part I cannot judge from here."

She had mentioned the band once, hours ago, in passing. She suspected he had simply listened to everything she said and kept it. "Look from here."

"The shoulder hides it." He was already moving. "Twenty paces. I will be back before you have found a hollow."

They had untied the wrist knots at the stone, as the rope allowed, and coiled the red length between them. He took his end up in his right hand, looping it through his belt, as a warden would. She had her back to him, scraping the shelf for dung cakes. She counted, because she counted everything. Six cakes. Seven.

"Guide."

His voice had changed.

She turned. He had gone up the slope to where the ridge bent, and he stood very straight, as men do when they are working not to fall. The rope ran from his belt out across the snow and lay taut as a bowstring in front of her boots. He had walked it out to the last span, and then one step more, because the shoulder had not shown him what he wanted.

The cold arrived in her chest before she understood it. It went into her like water through a seam. Her teeth ached. Her fingers lost their heat, and she saw the breath leave her mouth in one long smoke. She had felt it once before, fourteen winters back, the winter she was fifteen, on her first bound crossing with her mother. She had gone back for a dropped mitten, a few paces past the rope's length, and her mother had screamed.

"Back," she said. "Captain, now."

"I am attempting—"

"You're not attempting anything."

She dropped the dung and took the rope in both hands. She leaned back and hauled, hand over hand, the red wool running wet and heavy through her gloves, and he came down the slope at her pace, not his. He lost his footing once. The rope bit her palms. She hauled him the last three paces like a fish onto a bank, and when he was within a span of her, and then a step, the cold went out of her as if a door had been shut on it.

She stood bent over with her hands on her knees, shaking.

"I did not know," Rohan said. His face was pale and wet. "It was nowhere near—"

"It was eight spans. I told you eight." She straightened. "You wanted to see for yourself. That's what you do. That's all right on the plain, where nothing minds if you're wrong. Here it'll cost you your feet."

"It cost you too."

"Yes."

"I am sorry."

"Don't be sorry." She wound the slack round her fist. It took three tries, because her hand was not steady. "Listen. You walk where the rope lets you. If you need to see something, you ask me and I'll tell you or I'll go. Your body's not yours to spend on a look, Captain. It's mine. It's mine to keep alive until the thaw, and you're not going to make that harder."

He did not look away. In the grey light his eyes were the dull grey of wet slate.

"Yours," he said, "and mine to keep in turn, I suppose."

She had not thought of that. She opened her mouth and nothing came out.

"I will ask," said Rohan.

* * *

The shelf at the waystone held more than stones. There was a nest of old dung cakes under the lowest slab, the kind goat-crews left for the next crossers, dry as bread and grey with age. Meera knew it was there. Every guide on the pass did. It was the oldest law of the mountain, older than the crown's, and no one had ever written it down.

She built the fire in the lee of the pillar on a bed of flat stones, with a ring of snow blocks to turn the wind. Seven cakes, a little juniper bark from her pocket, a spark from the flint. The smoke rose thin and sour. The flame took hold, small and orange, and she held her hands to it and nearly cried at how little it was.

Rohan stood a good way off with his arms crossed.

"This is harbouring," he said.

"Mm."

"A fire shared with an unlawful crosser. By the letter of the Closing Law, I am to report you, seize your goods and carry you to the post. The penalty for a warden who fails to do so is the same as the penalty for you."

"Yes."

"I wish it understood that I know."

"Then it's understood." She fed in a cake. "Your wish is noted. You're letting the heat out."

He stood a moment longer, in his torn coat, with the queen's seal sewn in his collar and six packets of redbark in his pocket. Then he sat down at her fire, by the long way round, and held out his right hand to the flame.

He did not say anything else. She did not say anything either. It seemed to her that a man who had hanged people for this should be allowed a minute.

She melted snow in the tin cup. She mixed the sattu in two portions and set one in front of him, and then she took the other and put it in front of him as well.

"What is this?"

"Your wager. Winner eats the loser's share." She pushed the cup. "I'm to go hungry. Eat it."

"I would not take a woman's ration."

"It's a bet, Captain. A bet's the only thing on this mountain I don't resent paying."

He looked at her for a long time. Then he took the second cup, and with some gravity, drank half of it and handed the rest back.

"The winning is enough," he said. "The surplus I decline."

She took it before he could change his mind and was ashamed of how good it tasted.

* * *

They slept, in turns, wedged against the stone. By late afternoon the light had thickened. The wind fell, and from between the courses of the waystone a thread of blue began to rise, faint as breath on glass. The line would come back with the dark.

Meera woke with her face against the pillar and a stone's rim pressing her cheek. The fire was down to a glow. Across it sat Rohan, awake, with his legs folded under him and his left shoulder held very still.

She watched him breathe.

The right side of his chest moved. The left did not, not much, and then in short, cautious pulls that stopped halfway, as though a hand were pressing on the top of them. He took one now and she saw his jaw work before he let it go.

"You're favouring your left," she said.

"I have a stiff side."

"You have cracked ribs."

"You said it yourself last night. Two, perhaps three." He lifted his chin. "I have had worse. There is nothing to be done for ribs but wait."

"You're breathing like a man listening for footsteps."

"I am a warden."

She let that go. It was a dodge, and dodging cost nothing, so she had no right to complain. She fed the last cake into the fire.

"A question for a question," she said. "Since we're stuck with each other. I ask one, you ask one. Anything you won't answer, you say so, and we both go on. No cost."

He considered. "The rope would not charge us for it."

"It's an old trade. The crews do it on long nights. You'd be surprised what comes out when you're allowed to say no."

"Very well." He settled his hand on his knee. "You first."

She'd had the question since the cairn.

"What's at Garh Neshar?"

"I decline."

"That fast?"

"It is not mine to say." His face was steady. "I will tell you that it matters to more than me. I will tell you that I would tell you if I could. Both are true, and you may watch my hands." He turned them palm up toward the glow. They were red from the fire and nothing else. "My turn."

She waited.

"How many people have you carried across?"

She had expected something about the Salt Fox. This was worse, because it was a count, and she kept a count, and her whole body knew the number.

She could have said nothing. Nothing would have cost her nothing.

"Two hundred and six," she said. "Not counting salt."

He did not move.

"That's evidence, Captain. You know that."

"I carry no ink," said Rohan. "And the stones heard you, not me. Is the number correct?"

"Last I counted." She stared into the coals. "I wrote it down. Every one."

"You wrote it down."

"I keep ledgers. You'll laugh."

"I am not laughing."

He wasn't. That surprised her more than anything he had said. She had told a warden she kept books on her crimes, and he looked at her as if she had told him something about herself.

"My turn," she said. "Why do you hunt the crew?"

She thought he would refuse again. She thought she saw him think of it. The fire popped, and a spark rose up and went out against the dark.

"A warden died on this pass two winters ago," said Rohan.

The words were set down plain, with no weight added.

"He was young. He was chasing a smuggler across the ice in a storm. The report said what the report says. I read it, and I signed it, and I put his name in the book." His hand was closed on his knee, and she saw the knuckles. "I hunted your crew one winter because the law said so. I have hunted it two more for him."

"One for the law," Meera said slowly. "Two for him."

"Yes."

She turned the sum over. Three winters of hunting, and a boy dead for only two of them. A man shaping a lie would have rounded it, made it tidy, given himself the grief from the start. Rohan had given her the gap and let it sit there, to his own discredit. The waystone stayed low and level.

Something cold touched the back of her neck and was gone. It was the wind. There were many things in two winters. It had been a long one, and she had buried a neighbour in it, and Kiran had come in with an ear that would not heal and a story about a bad crossing. She put the thought down, because she did not have the strength to carry it and the redbark both.

"I'm sorry for him," she said. It was true, and she waited for nothing to happen, and nothing did.

"Thank you." He said it as a man says it when he is not used to being offered the thing. "My turn again, or yours?"

"Mine." She looked at his side. She did not decorate it. "Are your ribs hurt, Captain? Plainly."

"It's nothing."

The waystone hummed.

It had been a low sound all day, the sound of wind in a hollow. Now it climbed, a note at a time, as if someone were drawing a bow slowly across it. The blue thread between the courses thickened into light and ran down the pillar to the snow, and the whole fire-ring went the colour of ice.

Rohan did not seem to hear. He reached for his glove on the stone beside him, a plain thing, a man getting ready to travel. He worked it onto his right hand, finger by finger, with the patience of someone whose body is already a long way from comfortable.

Under the cairn-light she saw his fingertips go.

They went white from the nails inward, as hers had, a slow pale creeping up like milk drawn along a wick, and the wool of the glove closed over them before he saw. He tugged the cuff down and stood.

"It will be dark soon," he said. "We should go."

Meera did not answer. She sat by the dying fire with the red rope coiled in her lap, and counted.

One. Two. She got to six before he turned his back.
