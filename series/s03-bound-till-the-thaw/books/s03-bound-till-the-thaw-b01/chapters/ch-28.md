# Chapter 28: Asha's Price

"Price first," said Asha, and she did not move out of the doorway.

Meera got her back flat to the wall and stood, because she would not bargain from the floor. The stone slid under her shoulder blades. The room tilted, righted itself, and tilted again. Rohan's coat hung off her like a tent.

"You have a key," she said. "That's a good start."

"It's a start." Asha's eyes went to the chain between them, then to the red rope under it. "Your lieutenant's pardon, Captain. It's what I came over the Saddle for. A crown paper with my crew's names on it." She said it flatly, as if reading a bill. "I stood in the lane last night with my four. I watched the torches go down the line."

"We saw them," said Rohan.

"Then you saw a man with a paper tell forty men to burn children." Asha's knife hand did not shift. "I took Varun's silver because he paid on the nail and never haggled. I thought that was manners. It's just a man who'll never need to pay the full bill." She breathed out, and it smoked. "That pardon is the same noose with a nicer rope. When he's done with the seal, he'll be done with me."

"So you don't want the pardon," said Meera.

"I want my crew alive. Lalit, the ochre four, the girl on my second rope." She ticked them off on the knife hand's fingers without lowering the blade. "And I want my name out of his books. He keeps a book. He writes every payment in a tidy hand, because he's a man who believes in tidy hands. While my name's in it, I'm a hired knife for a traitor. I want it somewhere else."

"A warden can't pardon," Rohan said. "I'll not say otherwise to you."

"I know the law."

"Only the queen can. What I can do is stand before the assembly and say that you came in the dark, with a key, against the man who paid you. That is true. I will say it as loudly as I'm able." He kept his voice level and his hands still. "I can't promise what the assembly does with it."

Asha studied him. "You've got a face that doesn't know how to say a fine thing it can't back."

"No."

"Hm." She looked at Meera. "And you?"

*She wants a pledge,* Meera thought, *and I am bound.* A promise made on the rope and broken would strike her like a great lie, and she had one good hand left to spend. She would not say a word she could not stand behind.

So she did the other thing.

She put her right hand inside her shirt, past the brass badge, and drew out the count. The wax cover was warm from her skin. It had survived the chain, the iron band, and the sentries, who had taken her pack and her knife and never thought to look where a woman kept a thing she loved. She opened it on her knee and found the page by feel. Her thumb knew it better than her eyes.

She tore it out, along the stitching, with her teeth and one hand.

"Dates," she said. "The middleman. Eleven winters of drops, the day and the hour, in my hand. Crate marks: a bell scored through the stamp, then the stamp scraped off with a spoon, and the scrape is always a left-handed one. Where I paid, how much, what I was told when I asked who sold." She held it out. "It's a ledger. It's the only kind of evidence I've got."

Asha did not take it. "That's your neck."

"It's his." Meera reached into the binding of the count with two fingers, and the little thing came out onto her palm. Brass, domed, plain, with a stiff new shank. She had kept it there since the drop, because a token goes where the count goes. "And this."

Asha's eyes went to it. Her whole face changed.

"The crate man left that when I paid," said Meera. "His courier was meant to carry the twin. No twin, no crate. I never saw the courier. I never saw the man."

"I did," said Asha slowly. "I watched Varun thumb a button like that on his coat the day he hired me. New among the pewter ones. He said a pedlar sold it to him." She laughed, one breath. "He had it turned wrong. The shank to the cloth, you know, the way a man sews a thing he's been handed in a hurry."

"Say it plain," said Rohan. His voice had dropped.

Asha said it plain, to him, not to Meera. "Varun paid me. Four silver a week and a promise of a paper. And the lowland man who sold your guide her bark was his runner, a thin one with a bad left foot who drank in the salt-road inns. I've seen the two of them at the same table." She took the page then. She folded it in half and in half again and slid it into her sleeve. "That's the bill, then. A name for a name."

"A page for a page," Meera said. "Take the button too."

"No." Asha pushed her hand closed over it. "That's yours. You'll want it in front of a regent." The knife went into her belt. She fitted the iron key to the cuff on Rohan's wrist, and it turned with a click Meera felt in her teeth.

* * *

Both sentries sat against the lockhouse wall with their hands tied in a way Meera recognised. Three-nested-loops, snug as a bird's nest. The wet cough had a strip of ochre canvas in his mouth and the resigned look of a man who'd rather be tied than asked.

Lalit stood at the corner with his spyglass hanging from his neck and a pony's lead in his fist. He did not look at the captain of wardens. He looked at Meera, once, and then at the ground.

"The lane's quiet," he said. "The general's gone up. The red moustache is asleep in a chair."

"Where's the lieutenant?"

"Garh road, with the twenty. He left at midnight." Lalit shrugged one thin shoulder. "He thinks the village will be ash by morning. He wanted to be seen far away."

Rohan ducked his head at the forearm band. Asha's key took it off in two turns, and the iron slid from Meera's arm with a clank, and the torn strip of pattu fell out with it. The cold air touched her sleeve and she could not feel it on the left hand at all.

She put the hand in her coat and said nothing.

"There's a door I want," she said. "Nimmi's house. The chulha corner."

"The seal's in the woodpile," said Rohan.

"No." She felt, absurdly, a small flush of something near pleasure. "It was. It isn't now."

* * *

Nimmi opened before she knocked.

She stood in her doorway in her night shawl, grey hair loose, with a stub of candle. She looked at the four of them, one at a time, the way she looked down a bed of the sick. Then she stood aside, and the smell of boiling walnut leaf came out of the house into the cold.

"Pinku saw you from the tree," she said. "When you hid it behind the shed. He told me this morning that the lady had left a blue egg in the logs." She sniffed. "A shed can come down on logs. Wood is for burning. I took it out last night, after the soldiers marked the door, and I put it where nobody burns anything."

The chulha corner. The big clay pot sat on its stones with a cloth over the lip, the dark tea steaming gently, the first wave's walnut leaf rolling in it. Meera crossed the room and lifted the cloth.

Nothing but leaves and brown water. She shoved her right sleeve to the elbow and reached in anyway.

The heat bit. Her fingers closed on a cord, and then on cold stone, thumb-joint long and smooth, with a shoulder where the cord was threaded. She drew it up streaming. The tea ran down her wrist and dripped off her elbow. Lapis, the colour of a shut eye, cut with a goat finer than any coin's.

"Nobody lifts a lid on a healer's tea," said Nimmi. "They'd think it was a cure."

"It is," Meera said, and heard the shake in it.

She turned. Rohan stood in the doorway with his gloves in his hand. He had watched her go down on one knee by his coat and a hidden cord. He had watched her hide it, in a way, because he had counted aloud to a hundred and sixty at a wall. He hadn't thought to ask where. She held the wet stone out on her palm.

"You'll want that dry," she said. "And on your own neck."

He took it. His hand closed over hers for a moment, bare palm to palm, and she braced for the thing she had braced for since the lockhouse. It didn't come. There was no cold to pass. She had no lie on her now that his hand could reach. It was only a man's skin, warm and a little rough, holding hers a breath longer than the stone needed.

Then he stepped back. She turned the cord over for the warden's double hitch, the knot she had once untied and made again with her own hands. She tried to tie it. The left hand lay in her lap, loose, with nothing to give the cord. The right alone made a mess of it.

"Here," said Rohan quietly.

He reset it himself. Two turns round the standing part. A tuck. A locking half-turn. He had it done before she could find a way to be cross about it.

"Nimmi," said Meera. "Look at this."

She had not meant to. It came out because Nimmi was already looking. The healer's eyes had gone to the coat sleeve, to the way the left arm hung.

"Sit," said Nimmi. "Lamp."

Pinku held the lamp. He was in his nightshirt with a walnut tied up in red thread around his neck, and he looked at Meera's hand with great interest and no fear. Nimmi took the wrist in two hard fingers and eased the glove off.

Meera did not look at it. She watched the child's face. He wrinkled his nose.

"Ring finger," said Nimmi in the voice of someone naming a price. "Little finger. No colour. No feeling. Waxy to the second joint, grey-blue at the root, and the skin has begun to loosen like a cooked onion. Middle finger has blood in it. It'll live." She laid the hand back on Meera's knee as if returning a book. "Those two are dead. They've been dead since yesterday and they'll not be anything else."

"I know."

"Not off today. You'd bleed to feed a Fair. You'll have the hand for the pen, and the knot, and whatever else you're about." Nimmi sat back on her heels. "After the Fair. A day after, no more. If the rot goes up the hand into the wrist, it takes the arm, and I've no redbark for that and no wish to learn."

*After the Fair.* Meera put the words in a column. Two fingers. A day.

She raised her eyes then, and met Rohan's.

He was standing very still. He was looking at the hand. He had asked her one question in the dark, and she had answered it, and the thing he was looking at was the size of that answer. She watched him work the sum. She watched it go all the way down and come up with nothing he could say.

He did not ask. His mouth worked, once, and shut.

"Later," said Meera.

"Whenever you like," said Rohan. His voice was rough. "It will keep."

* * *

Kiran was on his feet in the back room, holding the plank wall.

He had put on a coat that was not his and had tied his boots with the care of a man who was not sure he'd be able to bend again. His face was the colour of wet ash, and the torn ear stood out against it like a sail rag. His eyes were bright and clear and, for the first time in days, quite sane.

"Don't," said Meera. "Sit."

"Didi." He did not sit. "You're going to Garh Neshar, with him, with the stone, and you're going to stand in front of the regent's bell." He glanced at Rohan and away. "I'm coming."

"You're forty hours from a fever."

"I'm nine hours from a fever. Nimmi counted." He turned to the healer. "Tell her."

Nimmi sniffed. "He's had two courses and a day of sleep. He'll tire. He'll not die of walking, if somebody puts him on a pony."

"We have one," said Asha from the door, in the tone of a woman noting a debt. "Lalit's coaxing it with a dried apricot. It's a salt-road pony, ugly and stubborn. It will take a boy."

"I'm twenty-four," said Kiran.

"It will take an old woman, then, and I'll find you one." She looked at Rohan and Meera and the stone in Rohan's coat. "The lane road's watched. Varun's twenty are on it, and the regent's riders beyond. The salt road isn't. It comes up the back of the walnut terraces and over two spurs and down the dry side of Garh Neshar, and it hasn't seen a warden in fifty winters." She tilted her head. "You'll like it, Captain. It's illegal in nine places."

"I'll write them down," said Rohan.

Asha snorted. It might have been a laugh.

* * *

They left by the back of the house while the blue was still thin. Pinku stood in the doorway beside his grandmother and did not wave. He held up the walnut on its red thread instead, as though it were a message. Meera held up her good hand.

The salt road went up behind the terraces, through bare walnut trees and out onto a hillside of frozen scree, and the first sun caught the tops of the far peaks and made them the colour of an apricot skin. Asha went ahead with her four. The pony came after with Kiran on it, wrapped, swaying and talking, because he could not bear a silence. Meera walked next to the rope. It was good to walk. Walking made the cold in the hand into something she could ignore.

By noon she was not ignoring it.

Her pack sat on her shoulders like a second person. Eleven winters of habit said not to take it off. She had carried a pack up the Saddle with a cracked rib, and a wet foot, and a boy who wouldn't stop crying. A guide did not set her load down in front of a client. The load was the point of her.

The slope steepened. She stopped on a stone, with her breath going in and out too fast, and put her good hand on her knee.

"Guide," said Rohan.

He had come up beside her and stopped within the rope. He wasn't looking at her face, which she was glad of. He was looking at the pack, and at the way the strap had cut a groove into the shoulder of his borrowed coat.

"May I carry that?"

She opened her mouth. She had the answer ready, the dry one, the one she had given everyone for ten winters. *It's fine. I've got it.* It had been the truest sentence she knew. It had never once been a lie, and she'd said it to the sick and the frightened and the proud.

It was not true now. She could feel the rope against her wrist, waiting.

"The oilcloth roll's at the bottom," she said. "Nothing in it now. The count's in my coat. The rest is rope and a pot and sattu for two." She took her arm out of one strap, and the pack came off her shoulder with a sigh of leather, and she held it out on her good hand. "It's heavy on the left. Mind the left."

He took it. He did not make a speech. He settled it high on his back, over the ribs that were still not whole, and tested it with one shrug, and walked on at her side.

It was a small thing. It was the smallest thing she had ever handed anyone. She walked light, for the first time in years, and her whole body knew the difference and did not know what to do with it.

"Thank you," said Rohan, after a while.

"Don't. I'll start charging."

"I will pay it." He did not smile, and the line of his mouth was warm. "In sattu."

* * *

The sun slid behind the second spur. The shadows went blue and stretched out ahead of them, and the pony's bells rang thin on the cold.

Asha called the halt in a hollow below a lip of rock, out of sight of the road. Her four made a fire the size of two fists, juniper, and nobody complained. Meera sat down on a flat stone, with the count open on her knee and the fire on her right side. Rohan set the pack beside her, and it took him longer than it should have to straighten.

Kiran got off the pony by himself. He stood holding the saddle, swaying. Then he let go of it and crossed the snow to where Rohan stood with the lapis stone on his chest, and stopped an arm's length away.

He didn't joke. He did not call anyone Captain-ji. Meera watched him square his shoulders and close his hand on a place in his coat where a badge had lain until a night ago.

She started to rise. "Kiran—"

"No, Didi." He did not look at her. "You paid for this. Let me."

He looked at Rohan. His voice shook once and held.

"Captain," said Kiran. "I have to tell you what happened on that rope."
