# Chapter 28: Asha's Price

"Price first," said Asha, and she did not move out of the doorway.

Meera got her back flat to the wall and stood, because she would not bargain from the floor. The stone slid under her shoulder blades. The room tilted, righted itself, and tilted again. Rohan's coat hung off her like a tent.

"You have a key," she said. "That's a good start."

"It's a start." Asha's eyes went to the chain between them, then to the red rope under it. "A crown pardon is what I came over the Saddle for. A paper with my crew's names on it." She said it flatly, as if reading a bill. "I stood in the lane last night with my four. I watched the torches go down the line."

"We saw them," said Rohan.

"Then you saw a man with a paper tell forty men to burn children." Asha's knife hand did not shift. "I took Varun's silver because he paid on the nail and never haggled. I thought that was manners. It's just a man who'll never need to pay the full bill." She breathed out, and it smoked. "That pardon is the same noose with a nicer rope. When he's done with the seal, he's done with me."

"So you don't want the pardon," said Meera.

"I want my crew alive. Lalit, the ochre four, the girl on my second rope." She ticked them off on the fingers of the knife hand without lowering the blade. "And I want my name out of his books. He writes every payment in a tidy hand, because he believes in tidy hands. While my name's in it, I'm a hired knife for a traitor. I want it somewhere else."

"A warden can't pardon," Rohan said. "I'll not say otherwise to you."

"I know the law."

"Only the queen can. What I can do is stand before the assembly and say that you came in the dark, with a key, against the man who paid you. That is true. I will say it as loudly as I'm able." He kept his voice level and his hands still. "I can't promise what the assembly does with it."

Asha studied him. "By my grandmother's left boot. You've got a face that doesn't know how to say a fine thing it can't back."

"No."

"That's rare. I'd charge extra for it, if it weren't so inconvenient." She looked at Meera. "And you?"

*She wants a pledge,* Meera thought, *and I am bound.* A promise made on the rope and broken would strike her like a great lie, and she had one good hand left to spend. She would not say a word she could not stand behind.

So she did the other thing.

She put her right hand inside her shirt, past the brass badge, and drew out the count. The wax cover was warm from her skin. It had survived the chain, the iron band and the sentries, who had never thought to look where a woman kept a thing she loved. She opened it on her knee and found the page by feel.

She tore it out along the stitching, with her teeth and one hand.

"Dates," she said. "The middleman. Ten winters of drops, the day and the hour, in my hand. Crate marks: a bell scored through the stamp, then the stamp scraped off with a spoon, and the scrape is always a left-handed one. Where I paid, how much, what I was told when I asked who sold." She held it out. "It's a ledger. It's the only kind of evidence I've got."

Asha did not take it. "That's your neck."

"It's his." Meera turned the count over and set her thumbnail to the spine, where the binding thread ran in tight little stitches. She had sewn them herself, ten winters back, so that nobody would find what lay under them unless he meant to take the book apart. She picked the first stitch loose. Then the second. The thread was waxed and fought her. She worked the third out with a tooth and the point of her nail, while Asha watched and Rohan did not breathe, and the binding gaped like a mouth. A little thing slid out onto her palm. Brass, domed, plain, with a stiff new shank.

"And this. The crate man left it when I paid. His courier was meant to carry the twin. I never saw the courier. I never saw the man."

Asha's eyes went to it. Her whole face changed.

"I did," she said slowly. "I watched Varun thumb a button like that on his coat the day he hired me. New among the pewter ones. He said a pedlar sold it to him."

"Say it plain," said Rohan. His voice had dropped.

Asha said it plain, to him, not to Meera. "Varun paid me. Four silver a week and a promise of a paper. And the lowland man who sold your guide her bark was his runner, a thin one with a bad left foot who drank in the salt-road inns. I've seen the two of them at the same table." She took the page then, folded it twice and slid it into her sleeve. "That's the bill. A name for a name."

"Take the button too."

"No." Asha pushed Meera's hand closed over it. "You'll want it in front of a regent." The knife went into her belt. She fitted the iron key to the cuff on Rohan's wrist, and it turned with a click Meera felt in her teeth.

* * *

Both sentries sat against the lockhouse wall with their hands tied in three nested loops, snug as a bird's nest. The wet cough had a strip of ochre canvas in his mouth and the resigned look of a man who'd rather be tied than asked.

Meera stopped. She knew that knot. Three loops, one inside the next. It had hung in the juniper outside the Rope-Hut with sattu and apricots in it. It was carved on the cairn's inner face, two hands and a rope. *Same as cairn,* she had written in the count, and had not known why she wrote it.

"Asha," she said. "That knot. It's the one on the—"

Asha's eyes came round, sharp as a nail. Not here, they said, and not before him. Meera shut her mouth. Silence was free. She put the question in a column and left it there.

Lalit stood at the corner with his spyglass hanging from his neck and a pony's lead in his fist. A heap of taken gear lay by the wall. He dragged her pack and her knife out of it with his boot, and held them out. He did not look at the captain of wardens. He looked at Meera, once, and then at the ground.

"The lane's quiet," he said. "The red moustache is asleep in a chair. The lieutenant went up the Garh road at midnight, with his twenty. He thinks the village will be ash by morning. He wanted to be seen far away."

Asha's key took the forearm band off in two turns. The iron slid from Meera's arm with a clank, and the torn strip of pattu fell out with it. The cold air touched her sleeve and she could not feel it on the left hand at all.

She put the hand in her coat and said nothing. She slung the pack on her good shoulder, and the knife went into her boot.

"There's a door I want," she said. "Nimmi's house. The woodpile behind the goat shed."

Rohan said nothing. He had never asked her where she put it.

* * *

Nimmi opened before she knocked.

She stood in her doorway in her night shawl, grey hair loose, with a stub of candle. She looked at the four of them one at a time, the way she looked down a bed of the sick. Then she stood aside, and the smell of boiling walnut leaf came out into the cold.

"You'll not find it in the logs," she said. "Pinku saw you from the tree, when you hid it behind the shed. He told me at dusk that the lady had left a blue egg in the woodpile." She sniffed. "Wood is for burning. A shed can come down on it. I took it out last night, after the torches came down the lane, and I put it where nobody burns anything."

The chulha corner. The big clay pot sat on its stones with a cloth over the lip, the dark tea steaming gently. Meera crossed the room and lifted the cloth.

Nothing but leaves and brown water. She shoved her right sleeve to the elbow and reached in anyway.

The heat bit. Her fingers closed on a cord, and then on cold stone, thumb-joint long and smooth, with a shoulder where the cord was threaded. She drew it up streaming. Lapis, the blue of deep ice, cut with a goat finer than any coin's.

"Nobody lifts a lid on a healer's tea," said Nimmi. "They'd think it was a cure."

"It is," Meera said, and heard the shake in it.

She turned. Rohan stood in the doorway with his gloves in his hand. At the shed he had faced the wall and counted to a hundred and sixty, and he had kept his word and never asked where. She let the stone hang from her fingers by its cord, dripping, and held it out at the full length of her arm.

"You'll want that dry," she said. "And on your own neck."

He took the cord. He took it by the very end, with two fingers, and the wet stone swung between them. His hand did not come near her skin. He had kept it away on purpose, and she saw that he had, and saw that he saw her see it. The cord stayed taut between their hands a breath longer than the stone needed.

Then he let her go. She took the cord back to tie it herself, in the warden's double hitch, the knot she had once untied and made again. The left hand lay in her lap, loose, with nothing to give the cord. The right alone made a mess of it.

"Here," said Rohan quietly.

He reset it himself. Two turns round the standing part. A tuck. A locking half-turn. He had it done before she could find a way to be cross about it.

Nimmi was already looking at the coat sleeve, at the way the left arm hung. "Sit," she said. "Lamp."

Pinku held it. He was in his nightshirt with a walnut tied up in red thread around his neck, and he looked at Meera's hand with great interest and no fear. Nimmi eased the glove off.

Meera watched the child's face. He wrinkled his nose.

"Ring finger," said Nimmi in the voice of someone naming a price. "Little finger. No colour. No feeling. Waxy to the second joint, grey-blue at the root, and the skin has begun to loosen like a cooked onion. Middle finger has blood in it. It'll live." She laid the hand back on Meera's knee as if returning a book. "Those two are dead. They'll not be anything else."

"I know."

"Not off today. You'd bleed to feed a Fair. You'll have the hand for the pen, and the knot, and whatever else you're about." Nimmi sat back on her heels. "After the Fair. A day after, no more. If the rot goes up into the wrist, it takes the arm, and I've no redbark for that and no wish to learn."

*After the Fair.* Two fingers. A day.

She raised her eyes then, and met Rohan's.

He was standing very still, looking at the hand. He had asked her one question in the dark, and she had answered it, and he was looking at the size of that answer. She watched him work the sum. She watched it go all the way down and come up with nothing he could say.

He did not ask. His mouth worked, once, and shut.

"Later," said Meera.

"Whenever you like," said Rohan. His voice was rough. "It will keep."

* * *

Kiran was on his feet in the back room, holding the plank wall.

He had put on a coat that was not his and tied his boots with the care of a man not sure he could bend again. His face was the colour of wet ash, and the torn ear stood out against it like a sail rag. His eyes were bright and clear and, for the first time in days, quite sane.

"Don't," said Meera. "Sit."

"Didi." He did not sit. "You're going to Garh Neshar, with him, with the stone, and you're going to stand in front of the regent's bell." He glanced at Rohan and away. "I'm coming."

"You're a day from a fever."

"I'm nine hours from a fever. Nimmi counted. Tell her."

Nimmi sniffed. "He's had two courses and a day of sleep. He'll not die of walking, if somebody puts him on a pony."

"We have one," said Asha from the door, in the tone of a woman noting a debt. "Salt-road pony, ugly and stubborn. It will take a boy." She looked at Rohan and Meera and the stone at Rohan's neck. "The lane road's watched. The salt road isn't. It goes up the back of the walnut terraces and over two spurs and down the dry side of Garh Neshar, and it hasn't seen a warden in fifty winters." She tilted her head. "You'll like it, Captain. It's illegal in nine places."

"I'll write them down," said Rohan.

Asha snorted. It might have been a laugh.

* * *

They left by the back of the house while the blue was still thin. Pinku stood in the doorway beside his grandmother and did not wave. He held up the walnut on its red thread instead, as though it were a message. Meera held up her good hand.

The salt road climbed behind the terraces, through bare walnut trees and out onto frozen scree, and the first sun made the far peaks the colour of apricot skin. Asha went ahead with her four. The pony came after with Kiran on it, wrapped, swaying and talking, because he could not bear a silence. Meera walked next to the rope. Walking made the cold in the hand into something she could ignore.

By noon she was not ignoring it.

Her pack sat on her shoulders like a second person. Ten winters of habit said not to take it off. A guide did not set her load down in front of a client. The load was the point of her.

The slope steepened. She stopped on a stone, with her breath going in and out too fast, and put her good hand on her knee.

"Guide," said Rohan.

He had come up beside her. He wasn't looking at her face, which she was glad of. He was looking at the pack, and at the way the strap had cut a groove into the shoulder of his borrowed coat.

"May I—"

He stopped. She watched him hear it. His mouth closed on the word as if on a hot coal, and he stood a moment with his jaw working. He had promised her no more questions, and he meant to keep the promise down to its smallest corner. Then he said it again, flat and plain, the way a man gives an order to himself.

"Give it here. I'll take the left strap."

She opened her mouth. She had the answer ready, the dry one she had given everyone for ten winters. *It's fine. I've got it.* It had never once been a lie.

It was not true now. She could feel the rope against her wrist, waiting.

"You stopped," she said instead.

"Nearly a question. I thought it best to be nothing at all."

"Nearly's free." She took her arm out of one strap, and the pack came off her shoulder with a sigh of leather. "The oilcloth roll's at the bottom. Nothing in it now. The count's in my shirt. The rest is rope and a pot and sattu for two." She held it out on her good hand. "It's heavy on the left. Mind the left."

He took it. He did not make a speech. He settled it high on his back, over ribs that were still not whole, and tested it with one shrug, and walked on at her side.

It was the smallest thing she had ever handed anyone. She walked light, for the first time in years, and her whole body knew the difference and did not know what to do with it.

"Thank you," said Rohan, after a while.

"Don't. I'll start charging."

"I will pay it." He did not smile, and the line of his mouth was warm. "In sattu."

* * *

The sun slid behind the second spur. The shadows went blue and stretched out ahead of them, and the pony's bells rang thin on the cold.

Asha called the halt in a hollow below a lip of rock, out of sight of the road. Her four made a fire the size of two fists, juniper, and nobody complained. Meera sat on a flat stone with the count open on her knee, its spine gaping, and the fire on her right side. Rohan set the pack beside her, and it took him longer than it should have to straighten.

Kiran got off the pony by himself. He stood holding the saddle, swaying. Then he let go and crossed the snow to where Rohan stood with the lapis stone at his chest, and stopped an arm's length away.

He didn't joke. He did not call anyone Captain-ji. Meera watched him square his shoulders and close his hand on a place in his coat where a badge had lain until his sister took it.

She started to rise. "Kiran—"

"No, Didi." He did not look at her. "You paid for this. Let me."

He looked at Rohan. His voice shook once and held.

"Captain," said Kiran. "I have to tell you what happened on that rope."
