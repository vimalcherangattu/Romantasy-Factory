# Chapter 3: The Second Slide

The gully was wrong, and Meera had known it since the first step.

She stopped at the lip of the upper bowl and put her glove flat on the snow. It sat hard on top and hollow beneath, like a drum skin stretched over nothing. She pressed down. A hand's depth of crust held, then sank with a soft, sick sigh.

"Wind slab," she said.

"Say it so a warden can follow," said Rohan, a pace behind her.

"Last night's wind blew snow off the face and dumped it here. It's lying on old snow that doesn't want to hold it." She pointed up the bowl at the long, smooth shoulder above them. "Your lieutenant says the gully is out of the wind. It is. That's why the wind put everything it was carrying in here."

Rohan looked at the slope for a long moment. He had a way of looking at things as if he were reading them aloud in his head.

"Varun walked it in summer," he said.

"In summer it was rock."

"The face has a cornice."

"The face has a cornice I can see. This has a cornice I can't." She heard her own voice go flat and made it flatter. "We go one at a time across the bowl, thirty paces apart, no stopping, no shouting. Then we're under the rock band and it's the cairn by dusk."

"We cross as you say," said Rohan. "I do not turn back on a guess."

"It's not a guess."

"Then tell me what it is, and I will weigh it."

That was fair, which annoyed her. She had no number for it. She had a feeling in the soles of her feet and ten winters of being right at the wrong moment.

"Odds," she said. "One in four it slides today. If it does, we lose whoever's under it."

"One in four." He nodded, slowly, as if she had given him a price. "And the face?"

"One in three. The cornice is real." She hated saying it. "Your lieutenant picked the lesser killer. I just don't like either."

"Then we go the way I can defend," said Rohan, and went to find his men.

Meera stayed at the lip, flexing her cold hands. Behind her the goats came up in a string, five of them, bells knocking. Jassi walked at their head with his breath smoking and his hood pushed back, because he hated it over his ears.

He dropped back to walk beside her. He did that on a bad slope. He never said why.

"Pull your hood up," she said.

"I can't hear the goats."

"You can hear them in your sleep." She fished in her coat, found the spare mitten liners she had stitched in autumn, and shoved them at his chest. "Wear these under the gloves. Your hands were blue at the last halt, and don't tell me it's your age."

"It's my age." But he put them on, and his face loosened as the wool warmed him. "My sister has apricot trees. Did I tell you?"

"Twice a day for six winters."

"Forty trees. They bloom white, in the third week of spring, and you sit under them and the petals get in your tea." He grinned. "Badami will like it. She's too old for this run, so I left her in the pen with her bell on the peg. She'll walk the whole orchard like she owns it. She'll eat the fallen fruit and be sick, and she'll deserve it."

"Badami has your bell?"

"She has her own. This one's from her kid days." He pulled a small, dented brass bell on a thong out of his collar, and it gave a tinny little clunk. "I take it for luck. When I'm done, she goes to the orchard with me, and so does this. Retired. Both of us."

"After this one."

"After this one." He tucked the bell away, slapped her shoulder and went forward, calling to Sheriff in a voice of deep personal grievance.

Meera watched him go. She counted the goats, because she counted everything. Five. Jassi made six on the slope.

* * *

They crossed the bowl as she had said, one at a time, thirty paces apart.

Jassi went first with the goats, a dark string on white. Then Meera. Rohan came behind her, quiet, one pace of his to two of hers, his eyes on the slope above and not on his feet. At the back, Varun.

She was nearly across when the lieutenant's voice carried up the line, easy and unhurried. "Captain-ji! Strap's gone on my pack. Go on, I'll catch you."

"Quickly," Rohan called back.

"Quickly, sir."

Meera did not turn. Her eyes were on the rock band ahead, forty paces off, where the snow thinned and the slope stopped being a drum. Jassi was nearly there. She could hear his bells, a thin bright clatter. Her breath came in short counts. Twenty-nine. Thirty.

Behind her, down the line, iron struck.

Not a probe in snow. A clean, bright ring, metal on rock, once. A hammer's note, or an ice-pick set against stone.

Then, above her, the whole slope answered.

The sound was a crack like a rifle shot, long and flat, running away across the bowl. It travelled under her boots first, and she felt it in her knees. The drum skin tore open from one side to the other.

"Down!" she screamed. "Jassi, leave them, come!"

Jassi turned. She saw his face, small and white at thirty paces, saw him lift his hand. Then the shoulder of the mountain slid down onto him and the goats, and there was no bowl and no Jassi, only a roaring wall of white.

A hand fisted in the back of her coat and she left the ground.

She flew. She had time to think, with great clearness, that she was being thrown. Then her shoulder struck rock and she was in a hollow behind a boulder as big as a cart, and something heavy came down across the world.

The first slide poured over the boulder and went on. It was loud. The second was quiet, and worse. The snow came in a long dense roll, like a wet fleece pulled over a face, and she heard a sound over her head that was the sound of a man taking it across the back.

Then there was no sound at all.

She lay with her cheek against stone, in the small dark of the lee, with a pocket of air no bigger than her two fists around her mouth. She counted. One. Two. She got to forty before her hands moved. Then she dug, clawing up through snow packed like wet clay, until her fingers broke into cold light and she dragged herself out onto the surface.

The bowl was gone. In its place lay a field of broken white blocks, some as big as doors, steaming faintly in the sun. A single goat bell was ringing somewhere under it, then quit.

Rohan lay half across the boulder's flank, buried to the shoulders, one arm flung out.

She went to him on her knees.

* * *

He was breathing. That was the first count she took, and it held her up for a long moment.

His eyes opened when she cleared his mouth. They found her face, and he said in a thin, careful voice, "Guide. Are you hurt?"

"No."

"Good." He drew in a breath, and his whole face closed on it. "Then I would be grateful if you stopped looking at me and dug for the others."

She did.

She dug until her gloves were wet through, and then she dug without them. The wardens' iron probes were in the slide with the goats, so she used her bare hands and a flat piece of stone, and Rohan used his good arm and swore in the cold, formal way of a man who disliked swearing. She called Jassi's name until her voice cracked. She called it quietly after that, at the places where the snow had a certain give.

She counted strokes. Two hundred. Four hundred. She stopped at a thousand because her fingers had stopped answering.

Rohan worked at the other end of the field. He had found the place where his lieutenant dropped back, a flat patch beside a rock where a pack strap would be mended. He dug there for a long time.

"There's nothing," he said. "No pack, no print, no cloth. The slide came through here and took the line."

"The line goes down. He would have been taken down the bowl."

"Yes." Rohan stood a while with his good hand pressed to his side. "We dug the bowl. We have dug everything below."

"Then he's deeper," Meera said, because there was nothing else to say. She did not look at his face. He had walked beside the man for years.

The sun was down behind the ridge when her stone hit cloth.

She knew it by the colour. A strip of wool, goat-brown, dyed with walnut husk. Bindu's weave.

She dug with her hands. She uncovered a sleeve, a shoulder, the hood Jassi had hated. She cleared his face, and it was calm. The snow had packed his nose and mouth, and nothing else had touched him. Beside him lay Pomegranate and Auntie, both dead, still harnessed. Nothing had found the other three.

His right hand was shut.

She had to work the fingers open one at a time. They were stiff and still warm in the middle. In the palm lay a small dented brass bell on a broken thong, its clapper wedged with snow.

Badami's kid bell.

Meera sat back on her heels.

She had thought she would shout. She had thought something in her would break open, and instead it went quiet, like a lamp turned down. The count in her head ran and ran. Six on the slope. Six, and Bindu singing, and the orchard in the third week of spring. Forty trees.

"Guide," Rohan said, behind her. He did not touch her. He stood a careful distance off, one hand pressed to his ribs.

"Don't."

"I only—"

"I know what you only." She cleaned the bell with her thumb, set it in her coat against the note from Ranu, and buttoned the flap over both. Her hands shook so that the button took three tries. "He wanted to retire. After this one. He said it every crossing for six winters."

Rohan said nothing, and she was grateful, a little, in a hard, ungenerous way.

She made herself get up. First she put her hands into her pack, under the rope and the sattu, to the bottom, where the oilcloth roll lay as heavy and square as a loaf. She closed her fingers on the folds. Three. Twelve packets, dry. She did not look at Rohan while she did it.

The redbark was safe. Jassi was dead. She stood in the blue dusk with her brother's medicine in one hand and an old man's bell against her chest, and she could not make the two numbers come out the same.

* * *

"Your mountain," said Rohan.

He had lowered himself onto a rock, and the way he did it, one slow inch at a time, told her what the slide had done. A hand held his side. Grey showed round his mouth.

"Ribs?" she said.

"Some. I have had worse."

"Breathe in."

He did. It stopped halfway, and he waited, and let it out in small pieces.

"Two cracked at least," she said. "Maybe three. Don't lift your left arm over your shoulder."

"I will not."

"You'll try."

"Yes." The corner of his mouth moved, and it hurt him, so he stopped. "Your mountain, Guide. I am a captain on someone else's ground, and I have lost my lieutenant, and the line behind us is under forty feet of snow." He nodded at the bowl, where the gully they had climbed was now a slope of white blocks with no mark of any path. "There is no way back that I can see. Give the orders. I will follow them."

She looked at him. She waited for the catch, the clause, the warden's hook under it. She found none.

"Leave your spear," she said.

He unslung it and laid it on the snow.

"Leave the tent poles. They're two pounds of nothing."

He did.

"Walk in my steps. Not near them, in them. Where I put a foot, you put a foot, and if I stop you stop and don't ask why."

"Understood."

"No talking until the rock band. Wind carries it and I want to hear the slope."

He nodded once, and said nothing, and got to his feet by the long way round, with a small hiss through his teeth. He took each order the way a man takes a wage, quietly and in full. He was a stubborn man and a hard one, and he obeyed her in the dark all the way up the last of the gully, and never once raised his voice or his eyebrow.

It was somehow worse than if he had argued.

The rock band came and went. Beyond it the ground flattened, and the wind changed, and at a turn of the ridge, with the stars coming out like salt on slate, the cairn stood waiting.

* * *

It was a chest-high ring of flat grey stones, no wider than a threshing floor. A low note ran through it, the hum of the wind in its cracks, the sound the spirits made when they were listening.

Meera stepped over the lowest course and felt the hum fold round her ankles. Rohan stopped at the edge.

"The pair that crosses this ring may not recross it for any hand of the crown," he said slowly, in the voice of a man quoting a text. "Our post archive says as much. It is thin on the rest."

"The rest is short." She unslung the rope. "Behind us the approach is buried. In front of us is the pass, and the Long Saddle in winter is a lie without a line to follow. Nobody crosses alone. A single person loses the path inside an hour, and then the mountain takes them." She let out the red coil in her hands, eight arm-spans of madder-dyed wool, soft as a sleeve. "It takes a pair, with this. Both ends. Both names."

Rohan looked at the rope. He did not look like a man who loved it. He looked like a man who had hanged people for owning one.

"If I refuse, I stand here alone."

"If you refuse, you stand here until the cold finishes. I'll go on and I won't be able to stop." She said it flatly because it was the truth. Not a threat, a count. "I'm not asking you to like it. I'm saying it's the only road with both of us alive at the end."

"And the medicine?"

She stopped.

The question was quiet, light as a cough, and she did not answer it. Silence was free. She had learned that at six winters old, from a mother who kept the family's every secret in her teeth.

"Give me your left wrist," she said.

He held it out, bare, the glove stripped off. The bones were fine and the pulse jumped at the thumb. She looped the red wool twice and tied the knot Bindu sang. Two over, one under, the third one tells the truth. The wool was already warm from her hands.

"Now yours," she said.

His fingers were steady, despite the ribs. He had done a hundred knots in his life, and he did this one precisely, slowly, with the look of someone writing a signature. His thumb brushed the inside of her wrist when he pulled it snug, and neither of them moved.

"The name and where you're going," she said. "To the stones. Say it as if you mean it."

She went first, because she had to. She faced the ring and spoke into the hum.

"Meera. Ranu."

Rohan turned toward the stones.

"Rohan," he said. "Garh Neshar."

For a breath nothing happened. Then the low hum rose through the cairn, climbing, until it tightened into a clear singing note. Pale light began to seep from between the stones, thin and blue as ice at the edge of a lake. It lifted off the cairn in threads. It ran out from the ring along the ground, a faint thread of cold fire, and where it went the snow showed a path she had only seen in the dark of her own head.

The winter line. The mountain's road. It stretched out ahead of them, bright, and went up.

Rohan stared at it. He had gone very still.

"I have seen a hundred cairns," he said. "I have never seen one light."

"You never stood in one tied to a smuggler," said Meera.

He turned to look at her. In the blue light the lines of his face were hard and tired, and the bruise along his jaw had already started to come up. He was the man who would hang her at the thaw, if he ever learned her name. She was on a rope with him, and she owed him her life, and the rope would not let her step beyond eight arm-spans from his side.

"Why Ranu?" he said. "You gave the stones that name. I want to know why."

She could have said nothing. It was free. She could have said *I'm a guide, and a guide takes the work that's offered*, and let the cairn listen for a lie and ring.

She held the bell through her coat, a hard small lump over her heart.

"Because my brother is dying there," she said.

The stones did not ring. The hum went on, steady and low, like a held breath let go.

It was the first true thing she had given him for nothing. Nothing in the world would take it back.
