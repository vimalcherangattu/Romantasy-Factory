# Chapter 8: The Rope-Hut

"Rohan," Meera said again. "Count your fingers."

A scrape answered her, then a cough, then a voice from the bottom of the gully, flat with effort. "Ten. I have been through them twice."

Her knees nearly gave. She did not let them. The rope was still singing against the axe shaft, and every pound of him hung off her two fists.

"Can you see the wall?"

"I see a very blue wall."

"Is there ice you can kick?"

"There is ice I can kick."

"Then kick it when I say, and not before." She looked at the axe. One turn of wool around the shaft would hold a hanging man. It would not lift him. She had been taught, at fourteen, by Bindu's mother, a woman with no patience, what to do about that.

She worked fast. She dug the axe in to the head and stamped the crust around it. She ran the rope back through a loop knotted from its own spare end, tied a sliding grip in the line, and threw her weight on the grip instead of the rope. The tail ran through the loop and doubled her pull. Bindu called it the lazy woman's lift. It was the only way one pair of arms could do the work of two.

"Right hand on the rope," she called. "Left stays down. I'll count, you climb."

"On what count?"

"One is my pull. Two is your boot. Don't think about three."

She hauled. The line came in a hand's width and stuck. "One!"

Below, a boot struck ice. "Two," said Rohan, through his teeth.

"One! Two! One!"

It went like that, a hand's width at a time. She heard the binding of goat hair creak around his ribs. She heard each breath he took come out as a sound he would have refused to make in front of the post. He did not stop. He climbed on her numbers, because she was the only voice in the world with a plan for him.

"Forty-one," she said. "Forty-two. You're past halfway. Forty-three."

His hand came over the lip. She let go of the grip and seized his wrist with both of hers, and he came up over the edge on his belly, goggles knocked askew, snow in his hair and the colour of a candle.

He lay with his cheek in the crust. She knelt beside him and found she was shaking in a way that had nothing to do with lie-cold.

"You're a fool," said Meera. "You're a bloody, sworn, rule-bound fool. I said slow."

"You said walk." He rolled onto his back and looked at the sky through the slits. "I was walking."

"You shut your eyes!"

"At your instruction."

The laugh got out of her before she could stop it. It was a poor, cracked thing, half sob. Rohan made a sound that might have been a cough and then wasn't, and held his bound ribs with one arm while he laughed too, badly, wheezing, his head going back against the snow. It hurt him. She could see that it hurt him. It went on anyway, and so did hers, until she had to wipe her face on her sleeve and turn it into something else.

"Up," she said. "They'll be on the ridge."

* * *

They lay in the crew ledge until the light began to go.

The shelf was as she remembered it: a low cut in the cornice, dry as a cupboard, iron pegs in the back wall. Rohan sat against the rock with the leather slits turned to the floor and told her, with great correctness, that he was not looking at anything. She sat at the mouth and watched four ochre packs move along the bare ridge above, cast about like hounds that have lost a scent, and turn east along the Saddle's lip to look for tracks that were not there.

"That buys the night," she said.

"It buys a part of it."

"Part's enough."

Dusk came blue. The cairn-light woke along the south slope in thin threads, and she took the line down it with the red rope tight between them and the snow squeaking under her. Rohan walked in her steps with his hand on the wool and his jaw set. She did not call it out. She slowed by a third and said nothing.

The Rope-Hut came up out of the dark an hour later, a hump of dry-laid stone, roofed with slate and sod, with its door facing downhill out of the wind. Smoke stains blackened the lintel. A sprig of juniper had been nailed above it by someone dead.

She put her shoulder to the door. It gave on a room barely long enough for a man to lie in, and she stepped in and looked at what was hers.

One chulha, a clay hearth the height of her knee, its mouth black. One sleeping shelf, a plank slab of deodar worn to a dip by forty winters of crossers. And against the north wall, tied in a bundle with hide, the juniper pile.

She counted it with her finger. It took her a while, because her finger was clumsy, and she did it again.

"Forty-one sticks," she said.

"Is that a good number?"

"It's a number. One night's fire is five, if we're mean. Eight nights, and one stick over." She dropped her pack against the wall. "The next pile is two days' walk past the door. We can't burn this one to the last stick and hope."

Rohan had come in behind her and stopped under the lintel. He took the leather off his face in the dark and stood holding it. She watched him look at the hearth, and the shelf, and the stacked stones of the walls, and the two of them, and at last at the sprig above the door.

"Guide," he said. "I should say this aloud."

"Go on."

"A lawful warden may not enter this house. A lawful warden may not sleep in it, nor eat in it, nor warm his hands at its fire." His voice was perfectly level. "What we are about to do is harbouring. It carries the rope for me as it carries it for you. I would be hanged beside you, and rightly."

She lit the first stick with her flint. It took on the third try, a blue lick of juniper resin, and the room filled with the sharp clean smoke of it, which was the smell of every night she had ever been warm on this road.

"Then hang yourself warm," said Meera.

For a moment he said nothing at all. Then he crossed the floor, folded himself down on the dirt before the hearth with his ribs held stiff, and put his hands out to it.

"It seems a sound policy," said Rohan.

* * *

While the fire caught, Meera did the redbark.

It was her habit on every night of every crossing. She opened the oilcloth roll, took back the six Rohan had carried in his coat, turned the twelve small bundles over one by one, and pressed each between thumb and finger to feel for damp. Damp meant clumping, and clumping meant the grey cough would win. She had done it on the pass with her mother's old patience, and now she did it in front of a warden.

He watched her hands. Eventually he said, "May I?"

She weighed it. "One. If it comes to powder, I want it back."

He took a packet in his right hand. A seam had worked loose in the avalanche, and a pinch of dust had come out of the wax. He rubbed it between his fingers and bent to the fire to see it.

His face changed. It was slight. She would have missed it a month ago.

"This is crown-grade," Rohan said.

"It's redbark."

"It is ground fine. Finer than any hill apothecary has the sieve for." He rolled the grains on his thumb. "The lowland guild stores sift through a silk sieve with a ring in the weave, and the mark of that ring is pressed into the wax of the wrapper." He tilted the packet. In the firelight, along the fold, she saw it: a faint round print, like the ghost of a coin. "This is a crown storehouse. I have seen this mark on every medicine chest the queen has sent north."

"Which is not the same as stolen."

"No. It is not." He looked at her. He didn't accuse. He asked. "Where did you have it?"

Her hands were bare and the fire was near. She kept them where he could see them. "A middleman in the lowlands, who sells through my brother's runners. I've never met him. I send silver down the road and a crate comes up. I never asked how he came by it, because I never wanted to know the price." She held out her palm for the packet. "He never told me it was crown stock. I thought it was guild leavings. I swear it."

Her fingers stayed brown and warm in the light. He studied them, and she let him, and then he gave her the packet back.

"I believe you," he said. It was a plain statement, and she realised it was not his to make. The cold would do the believing. His belief was a courtesy.

"It means a crown man is selling medicine out the back door," she said.

"It means that at least." He did not say the rest. She saw him decide not to, and the decision was a weight in the room, and the juniper snapped.

Meera wrapped the packet again and put it in its row. Sleep did not come for a long time after.

* * *

He cooked.

Her hand was already on the sattu bag, with the pot beside it and the water melted, when he said, "I gave back half my winnings at the waystone, Guide. Let me cook, and call it the rest of the wager."

"You didn't win. You cheated. You hummed."

"I did not hum. I breathed in tune."

"Fine." She sat down. "Make it, then."

He made it badly. He poured the flour in before the water had warmed, so that it floated in grey islands. He stirred it with his knife, which was the wrong end of the wrong tool. He tasted it and gave a small clerk's frown, and added salt, and then added a great deal more, from which she deduced that the warden's mess served its sattu without any. When it was done it was a paste the colour of a wet slate and nearly as hard, and he held out the bowl to her with the same grave face he used for oaths.

She ate every mouthful. It was dreadful. It was hot.

"Well?" he asked.

"It's the worst thing I've eaten since I was fifteen."

"That is true?"

"Look at my hands."

He looked. He was smiling, she saw, with only one side of his mouth, the side he never offered, and then he took his own bowl and ate it as if it were a duty.

The shelf was the next trouble. It was one slab, three feet wide, with a hollow in it, and he stood looking at it with the half-formal air of a man at a council.

"You will take the shelf," he said.

"You've cracked ribs."

"I shall be lying on a floor which is dry and sound. You have been shaking for two days. You will take it."

"You'll take it. I'm the guide."

"You are the guide. That makes you the one thing on this mountain I cannot replace." He said it quite evenly. "I am a warden. There are forty more at the post. The mountain has one of you."

She opened her mouth and found she had nothing in it. She tried again.

"The rope's eight spans. Floor or shelf, you don't go farther than that."

"Then I shall be on the floor within the length." He had already spread the pattu on the dirt beside the chulha, and now he lifted it and held it out to her, folded. "Camphor and all. The shelf will be cold."

"It's your pattu."

"It has done more for you than it ever did for me."

She took it, and she took the shelf. She lay in the hollow the crossers had worn, with a warden's shawl over her and her red rope running across the floor to his wrist, and she did not know where to look. Rohan lay down by the fire with his coat for a pillow. The ribs bound with Jassi's cord made him lie very straight, like a stone figure on a tomb.

She listened. She had not meant to.

The breath was slow at first, and careful at the top, and then, when he thought she slept, it dropped. She heard it go long and low and even. She heard a sound he made when a muscle gave. She heard the juniper tick and the wind shift in the slates, and under all of it she heard a man she had been taught to hate sleeping three paces from her feet.

Every line of him seemed to reach her across the dark, she thought, with some alarm: the scuffed boots, the hand on his chest that rose and fell. She had put her palm to his and taken a lie from it. She knew the weight and warmth of that hand. She lay on her back and counted the cracks in the slate, and when she had reached sixty she gave up and counted his breaths.

* * *

Near midnight the fire sank. She rose to feed it, a single stick from the forty-one, and stepped to the door to listen to the cold.

The hut door stuck. She eased it open a hand's width.

Down the mountain, far below the cairn-light and the black fold of the south slope, a road ran into Neshar. She had come up it a dozen winters, in summer, with her pack and her goats. It was a pale thread under the stars, and on the thread there was a row of fire.

Torches, climbing. She counted them, because she could not stop. Nine, then fourteen, then more behind. They went up the road in a line too slow for a march and too tight for a caravan, and between them, thin and carried on the wind, she heard a horn: two notes, falling, the same pair the regent's hunters blew when they ran a man to ground.

They were not Asha's. Asha's packs did not carry fire.

She stood with her hand on the cold wood of the door. Behind her Rohan breathed on, slow and unaware, with the thing in his coat that the regent would pay a lordship for, and that she still could not name.

Nobody on the Neshar side should have known there was a man to hunt. Nobody should have known which pass.

She watched the torches climb until she could no longer count them.
