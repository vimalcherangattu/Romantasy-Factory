# Chapter 21: Six Days

The first thing Kiran asked for, on being alive, was that someone take the warden out of his sister's pattu.

"It's brown," he said from the pallet, propped on three sacks of walnut leaf. "It's Didi's second-best brown. It has her hole in the hem. Why is a captain of the queen's pass-wardens wearing it?"

"Because the grey one is at the bottom of a sack of leaf," said Rohan, "under the command of your healer. Twelve riders are looking for a man in grey."

"And so you came as a goatherd's wife."

"A goatherd. The sleeves are short."

"They are." Kiran's grin came slowly, as if it had a long way to climb. His face was the colour of tallow and a wool cap was pulled down over both ears, but the eyes were clear. He had slept two days and half a night, and Nimmi had stood over him with a lamp and pronounced the fever broken, and then said it again, as if she did not trust it. "It suits you. Don't take it as a kindness."

Rohan sat on the upturned water bucket by the pallet, with the rope running out through the door behind him. Meera was in the yard with Nimmi's second pot. He could hear her counting cups under her breath.

He had not been asked to sit with the boy. He had come in to see that the broth was warm, and found that he did not want to leave. Kiran had Meera's face in a younger cut, with the same quick mouth. Rohan found it hard to be stern with it, and harder to stop looking.

"You'll want to eat," he said.

"I'll want to be sick, first. Then eat." Kiran took the bowl in both hands. "Do you people ever laugh? Wardens? I've watched you at the Kothi gate for three winters, and I've never once seen it."

"We practise in private."

"That was almost a joke."

"It was entirely a joke. It was a poor one." Rohan looked at the bowl, steaming between the thin hands. "I had a warden once who could not be stopped. He sang a hymn at every watch and believed it was a love song. He had no tune. Dilan used to say that the stones had the better ear."

The spoon stopped.

It was a small stop. Kiran's hand held the spoon in the air above the bowl, and his face went smooth and still, the way a pond does when the wind drops. The pallet creaked once. Then he looked up, and the grin was back, a little too bright.

"Is that salt in the broth, or is Nimmi trying to kill me? Tell me it's salt."

"It is walnut leaf, mostly."

"It's vile." He took a sip and made a face. "My sister says you carried water. Eleven trips. She told me on the way in, as if you'd walked on it."

"I carried it badly. The rope made it slower."

"It would."

Rohan let the talk turn. The boy was tired, he thought, and wary of a warden in a small room, as any smuggler would be. A dead man's name was a hard thing to hear from the likes of him. He put it away and fetched the second blanket.

* * *

Nimmi caught him in the lane with a bucket in each hand and no patience.

"The grain carter came up the lower road this morning," she said. "First one in a month. The regent's men let him through because he had only turnips." She set both buckets down on the frozen mud. "He talks. I let him. He says there's a woman at Garh Neshar, a crown surveyor with a brass tube and a roll of linen, who's been shut up in the guest-house since the first snow. The regent calls it his hospitality. She calls it something else, loudly. She has measured the whole yard and says the north wall is two spans out of true."

Rohan did not move. He found that he could not.

"Is she tall?" he said.

"He said she laughed in the carter's face when he told her the wall was older than the regent. Then she corrected him on the date." Nimmi looked at him. "You know her."

"I know her." His mouth was dry. "Her name is Ila. She is my sister."

He had named her to Meera only once, in anger, at the stone fold. Ila was a line in a surveyor's roll. She was a girl on a stone wall with a plumb-line, thirty years old and certain the world could be measured. Last spring she had written to him that Neshar was lovely, and that the passes would be fully drawn by midwinter.

Winter had come a month early.

"Then you'll want to be at that Fair," said Nimmi, "rather more than before."

"Yes." He heard how level his voice was. It was the voice he used for bad news. "A crown surveyor who has watched the regent's court all winter and writes everything down is the worst thing for him if the seal is pressed. If it is not pressed, she is the first person he will make disappear. She cannot keep from saying things."

"Is that a failing?"

"It is a family one." He picked up the buckets, because his hands wanted something. "Nimmi. How many days to Garh Neshar?"

"A day on the road for a sound man. A day and a half for you." She glanced at his coat. "You should have gone yesterday."

"I know."

He stood in the lane with the ash handprints on the doors on either side of him, and the rope pulled quietly at his wrist. Wherever he went, Meera went. He could not reach his sister without her.

* * *

"No," said Meera.

She said it without heat. She was sitting on a sack in the storehouse with the count open, and had finished writing before she answered, which was how he knew she meant it.

"Hear it first," said Rohan.

"I heard it. You have a sister at Garh Neshar and five days till the Fair and a day and a half of road, and you want to go at first light tomorrow." She capped the ink. "That's a good plan. It's the plan I'd make. I'm still not going."

"Then I cannot go either."

"I know that too."

"Meera." He kept his hands open on his knees, where she could see them. "If the assembly sits and I am not in it, Pritam is invested on the claim that the queen is dying. Neshar passes into his hand, and Ila with it. I think he has already decided what to do with her."

"I believe you. Look at me. I believe you." She laid her hand flat on the book. "There are forty-one sick in this village. Eighteen courses. Nimmi can brew six cups at a time, so they go in waves. The first wave is the three youngest and Bholu's mother. They need a third dose, and then a second course for the worst of them, and that's six days, Rohan. Six, to do it properly. If I go tomorrow, every child on the list is half-treated, and half-treated is a very good way to bury someone."

"Six days is longer than the Fair."

"I know what it is." Her voice thinned. "I'm not asking for six. I'm telling you what I'd do if I were free, so you know what I'm giving up."

He had not seen it that way, and he was ashamed of that. He had heard a refusal. She was laying out a cost.

"What can you bear?" he said.

"What can *you*?"

It was not the question he expected. He thought of his sister standing in some stone yard with a measuring tube. He thought of Sunni's mound, and the row of them going up the slope.

"Show me the waves," he said.

She turned the book. She had ruled it into boxes, day by day, with a name in every box. He read it slowly, the way he read an order he was about to sign. Near the bottom of the page the two worst cases sat in a bracket, with a small mark beside them.

"The ones with the mark. When do they take their last dose?"

"Dusk tomorrow."

"And after that Nimmi can carry the rest herself. She has done it for thirty years."

"She hates being told that."

"She will hate it less than a fourth winter." He took a breath around the rib. "One more day, then. We leave at first light the morning after. A day and a half of road puts us at Garh Neshar with one day in hand before the Fair, and time to find where Ila is kept. It is less than I wanted. The road may take the spare day, and I am giving you this one."

She did not answer at once. Her fingers moved along the bracket.

"The patrol," she said. "Sixth day, their rule bites. No one in or out of a marked house without a pass. So I leave at dawn, and by the next night Nimmi finishes my waves behind a shut door, with no one to carry water, and I can't come back to mend what goes wrong. Put that in the cost, Rohan. It's mine and I'm paying it."

"Then I put it there."

"One isn't six."

"No."

"It's the dusk dose for the two with the mark." She breathed out. "I can live with those."

"Then you will give me something," said Rohan.

He had not meant it to come out as a bargain. But it was one, and she set the pen down and sat forward on the sack.

"Name it."

"At Garh Neshar you do not wait at the gate while I go in. You come with me to the meadow. In daylight, in front of the assembly, with the seal in my hand." He held her eye. "I know what that costs you. A smuggler in the open, in front of the bell. I would not ask it if the rope did not make it so, and I would not ask it if I did not think you could do it."

"You want me to walk the seal."

"I want you beside me when it is pressed. You carried it first."

She said nothing for a long time. The brazier ticked. Through the wall, Kiran coughed once and was quiet.

"If I walk it in," said Meera slowly, "I'm in the Captain's keeping, in front of the whole valley. And the writ."

"I cannot promise you the writ." He had known she would come to it. "I will not say a thing I cannot hold. The rope would know it, and so would you. I can only say that I will not take you at the gate, and that I do not know what I will do at the thaw. That is the whole of what is true."

"It's more than I expected." Her mouth twisted. "Fine. Yes. I'll walk it in."

"Meera."

"One day," she said. "I'll hold you to the dawn after. If you pull the rope at tomorrow's dusk because you're frightened for her, I swear I'll put you in the well."

"I will not."

"You'll want to."

"I will want to very badly," said Rohan, "and I will not."

Meera went still. Her head tilted, as it did on the Saddle when she listened for the wind to change. Her left wrist turned a little against the red wool.

"It's humming," she said. "Low. It hasn't climbed."

She uncapped the ink and wrote a line under the bracket, then turned the book so he could read it: *One day. R. promised.*

Then she put out her hand, and he took it.

"Done," she said.

* * *

He drafted the speech that night, by the lamp, on the back of one of Nimmi's tally sheets.

Meera slept on the blankets with her wrist on the rope, curled round the count like a guard round a gate. She slept as she did everything, with a hand ready.

*Elders and lords of Neshar,* he wrote. *I come under the queen's hand, with the queen's seal.*

That was easy. So was the fact of the regent's claim and the proof that the queen was alive. He was a man who wrote reports. He wrote it as one, in short lines, and was pleased with the shape.

Then he came to the law.

*The Closing Law has stood sixty winters,* he wrote. *It was made to keep the passes from those who would buy them.*

That was true. He read it over and felt the old wall of it, solid under his hand.

*It has also,* he wrote, and stopped.

The lamp hissed. What had to come was not hard to know. *It has also kept medicine from Ranu, three winters, and I signed the order.*

He set the pen on the paper and could not make it go.

It was not that he did not believe it. He had said it in this room with his hand open, and the rope had let it stand. But that had been a confession to one pair of ears. This was a sheet of paper, to be read aloud in front of Pritam's guard and every elder at the Fair. The moment he wrote it down, it was evidence. The regent would take the page and say: *the queen's own captain confesses that the wardens have killed Neshar.* The seal would be pressed in the ruin of its bearer. And Meera, who had written the worst of him into her book, would have to watch him do it.

She had trusted the book, he realised. That was more than he was doing.

He wrote, *It has also,* and struck it. He wrote it again. Three times he got as far as the pen touching, and three times he lifted it.

Beside him, Meera stirred. She did not wake. The rope lay quiet, a thing that wanted no lies from him and was not asking for any now.

He folded the sheet small and put it inside his coat, next to the clean-cut end of Dilan's rope.

* * *

The next afternoon, in the grey light, the last of Nimmi's first wave finished their dose, and Rohan went in to Kiran with a decision he did not know how to make easy.

Kiran was sitting up without the sacks. He had walked to the window and back on his own that morning, hand on the wall, and was insufferable about it.

"You look like a man about to bring bad news," he said.

"I am about to bring awkward news. Which is a thing a warden does badly."

"Go on, then. Do it badly."

Rohan sat on the bucket. The rope ran out behind him through the open door, taut and still, which meant Meera was in the yard or on the step, no more than eight spans off. He could hear the clink of the brazier pot. It gave him a courage he had not thought to ask for.

"I am going to ask you to forgive me for something," he said. "Perhaps not now. You are Meera's brother, and the Kothi people are yours, and what I say will not be pleasant for them."

Kiran's face did not change. Only his hands moved, drawing the quilt a little higher.

"Two winters ago I lost a warden on the Saddle. Dilan. I told you about the hymn." Rohan looked at the clean line of the boards. "He was chasing a smuggler in a storm. The smuggler's rope was with his, and the smuggler lived. Dilan did not. I have the end of the rope in my coat. I swore before eleven wardens that I would find the one at the other end of it."

The room was very quiet.

"I told myself it was the law. It was not. It was Dilan." He drew breath. "And I have lived on your sister's mountain for twelve days, and I have been fed, and mended, and written in a book, and I have begun to think that when I find that smuggler I will not know what to do. I have never said so to anyone. I wanted to say it to someone who might know the crews. If you know whose rope—"

Kiran had stopped hearing him.

His eyes were on the doorway, over Rohan's shoulder. His face had gone the grey of ash, with the colour dragged out of it from the mouth upward. He made a small sound that was not a word.

Rohan turned.

Meera stood in the doorway with the water jug against her hip, her hair coming down, a question on her face. She looked from one of them to the other.

"What did I miss?"

The quilt went back. Kiran was on his feet, which Rohan would not have said he could manage, one hand clawing at the wall. He went past the bucket and past Rohan's reaching arm. He went past his sister's shoulder so close that the jug struck the frame and slopped, and then he was out across the yard in his stockings, the wool cap jammed low over his ears, running down the lane on the frozen mud.

"Kiran!" Meera dropped the jug. The rope went tight across Rohan's wrist as she turned to follow. "Kiran, *stop*—"

He did not stop.

Rohan stood in the empty room with the sentence still open in his mouth. *If you know whose rope.*
