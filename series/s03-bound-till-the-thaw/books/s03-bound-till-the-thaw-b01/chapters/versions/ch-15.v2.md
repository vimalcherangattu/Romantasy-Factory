# Chapter 15: The Goat's Teeth

Iron rang on stone somewhere below the second ledge. Three clean strikes, a pause, three more.

Rohan went flat against the rock and raised his right fist. Above him, Meera stopped paying out the rope at once. They had come onto the Goat's Teeth at first light, and for two hours she had fed him down the spine as if he were a bucket in a well. He had stopped noticing her hands on the line. It was the best thing a man could say about a belay.

"Picks," she said, low, coming down the last pitch to the ledge. "Two pairs, by the sound. They're in the chimney."

"They were told the Teeth."

"Or they guessed. Every crew on the salt road knows what a goat can climb." She did not look at him. She was looking west, where the black wall stood on the edge of the sky, no nearer than at dawn and no farther. "Four hours before that breaks. Three, if it's in a hurry. I'd like to be off this rock before it decides."

"Then I had better be quick."

"You'd better be tidy. There's a plate of ice under your left heel."

He shifted his weight before he had finished hearing it.

The ledge was two strides long and a yard wide, with a rock horn at the south end and the chimney's mouth at the north. A man coming up the chimney had to come over the lip alone. Rohan put the horn at his back and pinned his left arm against his side. A warden took the weapon first, the wrist second and the life never, unless the writ named it. He had taught that to forty men. He had not taught it with two cracked ribs.

The first hand came over the lip. Then a hood, and a broad face under it. The man saw Rohan and did not look surprised.

"Captain," he said, almost pleasantly. "There's a lordship on your head."

"So I am told. Come up slowly."

The man did not. A hand-axe came up on a wrist loop in a short, hard arc. Rohan took the haft across his right forearm, turned in under the swing and rolled the wrist outward until the fingers had to open. The axe dropped into his palm. He threw it over the edge and heard nothing land. The man's other fist caught him on the cheekbone. Rohan let it. Dodging would have cost him a rib.

The second hunter came over the lip with a short sword already drawn. The tarred climbing line between the two men, three arm-spans of it, snagged on the first man's boot, and the sword came in crooked across his partner's back.

"Left!" Meera shouted from above. "He's low, Captain, *left*!"

He was already going. He trapped the sword arm against the horn, laid the heel of his right hand on the wrist and bore down over the stone edge. The bone gave with a small, dry sound. The sword rang on the ledge. The man screamed once and bit it off.

Rohan stepped on the line between them and held it there.

"Yield," he said. His breath sawed. "Both of you. Now."

They yielded. The first man sat down with his empty hand in his lap, and the second folded round his wrist and rocked. Neither looked like a man who had been promised a lordship. They looked cold, and young under their beards.

"The ice was real," Rohan said, up the rope. He did not know why he said it.

"I know," said Meera. "I read it."

That was all, and it was enough. For a moment he had stood on a yard of stone with a sword at his back, and a woman he did not trust with his name had told him where to put his feet. He had put them there without arguing.

* * *

He searched the men for knives and orders. The second carried a flint and a clasp-knife and nothing else. The first, the broad one, wore a leather purse at his belt, and in it were three silver pieces, a pinch of salt in a rag, and a twist of waxed paper no longer than a thumb, tied off with thread.

He knew it before he had it open. Powder, rust-red, and a smell like old tea and bitter bark. Redbark.

He turned the twist to the light. Along the fold, pressed into the wax, was a faint round mark, a ring within a ring. It was the print of a silk sieve in the guild stores in Taravan, where crown medicine was sifted and weighed. He had seen that same ring on a wrapper in Meera's oilcloth roll. He had told himself then that it meant nothing, that crown-grade bark could be had by anyone with enough silver.

A regent's hunter did not buy medicine for a sore chest. This was a crown packet, and it was not in a crown store.

He closed his fist on it. Meera was looking down the chimney, not at him. He put the twist inside his coat, against the shirt, and said nothing, because he did not yet know what the saying would cost.

"Captain." Her voice had changed. "Come and look at this, and tell me I'm wrong."

Below the throat of the chimney the second pair had stopped. Two hoods, forty feet down, their picks driven into the ice. One of them had a hand up to his eyes, looking at the ledge.

"They've seen the others," said Rohan.

"They've seen the others went quiet." She pointed across the chimney with her axe. "Look up there."

A tongue of snow hung off the shoulder on the far side, grey-white and smooth as a plastered wall. Along its top lip ran a thin black crack. "That's slab. The wind built it in the last storm and it's been sitting on a skin of nothing ever since. Hear that?" She struck it with the haft from the end of the ledge, and it answered with a flat, hollow note, like a drum with no skin.

He understood. "You can bring it down."

"I can cut it. I can't tell you how far it runs." She counted it out as she would count goats. "Five in six it dumps itself in the throat and stops. One in six it takes the chimney and the ledge and us. And it's what's between us and a fight I'd rather not have."

He thought of the other slide, and of Jassi. He saw her think of it too. She laid her palm on her coat, over the small dented bell, and the hand was not quite steady.

"You were the one who read the gully," Rohan said. "I would be a fool to argue the slope."

"You are a fool. A tidy one." But the corner of her mouth moved. "Take my rope, and keep it short. If I shout, sit down hard."

"And swear."

"I'll do the swearing."

She went out along the north end of the ledge, as far as eight arm-spans would let her, and he paid the line out with his heel dug into the stone. She did not look back. She set the adze of her axe in the crack, leaned on it, and the crack ran. Then she stepped back, once, and kicked.

The slab slid off the shoulder with a long sigh. It was not the roar he remembered. It slid, bowed and broke, and poured into the chimney's throat in a grey rush, packing as it fell. Spindrift blew across the ledge and stung his face. Below, a voice cried out, and a pick rang, and then there was only the settling hiss, and a wall of tight white snow where the chimney had been.

No blue light had stirred. It was snow, and her knowledge of it, and the stone it hung on. He had not thought to be glad of that.

She came back along the ledge with her face grey. "They'll dig," she said. "It'll take them till dark. I didn't want them under it." She took a breath. "I didn't."

"I saw," said Rohan.

"There's a price in it." She nodded at the white plug. "That chimney was our road down. I've shut it along with theirs. We climb back to the hut and sit out the storm, and look for another line when it clears."

"Then we climb," said Rohan.

The two hunters on the ledge had watched all of it without a word. He knelt and splinted the broken wrist with the man's own scabbard and a strip of his cloak, because it was what a warden did. Then he stood, and they looked up at him.

"The storm will be here within the hour," he said. "Your friends will dig through by evening. Get into the lee of that rock, put on both cloaks between you, and keep each other awake." He had no better counsel. It was not enough, and he knew it, and they knew it.

"Captain," said the broad one, as Meera drew the rope tight. "You'll not make the Neshar side."

"Nor you," said Rohan. "Not this week."

* * *

The wall reached them on the climb back.

First came the wind, a clean cold shove that nearly took Rohan off the second pitch. Then the light went. It went the way a lamp does when a hand is laid over it, and the Saddle above them turned the colour of tin and then of nothing. Snow came sideways, fine as flour. He could not see the rock he gripped. He could not see his own hand.

He could see the rope. A short curve of red wool went up into the white, and at its end was a woman's wrist.

"Cairn-light!" Meera shouted. He barely heard her. "Watch for the blue, Captain! It'll come!"

It came. Faint at first, a thread laid over the nothing, and then thicker, a line of cold sapphire running up the spine of the Teeth and across the Saddle's shoulder. He had never seen it so near. He set each boot where the blue touched the rock. Every breath caught on the seventh and eighth ribs like a hook in a sleeve, and his left shoulder burned from the socket down. The thread did not waver, and the mountain, which had been trying to kill them all morning, closed its hand at last round the path and let them walk it.

How long it took he could not have said. Hours, perhaps. When the door of the Rope-Hut came out of the whiteness it was dark already, and the bare nail above it was furred with ice.

* * *

Meera barred the door with both hands and leaned her back on it.

"Light," Rohan said. He was down on one knee by the chulha, unable to rise. "I will do it. You have the flint."

"I've the flint." She held it up between finger and thumb, and it dropped. She looked at it on the dirt. She tried to pick it up, and her fingers would not close.

He saw her hands then. She had stripped her gloves for the axe work, for the slab and the knots in the chimney, and she had not put them back on in the wind. The skin was swollen and a hard, shining red, split across both knuckles. A strip of the haft's varnish was ground into the palm. She held them in front of her like things belonging to another woman.

"Give me your hands," said Rohan.

"It's ordinary cold. Not the other kind." Her voice was thin. "I'd know."

"I know. That is why I am asking."

She did not move. He took off the pattu, opened his coat, and then the shirt beneath it, on the right side where the ribs were sound. The air struck his skin like a slap. He held out his own hands, palms up, and waited. It was the first thing in ten days he had asked of her without a writ behind it.

She put her hands in his.

They were as cold as the stones of the cairn. He drew them in against his bare side and held them there, her palms flat on his ribs below the arm, and he did not flinch, though it was a near thing.

For a moment he braced. It was an old, absurd thing, the way a man braces for a cold plate. If there had been lie-cold in her hands, he would have taken it through the skin and known at once that she had lied to him, and how greatly, and nothing else. He had held them anyway. He found he could bear that. There was only the ordinary cold, slow and honest, draining out of her into him.

"That hurts," said Meera.

"It will for a while."

"I wasn't complaining."

"You are doing it very well."

She made a sound that might have been a laugh. The blood was coming back into her fingers, and he felt it as a faint pulse against his side and then a tremor. She hissed through her teeth and did not pull away. Her forehead came down, briefly, against his shoulder, the width of a breath, and lifted again.

"This isn't—" she said.

"No," said Rohan. "I do not think it is."

She did not say what it was. She sat with her palms flat against him till the red had turned to pink, and the pink to the colour of hands. Then she took the flint from the dirt, struck it, and lit the chulha with a single stick of the juniper. The fire caught and held. The warmth reached her face.

She looked at her fingers in the glow, and flexed them. "Fire warms this kind," she said. "And so does a hand."

"Both work," said Rohan.

"Don't make a speech."

"I had not planned one."

* * *

She counted the sticks by firelight. She laid them in a row on the dirt against the wall beside the door, the one stretch of floor the roof drip never reached, because wet juniper smoked and she would not burn smoke. She touched each one with a finger that finally obeyed her.

"Eleven," said Meera. "One to light. Eleven left, and a night of storm."

He did not answer. He was thinking of the twist inside his coat, and of the ring pressed into its wax. He had held it back on the ledge for no better reason than that he did not know how to hold it. Now there was a fire, and her warm hands, and he had no excuse left. He took it out and laid it on the hearth stone between them.

"I took this from the broad man's purse," he said. "Before the slide."

She looked at it. For a time she did not touch it. Then she bent close, picked it up, and turned the fold to the fire with her thumb along the wax.

"Redbark," she said. She held very still. "It's the guild's silk. That's the sieve ring, there, same as—"

She stopped. She looked up at him, and then, quickly, away, to the oilcloth roll beside her pack.

"The redbark in my pack and that purse came from the same—"

The door burst inward.

The bar did not break so much as leave, its old frost-split giving at last. The whole slab of oak went over on its hinges with a groan like a deodar splitting in a hard frost, and the night came in on top of it, white and howling. The chulha flared and went flat. Ash and sparks flew across the floor. Rohan saw the row of sticks by the wall lift and scatter, rolling and skidding, and the wind took them as a hand takes a fistful of straws and swept them across the sill, out into the dark.

"The wood!" Meera was already moving.

He was moving too. The rope snapped taut between them. She had dived for the door, and he was a pace behind, and the first stick had gone past the sill and was sliding downhill toward the felled juniper, a pale line against the black. Behind it went another, and another, and the snow rose to meet them.

Eleven sticks. And past eight arm-spans of rope, the mountain would not let either of them go to fetch them.
