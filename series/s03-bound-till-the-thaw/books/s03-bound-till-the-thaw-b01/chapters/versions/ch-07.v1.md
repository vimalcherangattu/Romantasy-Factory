# Chapter 7: Other Ropes

"Three hundred and eighteen," said Meera. "Three hundred and nineteen."

The snow squeaked under every boot, the high dry squeak that meant no thaw had touched it since autumn. The Long Saddle lay ahead like a sheet of beaten tin. Glare came up from it and in at the eyes, and by now the ache sat behind her face like a second skull.

"You are counting aloud," said Rohan behind her.

"It keeps the pace. Three hundred and twenty."

"And the number is?"

"Whatever the number is." She did not turn. "Three-twenty-one. Talk if you like. I won't answer till four hundred."

He did not talk. She listened to him instead, the careful sip of his breath inside the goat-hair binding, the long stride he shortened to match hers. At the three-hundred-and-sixtieth step the breathing changed. It went ragged, with a wet catch in it.

She stopped and looked back.

His eyes were streaming. He had them narrowed to cracks, and the lashes were clotted with ice. Tears ran down his face and froze at the jaw. He had not said a word.

"How long?"

"Since the hollow."

"You said nothing."

"I judged it a minor matter." He blinked, and winced. "It has become less minor."

"Snow-blind by noon, if you're lucky. By dark, you're led by the rope like a goat." She slung off her pack. "Writ case. Give it here."

That pulled him up straight. "I beg your pardon?"

"The leather one. On your belt." She held out her hand. "I've seen you touch it forty times. Don't tell me it's empty."

It was not empty. He undid the strap with his right hand and drew out a folded paper, thick and creamy, and she saw red wax on the fold before he tucked it inside his coat against the binding. He passed her the case. It was good leather, boiled hard and dark with years of handling, and stamped with a goat.

"This is crown issue," Rohan said.

"It's a strip." She drew her knife. "Hold still."

She cut a band the width of two fingers, doubled it, and pierced two slits with the knife-point, each barely wider than a barley grain. She threaded a length of the red rope's spare wool through the ends and tied it behind his head with her left hand, because her right fingertips still tingled from yesterday. The leather smelled of ink and old oil. His hair was damp under her knuckles. She did not look at his face while she did it.

"Better?"

He turned his head slowly across the white. "The world is a ribbon," he said. "It is a very good ribbon."

"You look like an owl in a bad temper."

"I have been called worse by wardens' wives." He touched the leather. "You have destroyed a writ case."

"I've improved it."

The corner of his mouth moved. It was nearly a smile, and then he put it away as though it were a thing that might be seized.

She took up her pace again. Three hundred and sixty-two. Three hundred and sixty-three. She looked down the long scoured fall of the Saddle to where it broke into the first folds of the south slope, and there, a very long way below, something moved that was not the wind.

She stopped counting.

"Captain."

He came up beside her with the slits of the leather turned the same way. Down on the white, miniature as ants on a sheet, four figures went in two short lines. Between the lines there was a gap of black rope, and between the pairs another.

"Pairs," she said. "Two bound pairs, on the line. Which means two ropes, a hand's width of cairn-light each, and somebody's already paid for it."

"Wardens?"

"Wardens don't cross. They have no one to tie to." She shaded her eyes with her mitten and counted them, then counted again. "Packs low. Covered in ochre canvas. There's one crew on the lower salt road dyes its packs with onion skin so they can find them in the dark." She felt her mouth go dry. "That's Asha's."

"You know the name."

"Everyone on the pass knows it. Asha runs salt and a little else, and she runs it below Shilak, where the wardens are lazy. She never crosses the Saddle. She says the mountain eats the cheap." Meera lowered her hand. "Unless someone's paying her more than the mountain costs."

"For what?"

"I was about to ask you."

He said nothing. He stood looking at the four small figures with the slits of leather angled down the slope, and it was a long time before he spoke.

"Four," he said. "Two pairs, spaced a rope and a half apart, so that if one pair falls the other is not dragged. No tent poles. No long packs. They carry what a person carries who means to be fast and not to sleep. They have left their lighter goods below." He paused. "They keep their tracks tight, in a single file, and they stop on a count. They stop every few hundred paces and watch the ridge behind them."

"Looking for us."

"Looking for something ahead. Then they move up in bounds." He turned his head. "Whoever trained them knew soldiers. That is a soldier's advance, done by people who have been paid to be soldiers for a week."

"They're closing."

"By a third since dawn, by my reckoning. Your slowing for me has cost that."

"It has," she said, "and I'd do it again." She started walking, fast. "Eight hundred paces to the lip. Then the ridge. After that I'll show you what I can do about it."

* * *

He waited until four hundred. She had said she would not answer till then, and he seemed to be a man who kept the terms of a bargain even when nobody was holding him to it.

"I carry something," said Rohan.

She kept walking. "I'd gathered."

"I told you at the waystone that what lies at Garh Neshar matters to more than me. It is not only a place I am going. It is a thing I am carrying." The slits of the goggles stayed fixed on the snow. "There are people in Neshar who would pay a lordship for it. They would pay in land and title and a hall with a bell on it. I believe now that someone has already offered, and that those four on the slope have been told where to find me."

She stopped so quickly the rope went slack.

"What is it?"

"I decline."

"A lordship," said Meera. "For what, in your pocket? A map? A ledger?"

"I decline." He stood in the glare with his hands open at his sides. "It is free to say that. I would rather give you the free thing than the false one."

She looked at his fingers. They stayed the colour of fingers. A fresh breeze combed the snow between them and drew a thin white smoke across their boots.

She was angry. It was a clean, sharp anger, and she was surprised to find how little of it belonged to him. A man had said he could be bought for a hall and a bell, and he was not selling. He had told her he was hunted and by whom, and what the hunters would pay, and had held back only the one thing she could do nothing with.

"Fine," she said. "Keep your lordship."

"I do not want one."

"I know." She set off again. "That's what's so annoying."

* * *

The ridge came up out of the Saddle like a spine, a long fin of black rock the wind had stripped to the bone. Meera led him onto it with the snow squeaking out behind them, and at the first bare stretch she stopped.

"Here the tracks end," she said. "There's no snow on this rock. They'll lose us for a quarter mile unless they've a tracker. And they'll have a tracker."

"Then they do not lose us."

"They lose us for long enough. Listen." She dug her axe into the crust and leaned on it. "Past the ridge there's a shelf on the south face. It's a crew shelf. Bindu's mother cut it forty winters ago, and it's been used by every guide who ever needed to sit out a storm without being found. From the Saddle, it's invisible. From the slope below, it's a dark line in the cornice that looks like a shadow. A bound pair can lie there while an army passes."

Rohan's head came round. "A crew shelf."

"Aye."

"A hiding place for smugglers."

"For anybody who's hiding."

"Guide." He said it slowly. "I would ask you something. I would ask you very plainly not to show me anything I would be required to report."

She had been waiting for him to ask it. She had not expected it to come in that voice, quiet and unhurried, with nothing officious in it.

"The shelf is a crime scene," Rohan said. "The pegs, the cut, the cache, if there is one: each is evidence of a continuing offence under the Closing Law. If I see it, I know it. If I know it, I am sworn to carry it to the post. I would not wish to be obliged to do that to you." He lifted the leather slits to her face. "You have lost a man already on my account. I do not wish you to lose a crew."

She had not expected that either. The pegs he named were iron, hammered into the rock by Bindu's mother with a stone hammer. The cache was three days of food and two dry blankets. Every guide in the crew had slept on that shelf one night or another, and a good half of them would hang if the shelf were counted.

He was asking her to protect herself from him.

It had never happened before. She had been protected from wardens by distance, by bribes, by Jassi's knowledge of a back stair. No warden had ever stood in front of her and told her how to stay out of his own report.

She heard the wind rattle in the crust below. Four small figures were climbing the slope.

"Rohan," she said. "How many hours do you think I've got?"

"Three. Perhaps four."

"If I take the high route to lose them, it's six. I'd have to leave you out in the open, and you'd go snow-blind by dark, and the first time they gained the ridge they'd have us in their sight." She hauled the axe out of the crust. "If I use the shelf, I save the time, and they don't see us go in. That's all it is. Time. I don't care a fig for the shelf."

"That is not true."

"It's true enough." She nodded to him. "Shut your eyes if you want. I'll walk you like a goat. But I'm not leaving a man to die so a peg stays secret."

"Meera."

"Eyes closed, Captain, or eyes open. You pick."

He was quiet. She watched him think about it. Then he did a thing she would remember for a long time afterward: he lifted the strip of leather off his face, shut his eyes, and put it back, so that there was nothing in the slits to see.

"I choose the first," said Rohan. "I shall see nothing. You will tell me where to put my feet, and I shall put them there."

"And when you're asked?"

"I shall say that I was blindfolded by a snow-glare, and that this is true." The corner of his mouth moved again. "It is a fact I can make good on."

For a second she could not say anything at all.

"Hand on the rope," she said finally. "Walk where I walk."

* * *

She read the snow as she went, because there was nothing else to read.

She told him the ridge was cold-hardened on the windward side and rotten on the lee. She told him the cornice beyond it was old and sound, and the wind slab on the south face would hold a man's weight if he was careful and not hold a goat's. She said it all in small, flat sentences, and he repeated each one in his head, she could tell, because he put his feet where she told him and never once asked why.

Halfway down the ridge he stopped.

"They have reached the foot of the Saddle," he said, without opening his eyes.

"How do you know?"

"The sound. A pair lifts its feet on a count. I heard two counts, then two, and the gap between them is the length of a rope." He turned his head slightly. "They are at a speed they cannot hold for long. They will halt at the ridge to read the ground."

"They'll halt at the first bare rock. They'll look for tracks and find none."

"Then they will send one pair across the ridge and keep the other back. That is how I would do it." He listened a moment. "That will cost them half an hour."

"Half an hour's good."

"It is better than good, if we use it."

"We're using it." She looked down the south face, at the long white fall of the slope and the dark seam in the cornice, so thin she would not have seen it if she had not known it by heart. "Shelf's there. You see?"

"I see nothing, as I swore."

"Good man." She took a breath. "There's a gully before it. A snow bridge, eight paces across. It's good. I crossed it six winters running."

"How good?"

"Nine in ten."

"And the tenth?"

"The tenth is the mountain's," said Meera. "It's cold. The snow's dry. The bridge has had a freezing night to set." She set her teeth. "I'll go first. I'll plant the axe on the far side and take your weight on the rope. You come across when I say, in my steps, not beside them. Don't look. Don't think. Walk."

"And if it gives?"

"It won't." She said it to him, and then to the snow, and then, because the stone on the ridge below them was humming faintly and she knew what it was listening for, she said it a third time and made sure it was true. "I believe it won't."

The ridge-stone stayed low and level.

* * *

The gully was a black slot in the white, twelve feet down at its lip and deeper beyond. Across it lay the snow bridge, a smooth blue-white arch no wider than a door, with the shelf's dark mouth at its far end.

Meera went over it like a woman walking on eggs. She did not hurry. She set each boot, tested, shifted her weight, and counted. One. Two. The snow gave a small dry sigh underfoot and held. At the far side she stepped onto rock, sank her axe to the head in the crust beside the shelf's lip, and wrapped a turn of the red rope around the shaft.

"Come," she called.

He came the way he had promised. He put his feet in her steps, eyes shut behind the leather, and his right hand ran along the rope as if it were a banister in a house he had known all his life. She watched his boots. Each time one went down she held her breath, and each time the snow held.

Four paces. Five.

"You're at the middle," she said. "Slow. Slow."

"I am aware of it."

"Don't be aware. Walk."

Six. The wind lifted a thin sheet of powder off the bridge and spread it across his coat. Seven. She could see the shelf's dark mouth over her shoulder, and the cache, and somewhere on the ridge behind them a pair of strangers coming up on a count.

On the eighth step the bridge breathed.

It was a sound she knew. It was the low, flat sigh of snow that has stopped being a floor. She saw his boot go down and the white around it dissolve, not in a crack but in a quiet, slow pouring, as if the bridge had turned to sugar. She saw him throw himself forward. His hand came off the rope and closed on air.

Then the whole span went, and Rohan went with it.

The rope hit its length with a bang she felt in her ribs. It snapped taut across the lip of the gully and tore the axe's shaft in her hands, and the weight on the other end of it dragged her one stride toward the edge before she threw herself back. She came down on her knees on the rock with both fists around the axe. The blade bit. The wool shrieked round the shaft and held.

It held, and every ounce of him hung off it.

"Don't you dare," Meera said.

She had not meant to speak. It came out of her through clenched teeth, to the rope, to the gully, to the long black drop and the man at the bottom of it. The rope sang under her hands, thin and high. Somewhere below, a boot scraped on ice, and a sliver of snow ran down the wall, and there was no other sound.

"Rohan," she said. "Answer me."

He did not answer.
