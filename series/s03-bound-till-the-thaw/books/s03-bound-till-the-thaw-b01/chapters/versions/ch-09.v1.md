# Chapter 9: Clean-Cut

She told him about the torches before he had his boots on.

"Fourteen that I counted," said Meera. "More behind. They were still climbing when I lost them."

She crouched at the chulha with her back to him, feeding it one stick. Grey light leaked through the gap she had left in the door. Rohan sat on the dirt with the leather strip tied over his eyes and his ribs aching like a bad tooth, and he went through what she had said once, the way he went through a report.

"Regent's hunters."

"A two-note horn, falling. I know it."

"How?"

"From being on the wrong end of it." She did not turn round. "Free to say. Not free to say more."

He let that stand. It was an answer of a kind, and he put it where he put such things.

"Then we have a choice," he said. "Go down on the line this morning, or wait."

"We."

"You are the guide. I am the man asking what the guide would advise."

That turned her head. He could not see her face through the leather, but he heard the pause. He had spent three winters giving orders on this mountain and one winter of that being wrong about it. He found it was not hard to say. It was only unfamiliar, like a left-handed knife.

"The line comes out on the Neshar road at the third bend," she said. "Half a day down. Fourteen torches are climbing that road. We'd walk into them at noon."

"And if we wait?"

"They can't cross the Saddle in a column. Nobody can. They'll make camp under the lip and send out pairs, as Asha's done, and pairs take days to bind and cross." She poked the fire. "So we wait. We keep the fire low by day and watch the road from the door. We go down when they've gone up past us, or split."

"You do not like waiting."

"I like it better than being found."

He weighed it as a warden would, and found that it held. Going down meant fourteen men with a lordship in view. Staying meant a hut with one door, smoke from one chimney, and four ochre packs somewhere above them on the Saddle's lip. Both were bad. One was slower.

"We wait," said Rohan. "Your judgement, Guide."

"Don't thank me for it."

"I was not going to."

"Good." She sat back on her heels. "You were about to look pleased with yourself."

* * *

By dusk the road below had gone quiet and the ridge above had shown nothing. The hunters' fires burned in a long red string beneath the lip, as small as sparks from a forge. Nobody had come to the hut. Rohan had slept most of the day in the dark, and his eyes had stopped streaming.

Meera knelt at the woodpile and counted the bundle with her finger. Her lips moved.

"Thirty-six," she said. "Five a night is seven nights, and one stick over. We can't spend more than that."

"Then we will not."

"We'll be spending something. Sit down. I've got a rule."

He sat. She dropped onto her heels across the chulha from him, so that the fire stood between them like a third party at a hearing, and held up one finger.

"A question each, every night. Any question. You can answer it or you can refuse." She tapped the juniper bundle. "A refusal costs a stick. It goes on the fire as it is, over the five. You feel it in the wood, not in the hands."

"The mountain does not require this."

"The mountain says silence is free. I'm saying it isn't, in here." Her mouth went crooked. "You keep your secret and I keep mine. But there's seven nights of wood, and whoever won't talk burns the rest of us colder."

He saw the design at once, and he admired it more than he liked. A silence on the rope could be a lie in a better coat. Her rule made it a thing with a price that both of them could count. It was a tariff on evasion, and she had set it at the exact weight of what they could afford.

"It is a sound rule," said Rohan.

"Is that a yes?"

"It is an acceptance. I wish to say that I dislike how well it works."

"Good. I'll go first."

She sat quite still, the firelight in the lines beside her mouth. He thought she was going to ask what he carried. He had his refusal ready, and he had already priced it: one stick, and a look from her that he did not want to see twice.

"Who died?" said Meera.

It was not what he had braced for. He opened his mouth for the refusal and shut it.

"You told me you hunted my crew two winters more for a dead man's sake," she said. "I'd like the man. Not the reason. The man."

The fire ticked. The wind moved in the slates.

"His name was Dilan," said Rohan. "He was twenty-three. He was the youngest warden at the Shilak post, and the worst at knots, and he could not carry a tune in a bucket. He sang anyway, every watch, a hymn he thought was a love song." He looked at his hands. "He died on this pass two winters ago, in a storm, chasing a Kothi smuggler."

He had not said it aloud to anyone since the report. It came out in good order, in the voice of a man reading a report, and that was the only way he could have said it.

Meera did not move. Her hands lay open on her knees. Her eyes were on the fire, and they stayed there.

"He's why," she said at last.

"He is why. I would have hunted your crew for the law alone, and I did, for a winter. After Dilan I hunted it for him." He stopped. "I should not have said that last."

"You said it. I'd rather have it." Her voice had gone low and level. "I'm sorry, Captain."

He looked at her fingers. They were as brown and warm as the fire made them. She spoke no lie, and for a moment he could not think why he had checked.

"It is my turn," he said.

He had a question ready, and he looked at it plainly. What are you to the Salt Fox? It sat in his mouth like a coin. He could ask it. She would refuse it, and burn a stick, and say nothing, and he would have the shape of her silence to turn over in the dark. It would be evidence, and he knew by now what evidence did to him.

"Who taught you the line?" said Rohan.

Her head came up. He watched her take it in, and take in what he had left out.

"My mother," she said. "I was fifteen. She took me over bound, my first winter crossing, and made me walk it twice with my eyes shut." A small breath. "She died ten winters back. Fever, in a closed winter, with the medicine on the wrong side of a notice on a gatepost. I was nineteen."

"Guide."

"That's the answer. It's all of it." Her hands stayed warm. "Don't say sorry. You've said it once tonight, and I made you."

"I was not going to say it."

"What were you going to say?"

"That it is a very good line," said Rohan. "The one she taught you."

She laughed, one short breath through her nose, and looked away. Her eyes were bright. Then she picked up a stick, turned it over, and set it on the coals, where it caught blue.

"Five," she said. "That's the night's. Go to sleep, Captain."

* * *

She slept, or did the next best thing, curled in the hollow of the shelf under his pattu with the red rope slack across the floor between them. He listened to her breathing change, shorten, deepen. Then he lay quite still for a long time with the dark pressing on his eyelids.

At last he put his right hand into his coat.

It lay where it always lay, against the lining over his sound ribs. A forearm's length of grey hand-spun wool, rolled and tied with a scrap of its own cord. He drew it out and held it in the last orange light of the coals.

One end was whipped with black thread, tight and neat. Dilan had done that himself. It was the only knot Dilan had ever tied well, and he had tied it on everything. The other end was where Rohan's thumb always went.

He rolled it over, and found the cut.

He tied an overhand in the loose fibres and pulled it tight, then picked it out again with his thumbnail. He did it slowly, so that the wool made no sound. Tie, pick. Tie, pick. The fibres lay together, every one, severed at the same line, as flat as bread under a good knife.

A mountain did not do that. A mountain frayed. Rock sawed a rope, ice chewed it, and the wool came apart in a beard of broken ends. He had seen a dozen ropes that had parted on the pass, and all of them ended in a ragged mess. This one ended like a cut loaf.

Somebody had held a knife.

He had told himself so on the day he found it, in the cold room at the post with the body on the board and the report in front of him. He had stood in the warden hall that evening, before eleven men and a bare table, with this rope in his right hand, and sworn on it to find the one who had stood at its far end. He had meant every word. He had said the oath, put the rope in his coat, and never taken it out in company since.

And now he was lying in a smuggler's hut with the nearest thing to a smuggler he had ever met three paces from his boots, who had made him sattu-bread jokes, and who had pulled him out of a gully on a lazy woman's lift. He could not have said what he wanted. He turned the cut over and over.

The shelf creaked.

He did not move. He had the rope in his open hand, with the neat black thread on one end and the flat white ruin on the other, and he was perfectly aware of it.

She had half-woken. He could tell by the quality of the silence from the shelf, which had changed from the silence of sleep to the silence of someone deciding. Her eyes were on his hand. He felt it as he might have felt a lamp.

He closed his fingers. He drew the rope back into his coat, slowly, and laid his palm over the place it lay.

She did not ask.

He waited for it. It would have been a fair question, a free one if she wished. He could have answered it or refused it, and the price of either would have been small. She had the right to put to him anything she liked. She looked at him a moment longer, and then she turned her face to the wall, drew the pattu over her shoulder, and settled.

She had chosen silence. He understood that it was a choice. No rule of the mountain gave it to him, and no tariff of hers. It cost her nothing the cold would count, and it was the kindest thing anyone had done for him in a year.

He lay with his hand over his coat, and the thing that settled in his chest was not gratitude. It was something sharper, and he took a long minute to name it.

I will have to use it, he thought.

Not tonight. But there was a ledger in him that never closed. It had already written the evening in a clerk's hand: *Her mother crossed. A line taught at fifteen, over a closed pass, in winter. Her mother died of fever in a closed winter, ten winters ago. Motive. Knowledge of the hunters' horn. A reluctance when the dead man was named.* He had set each truth down as she gave it, in its column, as he had done with every truth since the waystone. He had done it as he breathed.

She had given him her mother, as plainly as she would give a man a cup of water. And he had been counting it.

He turned his face to the dark and was, for the length of one long breath, as angry with himself as he had ever been with a smuggler.

It was not fair, he thought. The law was not unfair. It was merely exact, and he had made himself exact in its service until there was nothing in him that could hear *my mother died* and not hear *a motive.* He would have said, a year ago, that this was his virtue. He would have said it in a hall.

The coals sank. Somewhere above the hut the wind moved over the lip of the Saddle and found the stacked stones of the nearest cairn, and he heard it hum, low and level, the sound of a true word.

Frayed, he thought, he could have forgiven the mountain. He had been trying to do it for two winters. But the rope was cut clean. Somebody had held the knife, and the somebody was still out in the snow, and he lay awake under the juniper smoke with his hand on the place where the rope lay, not asking himself whose hand it had been.
