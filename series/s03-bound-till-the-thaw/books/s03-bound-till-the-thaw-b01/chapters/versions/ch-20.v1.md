# Chapter 20: Across the Rope

Nimmi gave them the storehouse because it was the only room in Ranu without a sick person in it.

It stood against the back wall of her house, a plank room full of sacks of dried walnut leaf, so that the whole dark smelled of bitter tea. Someone had swept a space clear. Someone had left a clay brazier, a heap of blankets and a lamp. The ash handprint on the lintel outside was still damp.

Meera sat on a sack with the count open on her knee and wrote Sunni's name.

She had struck it that morning in the choosing list, straight as a rule. That was the dead column of a different sheet. This was the count itself, the waxed book, where she wrote the ones she carried and the ones she lost on the way. *Sunni, seven, Ranu. Third water, dusk. Died before light.* Under it, in the same hand: *Eighteen courses. Forty-one sick.*

She did not write what she thought about the quarter hours. The page would not hold it.

The rope lay slack between her wrist and Rohan's. He sat on the floor with his back to the wall, a careful distance off, with his left arm held against his ribs. He had not asked what she was writing. He had watched her pen for a long time, the way he watched weather.

"May I see it?"

She almost said no. The count was hers, and nobody had read it. Bindu had never asked. Kiran had joked about it for ten years and never once looked.

She held out the book.

He took it in his right hand and read as he read everything, to the end of the line. His eyes stopped on the last entry. He turned back a page, and another, and she watched him find the long run of names: two hundred and six people, each with a date and a price and a village. Some of the prices were crossed out. Beside those, in smaller writing, she had put *free*.

"You charge the rich double," he said.

"The file at your post never says that."

"No. I have read it eleven times." He turned another page. "I was wrong about that. I want it said, and I want it said now, while we are not in a hurry."

"You were wrong about a lot of things."

"I am working through them." He closed the book and gave it back, gently, the way you hand over a thing that might wake. "Thank you. That was not a small thing to let me do."

She put the book on her knee and her hand on the book. Through the wall, Kiran coughed, low and wet, and was quiet.

* * *

"A question," said Rohan. "Yours, if you want it. Mine if you do not."

It was the old trade, the one they had made in the hut with sticks of juniper for every refusal. There was no juniper here and no one counting. She noticed that it had never needed to be counted.

"Yours," she said. "Go on."

"Then I will answer one before you ask." He set his right hand on his knee, palm up, in the lamplight. "I signed the Shilak blockade order. Three winters running, in the autumn, before first snow. The post commander writes it and the captain signs it, and I signed it with a clean pen and slept that night." He did not look away. "Sunni is in my hand too."

The rope stayed quiet. Outside, the wind in the stacked leaf-sacks made a small dry noise.

His palm was warm. She could see it. The fingers were relaxed, the split knuckles pink and ordinary, and the lamplight lay on them evenly. She had been waiting, without knowing it, for the whitening that would tell her it was only words.

She said, "Say how many."

"I do not know how many. I only know the winters." He drew breath carefully, around the rib. "Nimmi says thirty-one dead this winter before midwinter. I could say that is not mine alone, and it is true. The regent's grain, the Guild, a queen who has signed pardons for sixty winters and never repealed one line. I could say it, and each word would be true, and I would be using them to stand behind. So I am not going to say it."

She had wanted this. For three winters, and especially since the gully, she had wanted to hear him say it. She had imagined it in a voice full of shame. This was not shame. It was an account, set down plainly, by a man who knew the sum.

It did not feel like winning. It did not make Sunni lighter.

"That's not absolution," she said. "I don't have any. I'd like you to know that before you think you've bought some."

"I did not come to buy."

"I know. That's what's so—" She stopped. Her own hands, in her lap, were warm too. She had said nothing but true things all evening. "I know you didn't."

"Then what is this?" he said, quietly. "If it is not that."

"It's the count." She opened the book.

* * *

There was a line she had never written a name beside. Near the back, ruled off by itself, it read: *Shilak closed. Three winters. By order.* Under it she had set down, winter by winter, the numbers Nimmi had told her over the years, because someone should. She had never put a hand to the right-hand margin.

She dipped the pen.

*R., captain, Shilak post. Signed.* She wrote the name in the same letters as everyone else. It looked small on the page.

Then, because the pen was still wet and honesty seemed to be catching, she added in smaller writing on the next line: *Carried water, eleven trips.*

She turned the book so he could see.

He read it twice. When he looked up his face had the stunned openness of a man who has braced for a blow and been handed a cup.

"It is the first time," he said, "that I have been written down by anyone as a thing that happened, and not a thing that was done to them."

"Don't get fond. It's a ledger." Her voice was not steady. "Everyone in it is someone I couldn't leave off."

He didn't say anything. He got up, slowly, with a hand against the wall, crossed the length of the rope, and sat down in front of her on the sack's edge. He took her bare right hand in his and turned it over. The knuckles were swollen and split from the Teeth.

"May I?"

"What for?"

"There is goat fat in Nimmi's shelf. I saw it. Your hands are not mending and you have not said so." He waited. "You do not have to say it now. I would like to do it."

She had said once, in the hut, in passing, that the cold got into her knuckles and she never could be bothered. She had forgotten saying it. He had not. She nodded.

He fetched the pot. He warmed the fat between his palms first, so it would not shock the cracks, and worked it into her knuckles one at a time with his thumb. It took a long while. Neither of them spoke. At the end of it her hand lay in his like a thing that belonged there, and she found she was holding her breath, and let it go.

* * *

"I'm twenty-nine," she said.

His thumb stopped. "Yes. I have read your file." A pause. "Thirty-five. You have it, I think."

"I do." She looked at the hand around hers. "I'm saying it because I want us both to have said it. I'm not cold, and I'm not afraid. I'm not looking for a thing to hold on to because a girl died this morning." She stopped. "That's partly a lie. I'll start again."

"There is no need—"

"Watch my hands, Rohan."

She held them up between them, both of them, in the lamplight. They were swollen and red and entirely themselves. "I want this. I want you, tonight, in this room. I've wanted it since the hut and I've been angry about it for days. Is it yours too, or am I the only one who's been counting?"

He took a moment. When he spoke his voice was low and not entirely under his control. "It is mine. It has been mine long enough that I stopped trusting myself to be in rooms with you." The corner of his mouth moved. "But you have asked me one thing. May I ask you one back?"

"Ask."

"What do you want?" He said it carefully, the way you ask the way across a snowfield. "Not whether. I have the whether. I would like the what. I have a rib that will not allow me to guess."

She laughed, startled, and it broke something loose. "You're going to make me say it."

"I would like to do it right."

"Then slowly," she said. "And not on your left side. And I want your coat off, because I've been looking at the back of that coat for twelve days." She put her hand flat on his chest, over the good side. "And you tell me if it hurts, and you don't do the thing where you pretend it doesn't."

"Those are rules I can keep." His own hand came up over hers. His voice was very quiet. "I'll tell you."

* * *

They untied the rope, because it tangled. The mountain's distance still held them, and neither of them forgot it, but there was a freedom in the length of it, eight spans of slack to be as near as two people can be.

The coat came off in stages. The grey pattu. The shirt, which she eased over his left arm herself, because he could not lift it, and then stopped. The bruise ran from his armpit to his hip, yellow-green at the edges, black at the centre where her binding of Jassi's cord had lain. She laid her fingers beside it, not on it.

"Does this—"

"No. Not there." He caught his breath. "That. Yes. That is fine."

She learned his ribs the way she had learned the Teeth, by feel and by patience. Where she could press, where she must not. His good hand moved over her carefully, as if she might be taken from him at any time, and when it found the burn-scar on her forearm he stopped and kissed it, and she made a sound she had not planned.

"Tell me," she said. "If it's wrong."

"I'll tell you." His mouth was against her wrist. "Meera. It is not wrong."

They were clumsy. It was cold, and then it wasn't. At one point she had to stop because he had gone grey and was breathing through his teeth, and she lay with her hand on his chest and her forehead on his shoulder until it eased. She did not feel cheated by it. She felt trusted. He said so, without being asked, in a voice that shook: that he had never been looked after like this, and that it frightened him far more than the Teeth had.

She moved over him, then, on his right side, where she could take her own weight, and watched his face. She had watched it in the dark of the hut, in fever, in fear. She had not seen it open like this. When he said her name it was not the formal voice or any voice she had ever heard from him. It was unguarded and almost surprised, and she answered it, and for a long while there was nothing in the storehouse but the brazier ticking and two people who had told each other the truth.

* * *

Afterwards she lay with her back against his good side and the blankets pulled to her chin. The rope was tied again, because it was easier. A single red line lay across the straw between their wrists.

"I should say it plainly," said Rohan, into her hair. "I have shared your fire since the hut. That was harbouring, and I did it half by accident, because the storm gave me no choice. Tonight I did it with my eyes open. I know what the law calls it and I know what the rope is for." He was quiet a moment. "I wanted you to hear that from me."

"I heard."

"My writ still orders me to deliver the Salt Fox at the thaw."

"I know."

"I do not know what I will do." His arm tightened a little. "That is the truth. I will not say anything tonight that I cannot hold."

She waited for him to go on. He did not. She had learned to listen to the places where he stopped, and this was one. He had not said *I'll find a way*. He had not said *it'll be all right*. A man who had been struck white once for a promise to a mountain did not give them out lightly, and a man who loved her would be more careful still.

She understood it. It was also a small cold place under her ribs.

"Is this only the cold?" she said.

It came out lighter than she meant, a joke with a hook in it, and the rope was quiet. He had gone still behind her.

"No," said Rohan.

She turned her head. His hand lay on the blanket beside her face, open, in the lamplight. The fingers were warm, rosy at the tips, as they had been all evening, and they did not move.

"Look at them," he said. "Whatever you want to ask, look first."

She looked. Then she took the hand and held it against her mouth, and said nothing else.

* * *

Through the wall, Kiran's cough stopped.

She was awake before she knew why. The lamp had burned low and the brazier was ash. She lay listening to the particular quiet where a sound had been, the way you hear a goat-bell stop on a ridge.

It had been going all night, the wet low bark she had learned like a song she hated. It was not going now.

She was up. She had her shirt half on and her boots in her hand and the rope was tight at her wrist, and Rohan, already awake, had a hand on the wall and his coat across his shoulders. They did not say a word. She went out through the door in her stockings, across the frozen yard and in by the back, with the rope running behind her and his footsteps in her ears.

The room was grey with early light. Nimmi's chair was empty. Kiran lay on the pallet on his side with the quilt thrown back.

His chest was moving.

Slowly. Evenly. His hair was dark and plastered to his forehead, and the quilt was soaked through. When she put her palm on his brow, her hand came away wet.

Cool. His skin was cool, and slick with sweat, and under it his pulse went on, slow and steady, like a man walking.

"Didi," he said, without opening his eyes. His voice was a rasp. "You're dripping on me."

She was. She hadn't known. She knelt and put her face against the quilt and shook.

Behind her, in the doorway, Rohan stood at the end of the rope. Two hundred and six names were in the count, and the list of those she could keep had not been so short in all her years. She felt him there, quiet, and knew, without looking round, that it was not only Kiran he was thinking of. Four days to the Fair. A writ in his coat that named her. She held on to the sweat-wet quilt and her brother's breath, and for the length of one slow count she let that be all there was.
