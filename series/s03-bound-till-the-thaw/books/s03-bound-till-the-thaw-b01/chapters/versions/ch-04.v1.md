# Chapter 4: Watch My Hands

"There are three rules," Meera said. "Count them, because I won't say them twice."

She walked ahead along the winter line with the red slack looped between them. The blue light ran at her heels like a stream, and it lit nothing else. Rohan kept his boots in her prints. Every third step his left side told him what the slide had done to it.

"One is distance," she said. "Eight arm-spans of rope. The rope is the measure, whether it's on your wrist or in my pack. Go past it and the heat leaves you fast. Both of us, at once. Go twice as far and the line goes out, and the mountain keeps whoever is standing there."

"And if the rope is untied?"

"Untie it to sleep, to wash, to climb. The distance still holds. Cut it, burn it, it makes no difference. It lasts until meltwater runs at the cairn where we tied it."

"The thaw."

"The thaw." She did not turn. "Two is lies. Any lie you tell the one on the other end of this costs you heat. A lie is something you believe is false, said so that you'll be believed. The size is how much the truth would change what I do next."

"And the price?"

"Small, your fingertips go white for an hour. Middling, frostnip and a night of shaking. Great, you lose what freezes." She stopped there. He noted where. "Fire won't warm it. Only time does."

"A lie about life or death?"

"Kills the liar. And a promise made on this rope is a contract. Break it while we're bound and it strikes you like a great lie." She said it as flatly as she had said the odds on the gully. "Three is what's free. Silence. Refusing to answer. A joke we both know is a joke. Anything you say to someone who isn't on this rope. And the truth, which is the cheapest thing on the mountain and the one nobody wants to buy."

Rohan walked a while. Under his boots the snow squeaked, dry and fine.

"I would like to see it," he said.

"You would." She stopped and faced him. "You don't trust a rule till you've seen the bill."

She pulled off her right glove with her teeth and held the hand out into the blue light, palm up. It was a worker's hand, scarred across the knuckles and cracked at the thumb. The fingertips were pink.

"Jassi hated those goats," she said.

Rohan had heard the old man in the yard that morning, cursing every animal he loaded. He had no way to know what lay beneath the curses. For the space of a breath he believed it, which was what the sentence was built to do.

Then the white came. It rose from the tips of her fingers like frost on a window and took the nails, bloodless as candle wax. She watched it climb with a face that gave him nothing.

Then it gave him something. Her mouth went crooked, and she looked away up the line.

"He named every one," she said. "Sheriff. Magistrate. Judge. He talked to them like a bench and then complained when they wouldn't mind him."

Rohan, who had sat on benches, said nothing.

"That's a small one," she said, with her voice almost level. "An hour. You'll see the white go. Then you'll know I'm not showing you a trick."

"Why that lie?"

"Because you'd believe it. It has to be believed or it isn't one."

"I am sorry for him."

"Don't be sorry. Carry something." She pulled the glove back on over the white. "You've been breathing like a leaking bellows since the rock band. If you're going to be of any use, be of use."

* * *

She stopped on a flat of wind-packed snow and unslung the pack. Rohan lowered himself to one knee, a thing his ribs allowed in stages, and took the sattu, the spare liners, and the coil of thin line. Those she let him have. Then she dug to the bottom for the oilcloth roll and her hands slowed.

"Give it here," he said. "My legs are sound, and the right arm is the better one."

"It's mine."

"It is a third of the weight in this pack, Guide. I am not asking whose."

She held it a moment longer and then let it go. It was heavier than it looked, and he felt a corner of the oilcloth give under his thumb. He did not mean to open it. The fold came away on its own, and the lantern of the winter line fell across what lay inside.

Three rows of four. Small packets, waxed paper tied with thread, each the size of a man's palm. A rust-red dust had worked through one seam. The smell was bitter and resinous. It was the smell on the grain he had crushed between his fingers in the salt-house yard.

Twelve. He counted without wanting to.

"What is this?" he said, though he knew.

"Medicine." She did not hesitate, and he watched her hands as she said it. The fingertips were already white, and he saw what that meant. For this hour they could not testify to anything. She could say what she liked, and her body would keep no record.

He noted that she had to know it. He noted that she answered anyway.

"Redbark. For the grey cough, in Ranu," she said. "Forty-one sick there. Nine of them under ten. Six already buried. That's from a note I carry, and I've no reason to doubt the man who wrote it." She met his eyes. "They're not for sale, and not for the Guild. And they are not for you. I will walk off this mountain before you take them."

"At eight arm-spans, I would be walking with you."

"I've done the arithmetic."

It was nearly a joke. Perhaps it was one. She did not smile.

"The law is plain," Rohan said. His voice came out as it did in council, which he disliked. "A load carried over a closed pass is seized where it is found. The carrier answers to the rope. At the thaw, if I am to be exact. I will not say otherwise to you because you can hear me say it."

"Then arrest me at the thaw, Captain." Her chin lifted. "Today you carry half."

She was right. That was the part he could not get around. He had a lapis seal in his coat, a deadline, and a broken side, and the only road to the first lay through a woman who wanted the second dead. He could hold the law up in front of her like a lamp. It would not carry him one step along the line.

He had also seen the gully go. He did not intend to forget what it had taken, or how the first order of the night had come from her mouth and the second had come from his.

"Are you the Salt Fox?" he said.

Her face did not change. The rope lay slack on the snow. She stood in the blue light, perfectly still, and said nothing at all.

It cost her nothing. That was the thing. Silence was free, she had told him so herself, and he had just been given a demonstration of how free.

"Silence is an answer, Guide," Rohan said. "Watch my hands, and I will watch yours."

He meant it to land as a threat, and it did. She had the grace to let him see it hit.

"Mine are white," she said.

"For the hour."

"Then you've an hour's grace." She turned her back on him and shouldered the empty pack. "Count the packets into your coat, Captain. I'll show you where the seam is dry."

Rohan counted six into the inside of his coat, below the collar where the queen's seal lay sewn in its seam, and he did not look at her while he did it. He was a warden holding contraband. That was evidence, in custody, carried in the open by the proper officer, chain unbroken. He built that story with great care and could see through it at once.

The truth stood plainer. The Closing Law gave no word for a man who carried a smuggler's medicine across a closed pass for her. It had one word for a man who sheltered, fed or aided an unlawful crosser, and the rope for that word was the same length as hers.

He had walked into it with his eyes open and a broken side. He did not feel noble about it. He felt the way a man feels at the edge of a frozen river, having decided the ice will hold.

* * *

They went on in the blue light. The wind came in long, patient pushes off the Saddle, and the ridge on their left stood against the stars like a row of broken teeth.

"I'll bet you," Meera said, "that you can't keep your mouth shut for an hour."

It was the first thing she had said in some time. Rohan said nothing, which was his answer.

"My mouth is generally shut, you'll say."

He said nothing.

"Name the stake, then. I'll name it. The sattu at the next halt. Winner eats the loser's share."

He nodded once.

"That's a yes. That counts as a yes." She seemed pleased to have caught him at it. "Your hour starts when I say, and I say now."

He walked.

She began with the weather, and the state of his boots, and the vanity of lowland cloth. She counted aloud in fours and fives, and then in a language of her own, a sing-song of knot-numbers that ended on a rising note as if it were a question. She asked what a warden did for supper. She asked whether he'd ever been laughed at. She asked, with some skill, whether it was true that wardens' wives only married men they could outrank in the dark. He walked, and the snow squeaked, and Rohan gave her nothing.

He had spent three winters not answering the questions of smugglers' wives and the families of the hanged. He had stood in the doorways of widows. A woman talking at him on a mountain, with the wind for company, was nearly restful.

The fingers of her right glove had no colour in them at the start. By the middle of the hour she took the hand out and flexed it, and he saw the nails had gone from wax to the pale of a tea-rose. The white was leaving.

She talked on. After a time she stopped asking things and only talked, about the knots Bindu sang, and the way goats looked at weather, and a lowland middleman who sold her bad lamp oil. Her voice had a drive in it. It was not quite a joke, and the jokes had thinned out of it a while ago.

He understood, somewhere past the middle of the hour, that she was not playing to win. She was keeping her mouth full of noise so that the quiet could not get in. He did not stop her. It seemed to him a poor use of his silence to spend it on something she needed more.

When her fingertips were pink again, as plain as the day she was born, he said, "The hour is done."

She stared at him. Then she stared at her own hand, and then at the sky, as if the stars might be called to appeal.

"You could have *twitched*," she said.

"I am not given to it."

"I called you a stone with a hat on."

"You did. It was well put."

"I hate this." She shook the red slack in disgust. "That's three hundred winters of rope-craft and I lose the first wager to a man who'd rather be a post."

"The sattu, Guide."

"Yours is on the way," she said between her teeth, and he saw her struggle with the shape of a smile and lose. He had won a ration and a small, ridiculous victory, and for some reason it did not feel like either.

"I will collect it at the halt," he said, with very great gravity.

She made a sound that might have been laughter if it had not been shaken with something else. She turned her face from him, and he let her.

* * *

Near the end of the night the line brought them to a waystone.

It stood at the foot of the climb to the Long Saddle, a squat grey pillar as tall as a child, furred with lichen on its north side. At its base lay a shelf of flat stones left by earlier crossers. Beyond it the ground rose, white and long, toward a sky going from black to the colour of iron.

Meera slowed. The rope between them drew, and then slackened as he came up beside her.

She did not speak. She went down on her heels at the foot of the waystone and began to choose stones. She took three off the shelf, each flat as a hand, and set the first on the packed snow in front of her. She set the second across it. She worked in the way he had seen her work on the ice, exact and without wasted motion, and he realised that she was building something that would have to stand.

"Forty winters," she said to the stones. "Ten of them with me on the Saddle. Seven goats in his time, and the last five of them the best." She laid the third stone on the pile. Her voice was nearly steady. "He said he was retiring."

That was all. She did not weep. She took the dented bell out of her coat, looked at it, and put it back.

Rohan stood a pace behind her. He had known the man two days. He had stood in the yard and heard him say *no* to the Crown's captain with a hay fork in his hand, and he had seen him laugh once, over a goat. He had not liked him particularly, and the loss ran through him all the same, the plain cold fact of a man who had been walking in the dark and was not.

Dilan had no cairn. They had brought what the ice gave up to the post and buried it in a wardens' row, and there was no mountain stone over it. There was a rope end in the lining of Rohan's coat, cut clean, that he had never put down.

He peeled the glove from his right hand.

The cold took his fingers at once, the wind getting into every joint. He chose a stone from the shelf, a small grey one worn smooth by someone else's hand, and bent over her shoulder, ribs and all, and set it on the top.

It sat. He did not take his hand away at once.

Meera did not look up. She was watching his fingers where they rested on the stone, bare in the thin light. They were reddening, and they were steady.

He let her.
