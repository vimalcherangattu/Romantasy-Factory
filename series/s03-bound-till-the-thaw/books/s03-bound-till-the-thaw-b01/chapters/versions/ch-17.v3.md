# Chapter 17: Below the Snowline

Rohan had her pack on his right shoulder and one boot laced before she opened her eyes.

The storm had gone out in the dark. He had felt it go, the way a man feels a fever break, and had lain on the sleeping shelf listening to the quiet until grey light came through the door seam. Now the hut smelled of cold ash and wet wool, and Meera lay with the red rope across her chest, one hand curled by her face.

She woke all at once, as she always did. Her eyes went to the pack, then to him.

"Put it down."

"It is forty pounds, and you carried it through the Teeth. My right arm is sound." He kept his voice reasonable. "Let me take the door as well. Let me kick the drift clear and test the sill before you put a foot on it."

"Captain."

"A cornice can sit against a door. I would rather learn that with my boot."

"Rohan." She sat up. It was the first time she had used the name when she meant to be heard, and he stopped with the strap in his fist. "Put it down. Your promise doesn't make you my guide."

"I did not say it did."

"You're doing it with a pack on your shoulder." She pulled her boots toward her. "I heard you last night. The rope heard you. You'll be under me if I fall. That's a promise about falling. It isn't about where I put my feet."

He set the pack on the shelf. It cost him something, and he was surprised by how much. For ten days he had reached for the downhill place on the rope and for the first step through every door, and each time she had stopped him, with a look and nothing more. It was not vanity. It was the oldest rule of his trade, and it was what *safe* meant to him.

"If you fall because I let you lead—" he began.

"Then I fell on my own line, and you caught me. That's the whole bargain." She laced up with fingers that obeyed her again. "Two sticks for the morning. I'm spending one on the sattu and keeping one. Eat."

They ate. Outside, when Meera drew the door and stepped through first, and he let her, the world had been scoured. Every slope was clean and blue-white, and the Saddle's lip stood against a sky so hard it looked glazed. She stood on the sill with her eyes narrowed to slits behind the strip of boiled leather she had cut from his writ case for goggles.

"The chimney's plugged," she said. "So we take the winter line itself. Down the south slope to the Neshar road, third bend. Half a day, if I read the snow right, and I will."

"And the hunters?"

"Digging. They'll come out onto the Teeth, find nobody, and cast east. I'd cast east." She tested the rope between them with a flick of her wrist. "Behind me, Captain. Uphill of me, always. Where you can sit down on the line."

Behind. Uphill. He saw why she wanted it. A man below her could only fall with her. A man above her could be an anchor. His reach for the downhill side had been an annoyance to her yesterday. With his promise humming in the wool, it was a danger.

He walked behind.

For an hour it was easy, and he hated it. She picked her way down the long slope in short, flat steps, stopping to probe with the axe haft. He wanted to call out every hollow she was already stepping around. He bit it back and watched the back of her neck, the stray black hair freezing against her collar.

Where the slope rolled over into a shallow bowl, he saw a clean, smooth stretch to the right, an easy shortcut, and his body began to take it. One stride off her line, and he had a good view of the way. He felt the rope go slack in his favour.

Three short pulls.

He stopped with his weight forward. Meera did not turn her head.

"Look at your left boot," she said.

He looked. The snow under it was hard and pale, and it seemed sound. She came back up the rope, knelt, and tapped the surface once with her axe. A square of crust the size of a door sank away with a soft, sighing sound, and under it was a black hollow where wind had undercut the slope. The hollow went down further than the haft was long.

"You'd have gone through to the hip," said Meera. "And I'd have gone after you, because that's how a rope works." She said it without heat. "That's not a lesson. It's a cost. You're paying part of it now by standing still when you want to be ahead."

"I am paying," said Rohan. His voice was not quite steady. "I had not understood the price was this."

"What did you think it was?"

"That I would be the one at risk. In your place." He stepped back onto her line, in her footprints, exactly. "I see now that keeping it means doing what you tell me. I had not thought of it as obedience."

"It's not obedience." She stood and brushed snow off her knees. "It's being on the same rope. I'd do the same for you if you were the one who knew the ground."

"Would you?"

"Ask me again when you do."

She went on. He followed, and it was not as hard the second time.

* * *

They stopped at noon in the lee of a boulder, where the sun had melted a hand's breadth of rock bare. Meera sat with her back to it and chewed sattu without tasting it. He could tell by how she held her jaw. When she spoke, she did not look at him.

"I want to ask you something I already know the answer to."

"Ask."

"You hold the writ." She pulled up a knee. "It names the Salt Fox. If you put me down in your report as dead on the mountain, or never found, or not me, can you do that? Or tear the name off the paper?"

It was the most dangerous question she had asked him, and she had asked it softly, with her bare hands on her knee where he could see them. He did not take long to answer, because the answer was not in doubt. He took long because he did not want it to be true.

"No," said Rohan.

Her fingers did not whiten. She had known he would say it.

"A warden cannot pardon," he said. "Not a captain, not the judge at Taravan, not the Salt Guild. I can arrest you and hold you. I can delay, and I can argue. I cannot unwrite a name once the crown has it. Only the queen can, with her own hand."

"Then you could tell the judge I was never the Salt Fox."

"I could. It would be a lie, and I would have to say it to a man who has never taken my word on less." He spoke slowly. "And it would not hold. Your crew know you. The paper would only say that I was a liar."

"So it's the queen."

"It is the queen. I would carry you to her myself, if she were nearer." He heard what he had said, and did not take it back. "I cannot make the law bend for you, Meera. I can only walk you to the one person who can."

She was quiet. The wind moved a skin of snow across the rock.

"That's the worst true thing you've said," she said at last. "And you said it while I could see your hands."

"Yes."

"I think I liked it better when you were just the man with the noose." She huffed out a breath that was nearly a laugh. "At least then I knew where to stand."

"You still do." He held his hands out, palms up, and they were warm. "Here. Where I can see you."

She looked at the hands for a long moment. Then she turned her face away, toward the valley, and he knew the conversation was finished and that it had moved something.

* * *

It was the stone fold that undid him.

They had come down to where the Saddle's snow broke up into scree and stunted thorn. A herdsmen's track crossed below them, and beside it a low wall of piled stone stood like an animal fold. Rohan knew it. He had walked this trail at twelve years old with his father's hand on his collar. Two nights before the crossing he had stood over the post table with the ruler held flat on a sheet of the post's good paper, and watched Varun's neat hand draw this very wall along its edge. *Captain-ji, you've forgotten the shrine,* Varun had said, and added it.

Rohan had not forgotten the shrine. Varun had only wanted it on the sheet.

He walked past the fold, and the thing came up through his chest without warning, so fast he could not shut it. Ten years. Varun had held his horse at the Taravan gate when Rohan was newly captain and sick with nerves. Varun had stood at Dilan's grave in the wardens' row with a skin of wine and said the wrong funny thing, and made the whole hall laugh when nobody could breathe. Varun had *sat with him*. He had sat in the dark of the post room on the night Rohan first read Dilan's report, saying nothing, and Rohan had thought: *this is what a friend is.*

And the pick had struck stone, and Jassi, forty winters old, was dead.

Rohan stopped. His fist went into the nearest slab of the fold before he knew he'd closed it. It split his knuckles. He hit it again, and the rope jerked at Meera's wrist.

"Rohan." She did not shout.

"He asked me about my sister." His voice was strange. "At the table, before we left. Whether Ila was safe in Neshar through the winter. He had a face like an honest man worried for her. He was already counting what I'd sell for."

"I know."

"You do not. You never trusted him." He pulled the hand back, bloody to the second joint, and found he did not care. "I stood at his *wedding*. I would have put my own neck in his hands. And I hope he is under that snow. I hope he is still under it, and that he woke, and knew."

It sat between them in the cold, ugly and entire. He waited for her to say something merciful.

She came back up the line and took his bleeding hand in both of hers, and turned it to the light. "That's a true thing," she said. "Don't take it back."

"I should not feel it."

"Why not? It's free." She pressed snow into the split knuckles, hard, and he hissed. "Jassi named every goat he ever owned. Auntie was the one who kicked. I'd like Varun to know that, before he's done." Her eyes were dry and fierce. "Be angry, Captain. I'll hold the rope."

He breathed. His chest was heaving, and his ribs ached like a stitched wound. Under the anger there was grief for the friend he had had, and he let that show too, since there was no one to see it but her.

When he was done, she let go of his hand and walked on. He fell in behind her, exactly in her steps.

* * *

The birches began at the third bend, thin white trunks standing in snow to their knees. Below them the trees changed. The bark grew rough and dark and the branches thickened into broad, heavy arms, bare now, and the slope turned to long terraces of walnut, stepping down toward a hollow in the hills.

He smelled it first. A green, bitter steam, thick as tea, hanging in the cold air from a hundred chimneys at once.

"Walnut leaf," said Meera. Her voice had gone odd. "They boil it for the lungs. Every house, every day." Her stride lengthened, and the rope drew him after her. "That's Ranu."

Then he heard the coughing.

It came through timber walls. It was not one cough, but many, a low rasp behind every door and shutter, wet and dry, near and far, a child's high bark answered by an old man's deep one. The sound never stopped. It simply moved from house to house, like a bell passed hand to hand.

The track dropped out of the trees onto the open shoulder above the village. Rohan saw timber roofs, black with damp, and the pale threads of smoke. And on the slope beside the track, in frozen ground, he saw the mounds.

He stopped. He had not meant to.

They lay in rows along the contour of the hill, each one a hump of earth the colour of rust where the spades had cut through the crust. Stones were piled on some. A bit of rag was knotted to a stick on another. The newest had no marker at all, only a scatter of dark clods across the snow.

Rohan had buried seven wardens. He knew how to count a grave. He started at the top corner and walked the first row slowly, and Meera did not stop him. Eight. Nine. A long mound, and a long one, and then one that was no longer than his own forearm and a half. He heard her breath catch. The next was the same length, and the one after, and he understood what the little ones were, and kept counting, because stopping would have been a way of looking away.

She was counting too. He heard it under her breath, the way she counted goats. Her hand had gone to her coat, over the bell.

The second row. The third. The ground was so hard here that the diggers had left stones at the edge of every cut, heaped up in small cairns of their own. Some mounds had fresh pine boughs laid across. Some had a toy: a carved goat, a spindle, a crooked wooden horse.

"Twenty-six," he said, and his voice came out thin and strange. "Twenty-seven."

He finished the row. He turned and came back along the lowest terrace, and the last of them were barely a hand's width apart, as if the diggers had given up the idea of space.

Thirty-one.

There was a shovel in the ground at the end of the row. A woman stood behind it, thick in the shoulder, wrapped in a shapeless brown pattu with its hem stiff with frozen earth. She was fifty perhaps, with iron-grey hair scraped back under a cloth and a mouth set hard as a ledge. She had been digging. Her breath hung in front of her in short, hard clouds, and beside her feet the raw earth was opened to a depth of barely a forearm. It was the right length for a child.

She looked at Meera's rope, the wool still red under its crust of ice. She looked at the grey pattu on Rohan's shoulder and at the warden's knot at his collar.

"Nimmi," said Meera. Her hand had gone to the pack, flat over the oilcloth roll. "Kiran, is he—I have it, twelve packets, near enough, it's here—"

"I know who you are." Nimmi's voice was flat as a plank. She did not look at the pack, or at Meera. She did not look away from Rohan. "Breathing. That keeps." She drove the shovel in again and left it standing. "Blockade dead. Thirty-one this winter, and it's not yet midwinter. I've buried most of them myself. You're the captain who signs it?"

He could have said that the post signed it. He could have said the warden-judge, or the queen, or the Closing Law itself, written sixty winters before he was born. He could have said that he had only held the line. Every one of those was true. The rope on his wrist lay quiet. Nothing in his hands would have moved.

The smell of boiled leaf sat in his throat. The coughing went on behind him, and thirty-one counted graves lay at his back, belonging to people whose names he did not know. He said the one thing that was entirely his.

"Yes."

His hands stayed bare at his sides. He did not hide them.
