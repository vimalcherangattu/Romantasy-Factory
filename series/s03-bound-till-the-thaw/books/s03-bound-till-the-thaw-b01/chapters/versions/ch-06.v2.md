# Chapter 6: Taking the Cold

"Are your ribs broken, Captain? Not hurt. Broken."

Meera asked it without turning round, the way she called out a distance. She was three paces up the pitch with the red slack looped between them, and she had stopped to wait for him for the fourth time since midnight.

Rohan had counted the stops. He had also counted the hours of the night they had left, and the days of the twenty, and he had come to a figure he did not like.

The slope above the waystone was scoured to blue ice in places and drifted in others. The blue line had died with the first grey in the east. His left side felt as if a hand were closing slowly on a wet cloth. He had not raised the arm above his shoulder since the slide, and he had gone to some trouble to make that look like choice.

"I saw your fingers last night," she said. "The white. I counted to six and let it go. A small one, I thought, and he's a proud man, and it's no business of mine." She turned then. Her face was raw with wind. "This is my business. If you slow on the Saddle, we lose the day, and Ranu hasn't got days. So I'm asking plainly."

The truth would cost him. She would halt them. She would build a shelter and sit him in it. She would take a day, perhaps two, and he had seventeen left and a regent counting them for him.

He weighed it as he weighed everything. A middling lie would bring frostnip and a night of shaking. He had been cold before. A lost day was a day of the grey cough in Ranu, and that was a debt he could not carry. His hands could pay for the first. Nothing he owned could pay for the second. It was a poor sum, and he knew it was poor, and he did it anyway.

"They are fine," said Rohan.

It was the plainest sentence he had spoken since the cairn, and it was false in every word.

He felt it land. A bite went into his right hand, along the knuckles, as if he had plunged it into a stream in thaw. He looked down. The grey was spreading under the skin, from the second joint toward the wrist, bloodless and patchy. The glove had not hidden it, because the cold was under the glove.

He closed the hand. It did not want to close.

Meera watched him do it. She did not say a word. She pulled off her right glove with her teeth, as she had done for her demonstration, and held out her hand.

"Give me your hand."

"Guide—"

"The right one. Take the glove off."

He did not know what she meant to do. The rules of the rope were hers, and she had recited them, and this had not been one. A man who has just been caught does not argue about terms. He peeled the glove from his hand and put the hand in hers.

Her palm was warm. That was all he understood, for the space of a breath. A rough, dry palm with a callus at the base of the thumb, closing over the back of his hand so that her fingers lay along the grey.

Then the cold left him.

It went out through his skin like a tide drawn off a flat shore. The ache in his knuckles thinned and ran to nothing. Colour came back into the hand in a rush of pins. He saw it go from him into her. It showed as a rime on her fingers, a pallor that crept past her wrist and whitened the nails one by one.

Her face did not change, but her breath did. It went short and flat.

"Middling," she said. "That was a middling lie, Captain."

"What have you done?"

"The loophole." Her teeth had begun to chatter, and she clamped them and spoke through them. "The one who's lied to can take it. Skin to skin. It passes over. The liar keeps their hands, and the other one pays." She let go of him. Her fingers shook so hard that she tucked them into her armpit, and the shaking went on in her shoulders. "I know it was a lie. I know how big. I don't know the truth of it. That's the price of taking it, you see. You can't spare someone and not know they lied."

He stood with his restored hand open in front of him. He had spent three winters hunting smugglers, and two had hanged from the deodar. None of them had ever done him a kindness in return.

"You did not have to," he said.

"No. I could've let you carry it. You'd have had frostnip to the wrist and a night of the shakes, and I'd have had a captain who couldn't hold an axe." She was blowing on her hands, which did nothing. "I'm not being good. I'm being practical."

"You are doing both."

"Don't," said Meera. "Listen. This is how it goes now. We go slow, and you tell me true, or I take it every time. I will take it every time, Rohan. So don't make me."

It was the first time she had used his name. He doubted she had noticed. She was too cold to notice anything.

"Now," she said. "The question again."

He understood that she meant him to answer it properly.

"Two ribs are cracked," he said. He said it to the snow first, and then he said it again to her. "The seventh and the eighth, on the left. The slide did it. I cannot take a full breath, and I cannot raise the left arm above the shoulder. I have been hiding both since the cairn. I have stopped on the pitch more than you have seen, because I stop when you are not looking."

Nothing happened to his hands. He watched them, and so did she. The wind moved in the loose cuff of his coat, and the fingers stayed the colour of fingers.

"There," said Meera. Her eyes shut, briefly. "That's a bill I don't mind not paying."

* * *

She bound him in the lee of a boulder where the wind could not get at his chest.

She had to do it with her hands shaking, and he offered to do it himself, which she declined with a look. From the bottom of the pack she pulled a coil of braided goat hair, grey and soft with use, and for a moment she only held it.

"Jassi's spare," she said. "He tethered the kids with it. He'd never go anywhere without a spare." She shook out a length. "Coat open. Tunic stays on. Lift your arms as far as they go."

He lifted the right. The left went to his shoulder and stopped. She said nothing of it. She slid the cord around his back, under the arm, and wound it round his ribs from below the cracked ones to above, crossing it flat across his spine so that there would be no knot to press on him.

"Breathe out," she said.

He did.

"Out more."

The cord drew in. The ends of the bone, which had ground on each breath since the gully, stopped grinding. A band of steadiness settled round him, snug enough to be felt and not so tight that it hurt. He breathed in a short, shallow pull and found the top of it was no longer a knife.

"You have done this before," he said.

"Twice. Both men." She tucked the end under the last turn. "You'll breathe small whichever way. This way the ends don't grate on each other. Tell me if it pinches. Tell me *true*."

"It does not pinch."

"Good."

He did up the coat's buttons one-handed. She was already moving ahead on the slope, the red rope paying out between them, and he saw she had slowed her pace by a third and made nothing of it. She had not looked at him the whole while she wound the cord. Her ears were red, which was not the wind.

He thought of that for a long time afterward.

* * *

They made the hollow by midmorning, a wind-cut bowl under the lip of the Saddle where the snow lay as hard as old bread. Meera cut blocks with the axe and laid a half-ring of them as she had at the waystone. Rohan hauled blocks with his right arm until she told him to sit down.

The day went in sleep and small chores. By dusk her hands had come back to a dull pink, but the shaking had not stopped. When the dark thickened and the blue line rose along the edge of the bowl, she was sitting with her knees drawn up and her whole body going like a sail in a squall.

"Frostnip and a night of shaking," she said, when he looked. "I know the rate. I'll live." Her teeth clicked on the last word.

He fed the fire. There were four dung cakes and a handful of juniper bark, and he set them as she had taught him, and they burned bright and honest. He pushed her closer to the coals. She held her palms to it and stared at it without expression.

"It won't help," she said. "Fire doesn't touch it. I'd tell you if it did."

He knew that. She had told him. He sat anyway, helpless as a man at a bedside, and watched the flames lick at the cold and the cold not give way.

Under his coat, rolled at his belt, was the grey pattu he had carried since the post. It was warden's issue, thick, woven close, and it was the only warm thing in the bowl that was not on fire. He had not touched it when the tents were lost, because he had been raised not to take warmth before the people in his charge had theirs.

He unrolled it and held it out.

"No," said Meera.

"It is wool, Guide. It does not say a great deal."

"You've cracked ribs. You need it." Her chin shook as she said it. "I'm the one who was fool enough to—"

"You took my cold, and you did not have to." He kept his voice level, as he did in council, and it came out less steady than he meant. "It is only a shawl. I am warm enough. Watch my hands, and you will see it is so."

She looked at the pattu. She looked at his hands, which stayed as they were. In the firelight she looked younger than twenty-nine and a good deal more tired. She had buried a man at the foot of this climb the day before, and she had not once asked for anything.

She took it. She dragged it round her shoulders with both hands and bowed her head into the fold, and for a while she only shook.

"It smells of wardens," she said at last, muffled.

"Camphor. It is meant for the moth."

"Terrible."

"I am told it is."

She made a sound that was not quite a laugh, and the shawl moved with it.

* * *

She slept near midnight, with the red rope slack across the snow between them. He sat up with the fire and did the sum.

It was a habit, and a poor one. He tallied what a thing cost, and who had paid it. He had done it all his life with fines and with men.

The day was gone. Sixteen of the twenty, then, and he had spent one on his own pride. That was the first line of the ledger, and it was the small one.

She had known the moment she held out her hand that she would learn he had lied to her. She had known she would pay for it. She had seen the lie in his face and weighed it as she weighed the odds on the gully.

She could have let him slow. It would have cost her nothing, if cost were measured in cold. She could have let him limp up the Saddle with frostnip and cracked bone, and put the blame where it belonged, and gone on with her sattu and her count. He had given her every reason. He was a warden. He was bound to arrest her at the thaw. He had seized her crew's goods twice, and he could not now remember the names of the families.

She had put her hand in the cold and held it there.

He turned his own hand over in the firelight and looked at it. The knuckles were whole. The fingertips were warm. They were warm because hers were not. He had priced his lie at a night of shaking, and she had paid the price, and the price had never been his to pay.

There was a thing he had never allowed himself to be, in all the years of the badge, and it was in debt. A warden gave and was owed. Rules kept the ledger straight. He did not know what rule covered this, and so he sat and watched the fire and let the figure stand where it was. He owed her, in a currency the Closing Law had no clerk for.

When the fire dropped, the shaking eased. She woke near the grey hour and lay with her eyes open, looking at the lip of the Saddle where the stars were going out.

"You didn't take it back," she said.

"The pattu? I am not cold."

She tested that, he saw. She waited, looking at his hands, and they stayed as they were. She seemed to relax by a degree.

"Rohan," said Meera.

He waited.

"Promise you won't lie to me again."

It came out small and plain, as if she had worn it smooth in her mouth. She was not asking as a guide, or as a smuggler, or as the woman who had bound his ribs. She asked like someone who had looked at the worst of a bill and was wondering whether it would come again.

He knew what a promise was on this rope. He had heard her say it among the rules, in the middle, where she kept the things she meant least to explain. A promise made while bound was a promise to the mountain. If it broke, the breaker was struck as by a great lie, flesh dead in the hands and feet.

He did not know that he could keep it. There would come a night, and he knew it as he knew the Saddle was there, when the true answer would hang her, and silence would cost more than silence could pay. He would not stand in that night with a promise round his neck and call it a vow. He had priced a lie once already, and the bill had gone to her.

"No," he said.

Her face went still.

"I will not promise it." He kept his hands open on his knees, where she could see them. "A promise is a rope, and I would be tying it to you with one end already frayed. I do not know what the mountain will ask of me. I do not know what you will ask. I will not hand you a word I may not be able to hold."

"That's not an answer."

"It is the only true one I have." He held her eyes. "I have not lied to you since the pitch. I'll show you. Watch my hands."

She did. The fire had burned down to a rind of red, and in its light his fingers lay still and steady and warm. She studied them for a long time, with the pattu pulled up to her chin and her own hands hidden in its folds.

She said nothing. It cost her nothing. He had never heard silence sound so much like a question.
