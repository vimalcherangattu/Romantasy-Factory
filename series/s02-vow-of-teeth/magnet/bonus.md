# The Warden's Silence

*A The Odiyan Contracts bonus story*

The bride was counting the doors.

Devan saw it from the high table, between the second course and the third. She did not turn her head. Her eyes moved in small stops: the hall door, the kitchen door, the stair arch, the gap behind the musicians. Her lips shaped numbers on nothing. Then she lifted her cup and drank, and became a pleased bride in ivory and gold.

It was the first honest thing she had done all day.

He kept his own face still. He had practice. Eight years of practice, and the council had sent him a woman who was better.

"You are not eating," Janu said at his elbow. She had come up from the kitchen with a fresh dish of fried plantain and the look of a woman who had been told not to scold today and was suffering for it.

"I am eating."

"You are moving rice from one side of the leaf to the other."

"That is a kind of eating."

"It is a kind of sulking." She set the plantain in front of him and lowered her voice. "She is lovely, Devan. And she knows Kannan's goat."

He looked down the hall. His bride was talking to the goat-boy, who had carried the garland that afternoon and still wore it round his neck like a captured flag. Kannan was nine or ten. He was explaining something with both hands. The bride had bent to hear, and she was nodding.

"What is the goat's name?" Devan asked.

"Pepper. It bites."

"She told you that?"

"She told *Kannan* that she had heard it bites. He nearly burst." Janu sniffed. "Nobody tells that boy he is understood."

Devan said nothing. Janu took his silence for agreement, which it was not. It was a note being made.

A woman sent to a stranger's fort, with a single trunk and a ward's cover, ought to be unremarkable. A woman who intended to leave no trace would not learn the name of a goat. She would not have learned Chinna's name at the kitchen door by the end of the first hour, or Raiu's, the nineteen-year-old with the cowlick who held the corridor torch. It had taken Devan four months, and two asks, to learn Raiu's name. She had it in an afternoon, and used it, and Raiu had gone the colour of a ripe chilli.

It did not fit. Devan did not like what did not fit. It had kept him alive for thirty-two years.

The council had pressed this match. That was fact. Councillor Ittan's name stood first on the letter, a kind man's name, and the council wax at the bottom of it was genuine. Genuine council wax was also on the forged tallies that were walking raiders through his pass. He did not know who sold them. He knew the match was the first move anyone on that council had made toward his fort in five years.

So he had married a stranger at sunset, in the old ivory and gold, with her hand cold in his. He had said the words plainly. He did not say anything he did not mean, so he had said only the words that were true. *I will keep faith with you in this house.* A warden could mean that of anyone.

When the plates were cleared she came to him.

"Husband." She said it lightly. Her voice was lower than he had expected, dry at the edges. "Will you tell me how the fort sleeps? I would like to know where not to wander at night."

It was a clever question. It sounded like a bride's nerves. It was a hunter's map-making.

He could have said *the guards are the guards' business.* He could have said *Janu will show you.* Instead he told her, because he did not lie, and because a thing said plainly was harder to use than a thing hidden.

"Two on the west stair," he said. "Four at the padippura. Six on the wall, in pairs. They change on the third clank of the cistern chain after the middle watch."

Her eyes flickered. She had not expected the whole of it.

"That is very exact," she said.

"You asked."

"I did." She turned the gold bangle on her wrist. It had been made from a measure the council sent, and it fitted perfectly, which he did not like either. "Most men would have said it was nothing for me to worry about."

"It is not nothing. You live here."

She looked at him then. Fully, for the first time that day. Her eyes were dark and tired, with something small and furious far back in them, the look of a person doing a sum she hated.

"Yes," she said. "I suppose I do."

She went back to her seat. He watched her go, and thought: *She is afraid of something, and it is not me.*

That was worse. A woman afraid of him he could have understood.

* * *

The room was too quiet.

He had known it would be. For five years, the warden's chamber had held one sleeper, one lamp, a teak bed, a brass kindi and a sword. Now there was a second pillow, a trunk against the wall, and the smell of jasmine oil over something sharper and bitter underneath, like scorched tamarind and pine sap.

He had smelled that sharpness once before. He had been twenty, at the border school, in a stone classroom with a thin instructor passing a sealed jar from hand to hand. *Learn it,* the man had said. *If you ever smell this near a sleeper, you are in the house of an odiyan.* Devan had held the jar. It had been warm from other hands. He had thought: *Such a small smell for such a large fear.*

The jar was in his head again. That was the thing about the wedding bangle, the exact measure, the council's hurry. It all led to a flask in a fold of cloth.

He lay on his side, back to the wall, and breathed the long slow count of a man asleep.

He had laid the sword on the floor on her side of the bed, hilt toward her. He had meant it as a test. If she took it, he would know what she was. If she stepped over it, he would know that too. The grip was plain wire, worn smooth at the thumb. His father's thumb, and then his.

She did not take the sword.

He heard her rise. He heard the bar of the shutters, which was not a bar tonight, only an iron latch, because the wedding guests had been given the sea breeze by his father's old rule that a room should breathe. The long teak bar hung unused on its hooks. He would think about that later, with a stomach that turned. Anyone could have lifted that latch.

She pressed the third slat of the shutters. It creaked. Of course it creaked. He had never fixed it.

She stopped. He heard her listening to him.

He kept the count. Four, five, six, and the small hitch he knew in his own breath a third of the way along, which he had never been able to cure, and which was now the one thing in the room he could not afford. He let it come. A sleeper hitched. An honest man hitched.

She went on.

Through the lashes, he watched her climb to the broad ledge. She crouched there with her back to him and her hair down, lit grey by the last light on the hills. Her wrists were bare. At the wrists and throat there were pale ridged scars, thick and layered, and she touched the flask to them without hesitation.

She had done this many, many times.

He had been told about the burn. The instructor had said it hurt. It was one thing to be told. It was another to watch a woman bite the inside of her cheek and make no sound.

Then she changed.

He had braced for horror. He had not braced for how quick it was. One breath she was a woman on a ledge. The next her spine bowed, her shoulders ran like poured water, and the gold of her bangle slid, with a small sound, from a wrist that was no longer a wrist. What crouched in her place was long and pale and spotted, heavy as a hearth-stone, and the whole room smelled of her.

*Leopard.*

The word sat on his tongue. He felt its shape. It had a hard first sound and a soft end, and it would take half a breath to say. He knew the rule exactly. Anyone who had seen her turn with their own eyes could speak her shape, aloud, while she was in it, and she would be an animal for life. Her mind would fade in a year. A bride on her wedding night, caged by her husband's careless mouth.

He had seen the turning. He was her witness now. He had not asked to be.

The leopard looked back at him.

It was only a look. Yellow eyes, lamplight on the pupils, one long second. There was a mind behind them, and the mind was calculating how much he knew. He lay with his face slack and his breath even, and he thought, with a clarity that surprised him: *If she knew I was awake she would kill me now. She is on a ledge, with all the dark behind her. She would not even need the knife.*

She turned and went out.

When the ledge was empty he let a long breath go, slow, through his nose, and then he lay and shook.

Not from fear. He had named that for himself, bluntly, as he named everything. It was something nearer grief. A woman had been sold at six, or so the school had taught, or at some other age, and made into a weapon the size of a hill cat, and handed him in ivory. And he had stood at an altar and said *I will keep faith with you in this house,* and had not known he was promising it to someone with a collar already on.

Asha would have known what to do.

The thought came whole and he let it. Asha, at twenty-two, with her slate of recruits' names and her fast hands, would have said: *Say it plain, Devan. Say what you know.*

But Asha was six years dead on the west stair. He had stood over her in the grey before dawn with Janu holding the lamp. He had sworn there, with his hand flat on the cold step, that he would never be the one who holds a throat. He had thought he meant it of enemies.

He sat up. He crossed the room, quiet as he had learned to be in the dark, and put his hand on the shutter. The night was loud with frogs. Below, in the long kennel behind the stables, fourteen hounds were singing at something on the hill.

They would run the yard loose tomorrow dusk, as they always did. They would see her. A hound could not become a witness; a hound had no word. But the boys who fed them were not hounds. Neither was the sentry above this window, with a lantern two spans over the ledge.

He counted what he needed to do.

By the time the cistern chain clanked the middle watch, he had counted it twice.

He did not sleep. At the grey, he was on the bed again with his face to the wall and his breath long, when she came in at the stair door, a woman, in a saddle blanket from the stable, smelling of wet stone and the hunger that followed a turning. He heard the rough wool drop. He heard the bed creak. He heard her whisper, so low it was almost nothing, a word he could not catch.

Then she slept. And he lay with the sword on the floor between them and thought: *I am the only man in this fort who knows what is in my bed.*

*And I must keep it so.*

* * *

At the kalari, the red earth was cold under his feet and the lamps in their niches smelled of sesame oil.

It was the eleventh morning.

He had drilled alone in this pit for eight years. Twenty paces across, shoulder-deep, shored with laterite blocks and stepped on the north side. He knew every stone of it. He knew how the light fell, and which lamp guttered, and how the lantana on the east path made a green wall of the slope, because Gopan kept meaning to cut it and never did.

He did the first sequence slowly. Palm, turn, drop. He did the second faster. The third he did at full speed, the old forms his master had pressed into him until the body knew them without him: the hundred and eight strikes, the points, the doors. He moved until his breath was loud and the sweat ran into his eyes.

It was a good way not to think.

Eleven days. In eleven days he had learned a great deal. He knew she was out on the hill every night she took the resin, and that she came back at grey, through the yard, through the stair door, not the ledge, so that the dawn found her in a woman's skin. He knew she did not sleep after, only lay with her eyes shut, counting. He knew her hands shook for an hour after a turning, and that she hid it by gripping the bed-frame.

He knew the paw-marks she left at the water trough by the stable, in the wet laterite. Each dawn he went and read them, and each dawn he scuffed them out with his heel. His reason for the sentry was longer and quieter than that. A sentry with a lantern, two spans above the ledge, could watch her turn. The sentry would become a witness. The witness would have a mouth.

He had barred the shutters from the second dusk. He had kennelled the hounds that same night, saying only *Rain* when she asked, under a perfectly clear sky. That was the only untrue thing he had said to her, and it was not even a lie. It was a hole where an answer should be. She had given him a long look, and let it lie.

He had pulled the sentry a few nights later. He would deal with Janu's stories last, because Janu's stories were the kitchen's favourite, and the kitchen must not think it was being managed.

None of it had been enough. Nothing could be enough. She might trust no one with a leash in his mouth, and he was the leash.

A sound on the north steps.

He did not stop. He had heard her coming, in the way he now heard her, a bare foot placed with care on the cut stone. She came down into the lamplight in her day cloth with her hair tied up and her arms crossed over her chest, the way she stood when she was cold or when she was lying.

"Janu says you rise in the last dark hour," she said.

"I do."

"I thought you were a sleeper."

"Janu says a great many things." He finished the sequence and straightened. The lamp showed the line of her throat, plain, no scar on view, only the soft gold chain she wore against it. "You are up early."

"I wanted to see." She came a few steps down onto the red earth. Her feet left clean prints. "I have heard it, I think. In my sleep. Palms on skin."

That was true. He heard it in the way she said it, flat and surprised, the way a person reports what they did not intend to confess. He had a sudden, unwelcome tenderness for it.

"Come and see, then," he said.

She walked the circle once. Not to look at him. To look at the pit. At the shoring, the steps, the lamps, the place on the rim where the sentry on the east wall would stand with his back to them. He watched her note them all. Her lips moved on a count.

"Who guards pepper in Kodiyur?" he asked.

She stopped. "What?"

"You told Muthu you learned to read feet at the Chirayil warehouse. From a man who guarded pepper."

"Yes." Her eyes went to his bare feet, then to the earth, and she smiled, thin. "He said you can tell a good fighter by where the weight goes when he thinks nobody is watching."

"And where does mine go?"

"Left," she said. "When you're tired. Your guard drops on the left."

It was true, and it was a thing no one had said to him in eight years, because no one had been near enough to see it.

He turned his back on her.

He did it slowly. He did it as a man might, to pick up a cloth from the pit rim. He did it with the clear knowledge that she was an odiyan, that she carried a plain guild knife in the gap under the mattress, and that a woman who had killed eleven men on contract could kill a twelfth with her bare hands if she knew the right place.

He knew the right place. A thumb's width below the base of the skull. The old masters called it the door.

He heard her come up behind him. He heard her breath stop.

Then he felt her fingers. Cool, steady, on the back of his neck, exactly on the door.

He stood very still, and did what he had been taught to do when the door was touched. He held his breath, and he let his body go loose.

It would take only a moment. A press, a turn. A man who looked like he had fallen.

"One," she said, very low.

He felt his own pulse in his ears.

"Two."

Her hand did not move. It was not shaking. He had expected it to.

"Three."

She lifted her hand away. The air on his neck was cold where her fingers had been.

"Your guard drops on the left," she said again, and her voice was quite level. "That's all."

He turned round.

She was already walking away from him, up the north steps, with her arms crossed again and her shoulders high. She did not look back. She went up the way she did everything, counting. He heard her lips shape each step.

He stood in the middle of the red earth, in the sesame-lamp light, and found that he was shaking now. It started in his hands. He pressed them flat to his thighs.

*She could have.*

It was the plainest fact he had ever been handed. She had stood with a killing point under her palm and her contract ten days from its end, with no guard on the wall to see and no leopard's secret to protect, and she had counted three breaths aloud, so that he would know she had had them, and she had let him go.

It did not mean she was safe. It did not mean she would not try again at some other hour. It certainly did not mean he could say the thing he was carrying, *I have seen you, and I will not say it,* because a man who gave a woman that gift was a man who held a rope, and called it kindness.

But it meant she would not kill him in the dark. Not today.

He took that. It was small, and exact, and he laid it carefully beside the other small, exact things he had kept in the eleven days. A name for a goat. A gap in the lamplight where her eyes went to the doors. A bangle she turned, over and over, as if it had burned her.

He did not know what she was. He knew now what she was *not.*

When he climbed the north steps the sun had cleared the wall. He went by the kitchen. Janu was there, in a rage over the rice, and Chinna was at the door making a wager with a cousin, and he asked for a bowl of curd and a spoon and the smaller brass jug of buttermilk.

"For her?" Janu asked, not looking.

"For the room."

"You never asked for anything for the room."

"I know."

She said nothing more. She made a noise in her throat that in anyone else would have been a sob and in Janu was a lecture held back. When he went up the stairs the tray was warm.

That night, as on every night since the second week, he filled the kindi on her side of the bed himself. He set the spout toward the pillow. She reached for it left-handed. He had seen it, the first night she thought he slept.

It was nothing. A brass jug. He did not mention it.

* * *

The rains had shut the pass nine days ago, and Kettamala had gone green and loud and wet.

Water ran down every stair. The cistern chain clanked on the hour, and under the clank was the long steady roar from the gorge, and under the roar was a silence he had not heard in his own fort for years. Nobody was coming. Nothing could. The Monkey Pass was a throat of white water, and until the rains broke they were an island.

He liked it more than he ought to.

Muthu came up from the lower wood road at dusk, soaked to the hair, with mud to his thighs and a face so tired the grin on it looked borrowed.

"Done," he said in the hall. "The cart got through at the second ford with the wheel in a ditch only once. Water, fodder, a clean cloth. Four men with me and none dead." He dropped onto a bench and the bench complained. "I put the cloth in the cage myself, Devan. Your lady said I should."

"Did she lie on it?"

"She did." Muthu's big voice dropped. He rubbed a hand over his face. "She had an anklet. Silver, with the bells cut off. I thought she'd bite me, and she didn't. She lay down on it as if she'd been waiting a year." He was silent a moment. "How many are there, again?"

"Forty-two."

"Forty-two animals and every one of them was a girl." He said it flatly, an army habit when something hurt too much to say well. "I fed a deer, Devan. I hand-fed it. It looked at me."

"I know."

"And the old man was there. The master. On the cook-house step, in the rain, with a book held to his chest like a baby." Muthu's mouth twisted. "Black cover. He didn't run when he saw us. He only stood and watched, and then he went inside."

Devan said nothing for a while. Neela's name for the book was *the ledger.* She had said it once at night, in the dark where they asked each other anything, and the word had tasted like iron.

"You did well," he said.

"I did what you said."

"That is what doing well means."

Muthu laughed. It was the big laugh, the one the whole fort knew, and it came a half-breath late.

Devan watched him over the lamp.

They had been boys together in the kalari pit. Muthu had been the one who held him when his father died. Muthu had opened the raider captain's arm eight years ago, and seen the killing, and carried the knowledge for eight years without once mentioning it. There was no man alive Devan would sooner have at his back.

And Muthu, when he lied, went quiet. He had done it at fourteen over a stolen mango. He had done it at the padippura with a grey-sealed packet, thicker than a month's letter, in his hand.

"Did you write to your mother, in the ford camp?" Devan asked.

The hall was loud with rain. Muthu's grin held for exactly as long as it takes to count three.

"No," he said. "Nothing to say, in the mud."

His voice was a very small voice. Devan heard the quiet in it, and nodded as if he had heard only the words.

"Go and eat. Janu will want to look at you."

When Muthu had gone he went to the strongbox at the back of the hall. The key hung on a cord inside his shirt. He opened the lid.

The packet lay where he had left it, on top of the court rolls. Grey wax, no mark. It was the one Muthu had carried at first light across the wet yard to the guardroom, to Ittan, and Devan had taken it from him gently, before the door was opened, saying *I'll hold that,* and Muthu had let him.

He could break the seal. It would take a moment. Whatever his captain had written, to whoever he had written it, it would be in his hand. The Keeper had called it *a letter for the south.* The south was Kodiyur. Kodiyur was the palace.

He lifted it. It was heavier than he liked.

*Say it anyway,* Asha had written. *The truth protects whoever finds it.*

But Asha had also been twenty-two, and fearless in daylight, and she was dead. Perhaps the truth did protect whoever found it. Perhaps it did not protect whoever acted on it too soon. Breaking the seal would tell Muthu he was watched. He would know, as surely as a dog knows a boot, that the man he loved had stopped believing him. And a man who has lost belief goes one way or the other. He tells everything, or he tells nothing.

Devan had no spare friends.

He put the packet back. He closed the lid. He turned the key.

*Not yet,* he thought. *Not until I know whose hand he is.*

* * *

The warden's chamber smelled of rain and lamp oil.

She was already abed when he came up. Not asleep. Lying on her back in the dim, with her hands folded on her stomach as she did when she was pretending. He lit nothing else. He undressed, laid the sword on the boards on her side of the bed, hilt toward her, and sat on the edge with his bare feet on the cold floor.

"Muthu came back," he said.

"I heard. Did he find Kirri?"

"He laid the cloth in her cage. She lay down on it. She still wore the anklet."

She did not answer. The rain did it for her, a long hush on the shutters. He had left them open, tonight. It was a thing he could do now. The night stood outside, full of the smell of wet laterite, and no sentry on the wall above, and she was in his bed and no one in the world but the two of them knew what she was.

Nobody but the two of them, and a man somewhere in the Blackbark Wood, with a book.

"Thank you," she said at last.

"I did nothing. Muthu drove the cart."

"You sent it."

"It was your promise."

"It was yours," she said. "You said, *Then I'll come.*"

He had. At the Resin House, in the yard, with the blood still wet, on a night he did not like to think about. He had said it to the girl he was frightened of loving, and he had meant it entirely, and he had learned afterward that meaning a thing entirely was no help at all when the thing was forty-two cages and a mountain of rain.

"Neela."

"Mm."

"The bundle behind the third slat." He turned his head. He could see only the line of her cheek. "You've not asked me what I'll do with it."

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because," she said, "you'd tell me." A small pause. Her voice had a smile in it, dry as a leaf. "And I'm tired of being told things I can't fix tonight."

He laughed. It was a short sound, startled out of him, the way it came now, rarely and without leave. Beside him she went still, and then he felt the bed shake, very slightly, and understood that she was laughing too, silently, into the pillow, at the whole improbable ruin of their winter.

When it passed, she reached for his hand.

Left-handed. Her fingers found his in the dark, on the first try, as if she had counted the distance.

"Come to bed," she said.

He did.

They did not speak again for a long while. The rain filled the gaps. He lay with his face toward her and his hand in hers, watching the faint grey of the shutters, and thought of nothing but that her hand was warm, and her scars at the wrist were against his knuckles, ridged and smooth, a thing he could feel and she let him.

She slept before he did. That was new, too.

He had been listening to her sleep for nine days now, and the pattern had begun to teach itself. First the long slow breath, then the small catch. Then, a few moments after, the counting.

It was always the same.

*One,* she said, so low it was not quite voice. *Two. Three.*

He knew it now like a hymn. She counted her steps. Not the kalari steps, nor the fourteen to the hall, nor the forty-four of the west stair, though he had once heard her say a number that was close. She counted a staircase in the dark, the way Asha used to count, because Asha had believed that numbers kept the dark from filling in.

*Eight. Nine. Ten.*

She stopped at eleven.

She always stopped at eleven. He had marked it, the first week. The eleventh step on the west stair rang hollow over a drain slot, and she took the turn on the eleventh of the hall stair, and he had thought it chance.

"Eleven," she said, in her sleep. A breath. Then, in a voice he had never heard her use awake, small and flat and very young: "It's broken."

His hand lay still in hers.

He had heard it before. Many nights. *It's broken,* and then nothing. He had taken it for a dream of a ruined thing, a cart, a cup, a mended bangle. He had told himself so, nine nights running, because the alternative was a door he did not have a key to.

But tonight there was a parrot in his head.

A forearm long, carved in teak, at the foot of the west stair. He had walked past it ten thousand times. Its beak had been broken for as long as he could remember, worn smooth as a river stone. Asha had hated it. She had drawn it on every note she ever wrote, in five quick strokes, and in every drawing the beak was whole. *A broken beak is an insult to the bird,* she had said, aged twelve, in the voice of a magistrate.

Neela had never seen Asha's notes. He had not shown her the parrots. He had shown her only the slate, and the name of a recruit, and a joke about Thursday sparring.

He lay in the warm dark with his heart going slowly and heavily, and turned the two facts over. A stair she counted. A beak that was broken. Neither meant anything. A woman from the coast, an orphan of the Resin House, had never set foot in this fort until the day of her wedding. Everything she said in her sleep was something she could have heard from a servant, or dreamed.

*Say it plain,* Asha said, somewhere in him.

*I have nothing to say,* he told her. *I have a rain-heavy night and a sleeping wife.*

*Then look.*

Beside him Neela turned her face into the pillow. Her lips moved once more. He could not catch it.

Outside, under the long voice of the water, the cistern chain clanked the middle watch. Down at the foot of the west stair, in the wet and the dark, a carved bird kept its post, as it had for six years.

In the morning, before the kalari, he would go and stand in front of it. He would tell himself it was to see whether the beak could be mended.

He had never once told himself a lie that well.
