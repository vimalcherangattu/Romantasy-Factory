# Chapter 15: Bear and Lamp

The first claw opened across his back while Devan was reaching for the hinge.

He did not cry out. He had one boot on the ledge and the other braced against the shaft wall. A shuttered lamp hung from a nail beside his head, giving a yellow slit of light on wet stone and the iron box at his feet. The heat started below his left shoulder blade and ran down to the right hip in three lines. A fourth came after, higher, burning like a dropped coal.

*Shallow,* he told himself. *They're shallow.* He had been told they would be.

Above him, through the ring of the well-house floor, came sounds no man makes. A heavy drag across flags. A cough, deep and wet. Claws that scrabbled and found purchase and scrabbled again.

*Three in ten.* He had said the number himself, on a river bank, as if reading a tally.

He put the flat of the pry bar under the hinge plate. Chathu had trusted his lock. It was fist-sized and good, and it hung on the hasp as shut as a jaw. But a man who trusts a lock forgets the hinges. Devan set his boot on the curve of the chain and leaned his whole weight on the bar. The first rivet sheared with a crack he felt in his teeth. The second held. Another line opened across his ribs, and he hissed through his nose and leaned harder.

The third rivet went. The lid came up on its ruined hinge like a mouth, and the lock stayed shut on nothing.

A black book lay inside, wrapped in greased cloth. It was thick as his forearm, and it smelled of scorched tamarind. He lifted it out in both hands. His thumbs found the edge of the cloth, and he stopped them there.

He had sworn twice, in the warden's chamber with a lamp between them. If he began to read a page at night, she would close her teeth on his wrist, and he would shut the book without asking why. He had made the oath before he held the thing. He found it was a different oath now that it was heavy in his hands.

He did not open it. He pushed it inside his shirt, against the old seam, and cinched his belt over it.

Then he took the rope and climbed.

Each pull opened something. He counted hand-lengths as she would have counted them. Nine. Ten. The welts on his back stretched and tore a little at every reach, and the sacking on his boots slid on the wet stone. At eleven his head came above the curb.

* * *

The well-house door stood open on the yard. It was full of lamplight, swinging.

Neela was not in the doorway. She was on the flags ten paces out, down on her side, with one forepaw lifted and her lips drawn back off her teeth. Her fur was black with wet from the ribs to the haunch.

The bear stood over her.

It had risen onto its hind legs, the bad one cocked and the good one planted. The pale Y on its chest shone in the lantern light. It reared there with both forearms lifted, as a man lifts a mallet to a post, and every claw was open.

Devan was not a man who struck first. He had made that vow on a stair six years ago, and he had kept it for eight years before that. He had kept it for people he had not loved.

He crossed the ten paces in four strides and did not strike first. He waited for the arms to start down.

They started. He swung the pry bar up and across with both hands, and the iron met the bear's shoulder at the joint, with a sound like a mallet on wet timber. He felt a ridge under the iron, an old healed seam in the muscle. He knew whose blade had put it there.

The bear roared.

It was a huge, cracked, furious noise, and it filled the yard to the wall. The great body came down off its hind legs and swung to face him, one arm hanging wrong. It was the second time in a month Devan had hit a living thing. He stood over Neela with the bar low and did not step back.

From the east wall a horn began to blow.

It was a flat, hard hunters' horn, going again and again. Lamps jerked along the wall. Boots came down the stairs. A hound found its voice, and then another.

*If the House wakes, Bhaskaran comes. If it doesn't, he comes at the lamp. Either way he comes.*

He was counting the breaths to that when the east wall bloomed with light.

Eight lamps rose over the coping at once, and under each a man came over. Two more came out of the drain grate, bent double, and straightened with spears. They ran in a line across the yard with Bhaskaran at the head, a face like a shut gate. Kuttan was grinning, even now.

"Spears out," Bhaskaran said, in his parade voice. "Lamps up. Hold the line. Nobody speaks to the animal."

It was Devan's order, given on the ridge, and Bhaskaran gave it as flat as a tally. Eight points of steel came level in a row, and eight lamps threw one hard white wash across the flags.

The bear swung its head from the light to the spears and back to the light.

It was a big animal and a hurt one. It dragged the hind leg, and the shoulder hung. It went back one step on the flags, a drag and a step. Then another. It was a bear, and a bear knows light and steel at a distance. A man inside it knows more. He had killed in the dark for twenty years, in the half-light of a stair, and he had never once been seen.

It backed toward the cook-house with its head low, and the light followed it.

* * *

A man stood on the cook-house step, holding a small lamp.

He was old and soft, in a sleeping robe with a shawl over his shoulders, and his left foot was set a little crooked on the stone. Devan had never seen him before. He knew him at once by the stillness, the way he held the light as a man holds a candle in a sickroom. Hunters were coming in at his back, with poles and nets and iron rings on chains.

Chathu looked at the bear, and the spears, and the leopard bleeding on the flags. Then he looked at Devan's belt.

The book was a hard square under Devan's shirt. He had not drawn it. Chathu had seen the outline.

Devan took it out.

He did it without a plan. A man in a room with eight hunters and a bear hurts for plans. He drew the black bundle from his shirt and held it up in the lamplight in both hands, as a man holds up a child to a crowd.

"Call them off," he said.

The hunters stopped. Their poles dipped, and they looked at their master.

Chathu did not look at the poles. He did not look at the spears. His gaze went past all of it to the dark corners of the yard, where something was gathering.

"That's eleven years of feeding, warden," he said, in a soft, patient voice. "Every grain. Put it down, and I'll write the rest off."

"Your terms are late," Devan said.

Chathu's mouth moved as if he were adding. Then, quite simply, he turned on his bad foot.

"Vellan," he said. "The wood. Now."

He went. He went across the back of the cook-house at a rolling, scuttling run, faster than a man of sixty-one with a broken foot had any right to. The bear went after him in its drag and step, and the hunters broke and ran with them, bearing a stunned man between two of them like a sack. The dark under the trees took them, and the lamps lit only empty ground.

"Sir?" Bhaskaran said. "Pursue?"

"No."

Devan stood with the book in his hands and understood what he had just done.

He had known the rule since the border school. He had known it as a line in a book. *A page is a life. Whoever holds it is a master, whether he wants to be or not.* Chathu had known it too. Chathu had looked at the book in a stranger's hands and seen at once what his house would do next. He had run, and he had been right to.

The yard was not empty.

* * *

They came out of the sheds and from behind the kennel roof, from under the wall and the drain. They came as low shapes at the edge of the light, and they did not hurry.

He did not count them aloud. Forty, near enough. There was a long-legged grey animal that stood like a heron's shadow, and a small lean one that was all eyes. There was a dark otter with a white chin and a forepaw held curled to her chest. There were others he had no names for, and he would not look for names. The hounds had stopped. The spears had stopped. The only sound was the lamps spitting.

They stopped where the light stopped, in a ragged half-ring, and sat or stood. None of them looked at the spears.

All of them looked at the book.

Forty to eight. He did the sum Chathu had done, and it ended where Chathu's had ended. Each of them was a trained killer in a shape no spear was made for. They had been fed and caged by the man who held that book, and now the man had gone, and it was held by a stranger with a pry bar and fifteen cuts on his back.

Devan did not move his hands. The book weighed more every breath he held it.

"Lower the spears," he said.

"Sir—"

"Lower them, Bhaskaran. Slow. Point them at the ground."

They lowered, one by one, like men setting down a child's toy near a sleeping dog. Nothing came out of the dark. The half-ring stayed where it was.

At his feet Neela got up.

It took her three tries. She stood with her flank against his leg, and he felt her ribs working against his knee. The wet on her fur was not all hers. She leaned on him, and he let her. He could not put a hand to her side with eight men and forty animals watching. He could not wrap a cloth round a hurt he felt as his own. He stood, and she stood, and in the lamp's glare his back ran blood down into his belt.

"No one speaks to them," he said to the men, quietly. "No one says what they are. You've seen animals tonight, and that is all you saw. If any of you are asked, you were here for a book."

"Yes, sir," said Kuttan, who could not write, and who was not grinning now.

He did not open the book. The back half held resin-ink pages, and he did not know whether the words in it were shapes or cipher. He did not mean to learn. He stood with it shut in both hands while the sky over the cook-house roof went from black to the color of a bruise.

*Hold it till she turns back. That was the plan.* He had said it on the ridge, with his hands open on his knees. It had sounded like a small thing then.

The grey came slowly. Under the wall, one of the shapes rose and turned. Another followed. Without a sound or a signal they went in a file across the yard, toward the long low sheds by the wood where the resin smell lay thickest, and one by one the dark doors took them. Somebody had taught them long ago to turn back unseen. The training held, even now.

"Spears to the gate," Devan said. "Faces to the gate. All of you."

They turned, with the stiffness of men glad of an order. He heard the boots grind round on the flags.

He took her folded cloth from inside his coat, where it had ridden against his ribs since the ridge. He put his back to the well-house door and held the cloth out behind him at arm's length. He kept his eyes on the line of Bhaskaran's shoulders. He had watched her return once, in daylight, in his arms. This was another thing. This was a thing she did with the wall of his back between her and the world.

Behind him he heard it. A breath that was not an animal's, long and shuddering. A woman's cough. The cloth slid from his fingers.

"Thank you," Neela said, hoarsely. She did not say it loudly.

"Don't," Devan said. "Eat."

He found the leaf packet in his sleeve, cold rice and curd, and passed it back without turning. After a moment he heard her sit down on the curb of the well, and heard her hands shake on the leaf. He kept his face to the gate.

* * *

They came out of the sheds at first light, dressed.

They came in grey work wraps, with wet hair, shaking with the hollow hunger of the morning after, as she was shaking. They came one at a time, then in twos, and stood in the pale yard in rows no one had ordered. There were men and women, tall and short, and one young woman at the front who held her right hand curled against her chest. A white scar ran across her chin.

Nobody spoke. They looked at Devan's hands.

He had been the warden of Kettamala for five years, and he knew what it was to stand in front of people who waited for an order. This was another thing. At the fort they waited because he had earned it, or been born to it, or because an office gave it. These forty had waited all their lives on a book. Now they stood in the cold, hungry, with the day opening behind them, and every face was turned to the thing in his hands.

*Whoever holds it is a master.* He felt it, plainly, as a kind of lean in the yard. If he spoke now, they would do what he said. He had never wanted anything less.

Neela had come to stand beside him. She was in her day cloth, hair stuck to her neck, and she held one arm clamped across her ribs. The leaf lay empty in her other hand. She was grey under the brown of her skin. She did not look at the book. She looked at the faces, one at a time, and he knew she was counting them and giving each one a name.

"Pavi," she said, very low. The young woman at the front flinched, and her chin came up.

"Chathu is gone," Devan said.

His voice carried across the yard without trying. He did not raise it.

"This was his. It isn't mine." He turned to her. "I said I would hold it until you turned back. You've turned back."

Neela did not move. "Devan."

"I know what it is."

"If I take it, I'm what he was." She said it so quietly the front rows could not have heard, but they saw her mouth. "Whoever holds it."

"Then you'll hold it where all of them can see you do it." His hands were steady. He was surprised. "And if you ever begin to read one of those pages at night, I'll take it out of your hand and give you no reason. You gave me leave. I'm giving you the same."

She looked at him then. In that look he saw everything she had not said on the ride: that she was afraid of it, that she had been hunted all her life by this book, that no one had ever handed it to her. She had wanted it destroyed. She had been right to want that, and she had agreed to carry it anyway.

*Whatever is in it,* he thought, *there is one page I want more than I have wanted anything in eight years.* He did not look down at the cover. He did not ask.

He held the book out to her in both hands, in the open grey light, in front of all of them.

Neela put down the empty leaf. She wiped her palms on her cloth, slowly, once each. Then she took the weight of it against her chest. Her arms sagged a little and caught.

It was done. His hands were empty, and lighter than he had known hands could be. The welts on his back burned in the cold, and he had never felt so little like a master.

Forty faces watched her. Not one of them moved.
