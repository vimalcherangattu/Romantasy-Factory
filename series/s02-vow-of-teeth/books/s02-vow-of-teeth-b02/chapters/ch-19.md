# Chapter 19: Assize Weather

Rema asked where the warden had been before he had the mud off his boots.

She sat in his father's chair in the hall with the tally chest at her elbow and a clean leaf under her pen. She was fifty-eight, with iron-grey hair under a plain white cloth. Devan had never seen her hurry, and he had never seen her late.

"Where does a warden ride when he leaves at first light with his wife and comes back four days later without her?"

He stood on the flags with a night and a morning of road in his legs and the welts across his back dragging at his shirt. Janu had pushed a rice ball into his hand in the passage. He had not been able to eat it, and he held it still.

"The Resin House, in the Blackbark Wood. A day's ride. I stayed two nights."

"Why?"

"The guild's ledger was there. I have seen it."

"Seen it." Rema wrote a word. "And it is now where?"

"Where it is safe. My wife holds the House. The contract leaves will come to your hands when they can be carried without losing them."

"Holds it against whom?"

"The men who ran it."

The pen stopped. "Is there anything in that book touching Kettamala?"

It was the question he had walked the whole road to meet. The Keeper's quire lay in a black book in a room he had slept outside. A leaf in it said *Hand, Thirty. Lookout, Seventeen.* Rema's court would ask who Seventeen was, and he had no answer yet that would not hand a crown clerk his wife.

"I won't answer that," he said.

Rema laid the pen down. She did it gently, the way she did everything. A woman who raised her voice would have been easier.

"A court takes that once, as a courtesy," she said. "Twice, as a habit. The third time it writes it down as an answer, and not the answer you would choose. I am not threatening you. I am telling you how the roll reads."

"I understand."

"I hope so. The assize sits at lamp-lighting. I will swear your wife as household witness to the closing writ, as I said I would. She will need to be here."

"She will come."

"When?"

He opened his mouth and found he did not know. He had said *one day* in the rain, and he had meant it as a thing he took for himself. He had not thought what it would cost the people waiting on the road behind him.

"As soon as she can," he said, and it was true, and it was thin, and Rema wrote it down.

* * *

The refusal came in the yard an hour later, and it was not loud.

He had given the order at the stable door, to the duty corporal, a plain one. A patrol of six to walk the lower narrows to the shelf and back before dark. Somebody had camped on the lip above it, with bows and a bronze mirror, and no one yet knew who waited beyond the cut. The mirror had flashed a count to somebody. He wanted to know who.

Six men came out of the barracks and stopped in the middle of the yard.

Corporal Kandan stood in front of them. He was past thirty, with a scar through one brow, and he had been Raiu's comrade. He had his spear grounded and his cap in his other hand, which was a courtesy, and the courtesy was how Devan knew it was serious.

"The corporal begs the warden's pardon," Kandan said. "The men won't walk the narrows."

Fourteen others had drifted to the barracks door to watch. Someone on the wall had stopped moving. Devan heard the cistern chain clank the hour and let it finish.

"Say why."

"The warden bowed to a green sash while the Keeper's neck was on the block." Kandan's voice did not shake. He had practised it. "The warden rode out at first light with his wife to the house that sent the thing that killed Raiu. We've heard where that house is." He drew a breath. "We'll walk for the fort. We don't know that this is the fort."

It was not an unreasonable thing to think. Devan would have thought it himself. A man who bows and rides off and will not say why is a man to be watched.

He had a hundred ways to answer. He could put the six on the wall in irons for the night and send for Unni's gate watch to hold them. He could say *I order it*, and the order would hold, because he was warden and the roll said so. He could close his hand and take Kandan by the collar, once, in front of everyone, and every man in the yard would walk by sundown.

He knew exactly how it would be done. That was the trouble with eight years of an open hand. It had not forgotten anything.

He opened it, and showed them.

"I bowed to Rema because she held a warrant," he said. "I let the Keeper die because I could not stop it without breaking the pass. I went to the Resin House because the men who killed Raiu keep their books there, and I wanted them in my hand and not theirs. I'll tell you no more than that, and you may hate me for it."

Nobody moved.

"If I'm wrong, the court will say so. It sits tonight, and the corporal's statement is in front of it. Rema has read your words and I have not asked to see them. Until the court says it, walk."

"And if you're not wrong?" Kandan said. "Who tells the next man's mother?"

It landed where it was aimed. Devan took it.

"I do," he said. "Every one. Raman's widow, and the next man's mother, and the one after. Not you."

Kandan's scarred brow twitched. Devan went on, without lifting his voice.

"This is what it costs, and I'll name it. It costs you a cold walk to a place where a man of ours died single file. It costs me your good opinion, and I have little enough that I notice the loss. If I could do it myself I would. I can't be at the narrows and the gate and the court." He lowered his hand to his side. "I could put you in irons. I haven't, and I won't. I've struck no one in this yard in eight years, and I'd sooner keep it so. I would rather you walked because you chose to."

The silence went on. A langur barked once on the cliff and was still.

Kandan looked at the open hand, then at the others. One of the six, young, a boy with a cowlick like Raiu's, shifted his feet.

"Six," said Kandan, to the boy and not to Devan. "Water bags. Lamps for the shelf." He put his cap on. He did not salute, and he did not look at Devan again. But they walked.

Devan stood in the empty yard with his hand still open. It was shaking again, a little. He closed it, and the welts pulled across his back as though someone had drawn a thumbnail down them.

Neela would have said something dry about it. He found he was composing her answer, and stopped.

* * *

Muthu was in the gate chamber over the padippura with the post chest open beside him. He rose at once, laughing before he was fully upright.

"Warden. You look like a wet mule. I have kept your fort for four days and nobody has died of it, which I count as a triumph. Kandan's six are at the narrows, I take it, because I heard a certain corporal's voice go up."

"They are at the narrows."

"Good. Good man, Kandan. Stubborn. It's the mother." Muthu rubbed his hands. "The chest is as you left it. Nothing went down the pass and nothing came up. Lady Rema asked to read the log and I let her. She read every line, and it took a while."

Devan took the brass post-key from the cord at his own neck. He had worn it since the day he took it from Muthu's belt, with a grey packet broken open on the long table and a captain who had written to Kodiyur every month for six years. He held it out across his palm.

Muthu looked at it.

The laugh was gone. It went all at once, the way a lamp goes when the oil is out. Devan had seen it go in the kalari pit when they were boys, at the moment of a lie, and he had never forgotten what it looked like.

This was not a lie. He had settled that in the weeks since sealing day. The quiet was not Muthu telling a falsehood. It was Muthu holding his breath between two doors.

"The log's yours," Devan said. "So is the chest. I should have given it back sooner."

"You took it for a reason."

"I did. The reason has gone with the day." That was nearly true. He did not say *the reason is a slip you carried in to the Keeper, and told me of only in the pit.* He said instead, "You ran this fort honestly while I was gone. I'm not so sure of many things that I can afford to lose one."

Muthu closed his hand on the key. The knuckles went pale.

"If anything is ever put in your hand that you can't carry," Devan said, "bring it to me first. Before you carry it anywhere."

For a long moment the big man said nothing at all. Below them in the court a mule coughed.

"Aye," Muthu said, very quietly, and that was all.

It was a risk, and Devan counted it. If Muthu wrote tonight, the household would learn within a week that the warden had gone to the Resin House and left his wife there. That was the price of the key. He paid it open-handed, and went down the gate stair without looking back to see which door Muthu chose.

* * *

Eravi caught him in the passage by the dispensary, stooped, with a stained apron tied over his shirt and his spectacles pushed up into his hair.

"Warden, a favour. A small one." He held up both hands as if Devan might refuse before hearing it. "I'm short of willow bark and the poppy, and the pass apothecary's stock is damp since the rains. There is a tapper I know up the wood road. Honest man, keeps a dry store. I'd ride up tomorrow at first light, back in two days."

"The wood road," Devan said.

"Only as far as the first clearing. I wouldn't go near the House, of course, not with all that's —" Eravi waved a hand. "I'm too old to be caught up in anyone's war."

His cuff brushed Devan's wrist. It smelled of green salve, scorched tamarind and pine sap, the smell that had followed Fourth Guard's pot into every room Neela slept in. Eravi's own receipt, probably, or near enough. Devan registered it as one registers weather.

Half his mind was in the hall and the other half on a road a day's ride north. A hurt woman's name had been lying in his chest since noon like a stone in a boot, and he could not find where it pressed.

"Go," he said. "Take Sankaran as far as the ford."

"Kind of you. I'll be back before you've noticed." Eravi smiled and bobbed, and went into his dispensary and shut the door.

Devan walked on. It did not occur to him until much later that he had not asked the tapper's name.

* * *

The assize sat at lamp-lighting, as Rema had said.

She had the hall set out with trestles and a green cloth. Janu had polished the eight pillars, because Janu cleaned when she was afraid. Rema sat at the head with the tally chest, and the clerk's chair was empty beside her. Devan took the warden's stool at her left hand. He had washed and eaten at last, under orders. His wife's seat, a wooden bench at the foot of the table, stayed bare.

Rema read Kandan's statement into the roll. *The bride has learned the name of every man's mother. The warden bowed.* She read it level, with no commentary. Then she looked at him over the page.

"The corporal says you bowed. Did you?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because the writ had your hand on it, and the Keeper was your prisoner. I had no right to stop you and no wish to be seen trying." He kept his voice level. "I thought I had a better use for the pass."

"Is that the whole reason?"

"It is the one I'll give."

Her mouth moved very slightly. "That is two," she said, and wrote.

The lamps burned lower. A clerk's boy brought the wine. Rema called the first witness, Unni, to speak to the sealing day, and the young man began in the careful voice of one who wanted the whole hall to forgive him for being there.

Heat came across Devan's ribs without warning.

It began under the left arm and ran across and down, shallow and quick, as though a hot wire had been drawn along the old seam from the ford. He did not move. His breath stopped on the inbreath and then he made it go on. Under the shirt the skin rose in a long welt, tender at the edges, and the old white seam, where the knife had gone in years ago, burned beneath it like a second line.

*Neela.*

It was not a thought. It was a sentence his body said by itself.

He put his hand flat over the place. The cloth was dry. No blood. A scrape, then, or the edge of something, or a graze. A cut so shallow it could be nothing. A cut deep enough would have taken his breath. But she had been hurt, and the House was a day's ride in the dark.

He stood.

The witness stopped. Rema's pen stopped. Somewhere in the long hall a stool scraped back.

"Warden?" Rema said.

"I ask leave of the court."

"To do what?"

"To go."

"Where?"

His hand was still pressed to his side. Rema's eyes went to it, then to his shirt, then to his face, and came back with the patient look she gave an honest ledger that did not balance.

He thought: she is too clever for this. He thought: I cannot say *the bond cuts me when she is cut*, not before a crown officer, not with a clerk's boy at the door. He thought of saying no, and of saying yes, and found neither would serve.

"I won't answer that," he said.

"That is the third time today," Rema said. She did not raise her voice. "I am keeping count."

"I know."

"The court may not grant leave. There is no blood on your shirt, Warden, and I am not blind."

"Then the court will say so," Devan said, "and I will hear it from the road."

He put his stool under the table. Nobody stopped him. At the doorway Muthu was on his feet with half his face open, and Devan shook his head before the man could speak.

"Hold the gate," he said. "Nobody follows."

* * *

He did not take a lamp. He took the grey mare, because she knew the wood road in the dark, and he took no one, because no one else could be told why.

The padippura swung wide for him on Unni's word. The night watch did not ask where. The cistern chain clanked behind him at the hour, and then the dark came down on the road, and the smell of wet laterite, and the langurs fell silent on the cliff as he passed.

He was not ready. He had asked for a day in the rain, and he had meant it. He had not decided what he owed the woman who had read *Lookout, Seventeen* and waited to be sentenced. He had not decided what the court would hear, or in what order, or from whom. The eleven leaves lay in him unsorted, and the hard thing had not settled.

None of it mattered.

The welt on his ribs was shallow. He rode as if it were mortal.
