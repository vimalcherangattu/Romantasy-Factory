# Chapter 6: The Ledger He Needs

"Bring me the guild's paper," said Rema, "and leave your grief at the door."

She said it to Devan, across the tally chest, with the lamps burning down the length of the hall. Neela stood at his shoulder and counted the eight pillars again, because her hands wanted something to do.

The sitting had run two hours. Rema had counted wax, weighed the satchel, and made Bhaskaran swear to the cart that passed on a council tally at the new moon before the wedding. She had been exact and dry and fair, and Neela had hated every minute of it. A fair justice is the hardest kind to lie to.

"You have a dead man's tally book," Rema went on. "It shows payments to a house in the Blackbark Wood and a mark I'm told means *delivered*. You have a slip in resin-ink that your wife says came off a courier. A slip is a slip. A sister's drawn parrot is a story." She did not look at Neela when she said *wife*. "But a house that sells deaths has to bill for them. Someone writes down who paid, who died and who was sent. Bring me that. Ink I can swear to, laid on a bench where a bench can see it."

"The guild keeps its contracts in one book," Devan said.

"Then it keeps them where I can reach them, or I have heard a great deal of grief for nothing." Rema folded her hands. "I've seen yours, Warden. It doesn't weigh anything in court. The paper might."

She dismissed them with a nod. The case with the second seal stayed unopened under her palm.

Neela walked out beside him and said nothing until the hall door shut. Then she said, "Contract books."

"Yes."

"She thinks they're only that."

"What else would she think?" He looked at her sideways. "What else are they?"

Neela did not answer. She was already counting the ways it could go wrong.

* * *

Devan wrote the letter at the table in the warden's chamber, by the one lamp, with the shutters barred behind him.

She had expected a short one. A man who answers questions with exact facts does not write long. But he had been at it a quarter of an hour, and the pen kept stopping. The brown jar stood by his elbow. It had come out of Ittan's writing case that afternoon with the lid still sticky. Kesu's grandson would carry it down the goat path at first light, wrapped in wet cloth against the cold.

Neela sat on the bed with her bandaged hand in her lap and watched him.

"You can read it," he said, without turning.

"I'd rather not."

"Then watch the wax instead."

She watched. He wrote to the household of the Keeper of the Passes, in Kodiyur. She caught that much over his shoulder, and a line near the foot: *He asked that you keep the window open in rain. I have promised it will be remembered.* He did not soften the first line, which said her husband had died at dawn by the queen's warrant. He did not blame anyone for it. At the end he wrote the words *the account of Kettamala*, and signed.

The wax was plain black, not the warden's red. He pressed the iron disc into it, a hill and a gate, and held it a breath longer than it needed.

"He bought my death," Devan said. "He bought hers." He did not say whose. "And he loved his wife. All of it was in the same case."

"You said that as if it were a sum."

"It is." He set down the disc. "I don't know how to carry the other thing, so I'm doing the arithmetic."

Neela thought about that. She thought about Chathu, who wept when members died and sold their pages afterward, and how she had spent twenty years unable to see that this was one man and not two. She doubted she could do what Devan had just done, with a pen, for Ittan's widow. She was not sure she would try.

"What if she hates you for it?" she said. "The letter."

"Then she'll have a jar and a roof, and she can hate me with a cough medicine in her hand." He turned the paper to dry. "Ask me the real question, Neela."

"I don't have one."

"You always have one. You ask the small ones so you don't have to answer mine."

It stung because it was true. She let it sting.

"What do you want from the book?" she said. "Not what Rema wants. You."

He took his time. The chain struck the hour, iron on iron, from somewhere below.

"I want the ledger in front of the high court before the monsoon breaks," he said. "Every contract in it. The Asha leaf, the pass-seller's leaves, the dates, the hands. Rema is a start. If she carries the paper to Kodiyur, there's a bench that has to look. I've spent six years being told I had nothing. I want to be the man who laid it down."

"And the rest of the book?"

"What rest?"

She had known it would come to this, and still her tongue felt thick. "The ledger isn't contracts, Devan. It's two kinds of leaf sewn into one cover. The front half is ink. Ordinary ink, no magic in it, in Resin House cipher. Who paid, who was sent. You couldn't read a line." She laid her left hand flat on her knee, the three bound nails curled. "The back is pages. Mine was in it. Every odiyan's is. Resin-ink, one true witness's hand, and whoever holds a page and reads it aloud at night has the power of the one who wrote it. It passes with the page."

"I know what a page is."

"You know it as a rule. I know it as a kitchen drawer I was kept in." She breathed. "If you give the court the book, you give it all of it. The court becomes the holder. It holds forty lives, and forty more in the kennels who can't be helped by anything now. And a court that holds a page becomes a master, whatever it swears. It doesn't have to want to. It only has to keep the book."

"The court isn't Chathu."

"No. It's better at it. It has a seal and a law to say why."

He was silent. She watched his jaw work, not in anger. He was looking for the hole in what she'd said, and he was a fair man, so he looked hard and did not find one.

"Then give me the other side," he said. "What happens if I don't take it to the court? If you hold it, or I hold it, and no bench ever sees a leaf of it? Asha stays a thing I believe. Ittan is dead with his mouth shut. Whoever bought her death keeps the name." His voice stayed level. "It's my sister, Neela. I want the name of the hand set down in ink. I want to hear it read out. Do you know how long I have waited to be told one true thing about that night?"

"I know."

"Then you know what you're asking me to set down."

She did. It was the worst of it. He needed the book and she feared what the book would become, and the space between them had a person's shape.

"I'm not asking you to set it down," she said. "I'm asking you not to hand the whole thing over for the sake of the half you need."

"That's what the court is for."

"That's what the court *says* it's for." She stood. Her legs were not steady. The nails, she thought, or the hollow place under her ribs that was past her last proper resin and getting worse. "And you can't read the front half either. Not a leaf. I'll have to read every contract aloud to you, in my own voice, and you'll hear what I was in each one. Ask me the next question. A question for a question. You've earned it."

"What's on yours?"

She had not expected that. "My page?"

"The one that burned. The witness entry went. You told me something stayed."

"A slip." The word came out low. "Sewn behind it. *Intake, sold at six years.* Two lines I couldn't read in the mud. Chathu put his thumb on it and took it." She heard her own voice and did not like it. "My name's on it, or my village. I think. I don't know what I'd do with it if I had it. I'd read it alone, maybe, with the door shut. That's the whole of what I want from that book, Devan, and it's small, and I would not trade it for a bench in Kodiyur."

He had gone very still. Then he rose from the table and stood in front of her and did not touch her.

"I did not know it was that small," he said.

"It's the smallest thing I own. It's why I'm frightened."

* * *

They settled it standing up, which was how both of them settled things.

"Say it as a bargain," Devan said. "I'll not agree to what I don't understand."

"The contract leaves come loose at the stitching. They're ordinary. They can't cage anyone. You take them to Rema, to the high court, to anyone who'll hear them." She counted on her fingers, as she did. "The pages go back to the people they belong to. By daylight, read aloud in full, to her face."

"By the holder."

"By whoever holds the book. I won't give that part away to the queen."

"Everyone in the room hears the night-shape."

"Everyone in the room becomes her witness." She did not flinch from it. "That's the price of handing back a page. Your own court clerk, if you bring one. Your captain, if you bring him."

He took it in. She watched him do it. "Then there are no clerks in the room. Only the holder and the one whose page it is, and whoever she chooses."

"That's a better rule than mine." It cost her something to say. "Add it."

"And the rest of the book? Anything neither of us thought of?"

"I'd burn it. I'd burn all of the back half, one at a time, by the owner's hand. But some of them have been rogue in the eyes of the crown since before I was born." She spread the good hand. "I can't promise that either. Not yet."

"Contract books to the court," Devan said. "Pages to their owners."

"A first term."

"A first term." He held out his hand and she took it, as she had taken it in the rain, quick and hard. Then he said, in the flat way he had of saying things that cost him, "I don't think you're right about the court."

"I don't think you're right about the dead."

"Then neither of us is wrong." He let go. "I'd rather that than a silence."

It was the oddest compliment she had ever been paid. She took it with both hands.

"There may be more in the contract leaves than you want," she said, careful with it. She thought of a hill fort she had seen blindfolded, and of nothing she could say. "Whatever's written, I'll read it to you the way it's written. It'll be written true."

"I know."

"Do you?"

"No." He looked at the shutter, at the third slat that creaked. "But I won't shut the book because it might hold a thing I can't bear. That's how I lived for six years. I'd like to try the other way."

She said nothing. She could not. The only answer she had was a stair she could not place, a woman she never saw, and a number.

* * *

At dusk the next day he went to close the shutters, and she stopped him.

"Not tonight."

He turned with his hand on the bar. There was a river pebble in his cheek. She had not noticed it until the words came out round it. He took it out and held it in his palm, and looked as if he had been caught praying.

"You're not—"

"I'm not." She sat on the window ledge with her back to the dark, where the dusk burn had begun faintly in the scars at her throat, and did not turn from it. "There's one smear of Ammu's resin left in the pouch. I spent the other the night of the execution, on the ridge, to see who walked the road. I'm keeping this one."

"For what?"

"For inside the compound." She pulled her bangle higher on her wrist and let the scars show, pale and ridged. "I can't spend it on the road to the groves. And I can't have the ache take me before then. It's coming. My nails split on the stair this morning, and the night the justice came I woke in a sweat that wasn't the rain." She did not say how near. She had counted. "A moon with no proper resin, and I'll be on a floor."

Devan set the pebble on the sill, beside the other one.

"Then what do we do?"

"We?"

"I asked it as a question."

She nearly smiled. "We do the oldest thing a rogue odiyan does." She looked past him at the shutters, and the dark beyond them, and the wood a day's hard ride off, in the rain. "I have to rob Chathu's groves before I can rob Chathu."

He did not ask if she was sure. He said, "Rema will call you to swear."

"I know."

"She's sat on that second case since the road. She'll open it when she's counted the tallies, and you're named in the closing writ whether you stand there or not." He said it flatly. "If you're gone when she calls, she'll read it as flight."

"Then she'll read it." Neela made herself hold his eyes. "And I'll have missed whatever's in the case. If it names me, I'll hear it second-hand, from you, with no chance to answer. That's the cost. I've counted it."

"And the alternative?"

"Sit in this room while the ache takes my hands, and then I can't swear to anything, or turn, or hold a knife." She held up her wrapped hand. "Resin is the only thing I own that the guild doesn't. I'd have to steal it anyway. Better now, while I can still climb a wall."

"We ride tonight," he said.

"At full dark. The wood is a day's hard ride, so we reach the trees by noon. Nightbark can be tapped at noon, with a knife and a flask. It only burns like fire if you wear it on bare skin. And the book I came to steal is in the same compound as the trees."

"They'll have walls on the groves."

"They'll have hunters." She heard how it sounded. "I know the ridges. I know a drain that isn't a path. I'll go over as a woman and keep the smear for the night."

"And I'll ride with you."

"You have a fort to hold. You have a justice in your chair."

"I have a captain for the fort." He said it without smiling. "And Rema asked for the guild's paper. Muthu can tell her at first light that my wife and I have gone to fetch it. She can hardly call that grief." He paused. "I have a wife who is going to cut a tree the guild owns, and a knife wound of hers will open on me wherever I am. I'd rather be near enough to hand her a cloth."

She looked at him. He looked back, steadily, as a man does who has made a plain statement of fact.

"You're a nuisance," she said.

"Yes."

"I'll need you to stay behind a tree."

"I'll try."

She turned her face to the dusk. The burn in her throat eased, as it did when she did not take the resin. A breath, and it was gone, and she was only a woman on a ledge with the dark going blue behind her.

She took the flask from the sill, the one she had carried out of Kodiyur and drained weeks ago, and thumbed out the cork. It was dry inside, and it smelled of almost nothing at all.

"Then we'd better fill it," she said, and held it out into the last of the light.
