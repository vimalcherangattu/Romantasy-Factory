# Chapter 25: The Gate Shut

Devan left the assize table in the middle of a sentence.

Neela watched it from the hall door. Rema had called the sitting at first grey, the moment Vasu's news was in, and had just asked a clerk to read Eravi's dated account into the roll for the second time. Devan stood, said "Forgive me," and walked out past eight pillars and forty-odd faces. He didn't hurry. A man who runs from a hearing gives it a story. He gave it a back.

Rema looked up from the warden's chair. She did not call him back. She dipped her pen.

"The court notes the warden's absence," she said, and wrote it, and the scratch of the nib followed Neela out like a hand on her collar.

Within the hour they were on the wood road, the morning barely begun. Devan, Neela, Bhaskaran and eight Kettamala spearmen rode two by two on wet clay, with the grey mare favouring her off hind under Neela. The ledger rode in her saddlebag, wrapped in greased cloth. She felt it against her knee at every stride, a block of other people's names.

Nobody spoke for the first long stretch. The wood closed over them, black trunks and dripping leaf, and the langurs in the high branches went silent as the column passed.

"Thirty-one real names on that plank," she said at last. "Nine blanks."

"I know."

"I told Twenty-two I couldn't give hers yet. I said a name means reading the entry aloud, and I couldn't do it safely." She stared between the mare's ears. "I wanted the reading day to be right. Daylight, the holder and the owner, no clerks. I wanted every one of them to pick who sat in the room. That took until after the assize."

"It was a good plan."

"It was slow."

He didn't answer that, and she didn't ask him to.

* * *

They reached the last rise in the long gold light of late afternoon. The Resin House sat in its clearing below, laterite wall and tapping sheds and the bitter smell of scorched tamarind coming up the slope on the wind. The gate was shut. The sun stood a hand above the trees.

The walls were full.

She counted without meaning to, because counting was what she did when she was afraid. Hunters first: eight at the gatehouse, more along the north wall, nets coiled on their shoulders, hooked poles ready. Then the others, standing among them in the open. Grey wraps and cropped heads, thin wrists. Men and women she had braided hair for and shared a bench with. They stood where the hunters had put them, with a pole's length of air at each shoulder.

Thirty-nine.

She went along the wall again and got the same number. One short. Beyond the wall, in the lee of the tapping sheds, five men in Kettamala brown huddled round a cold fire, and Pavi's curled old-broken hand showed among them. They were outside their own wall, and they looked up at her like drowned things. Pavi was the one of the forty Chathu didn't hold.

"Eight hunters at the gate," Bhaskaran said quietly. "More inside. We are eleven."

"And five outside," Devan said.

"Five who can't climb it."

Neela rode down alone to the edge of bowshot and reined in. She said the names. It was a habit, and now it was a plan. "Thilak. Madhavi. Kuttu. Meenu." Some of the faces flinched. One or two looked at their feet. Meenu, who had cried at the roll leaf, was pressed against the stone as if it might open. Twenty-two stood second from the end. She was looking straight at Neela with her mouth shut.

Then a voice came down from the gatehouse, thin and cracked.

"Master!"

Thilak, forty-one, tapper before he was an odiyan, stood at the parapet with a hunter's pole laid across the back of his knees. He had brought her tea every evening she held the House and never once asked her for a thing. His hands gripped the stone. She saw why he'd been chosen to shout. No one else on that wall would say *Master* to her and mean it as a plea.

"You kept the book!" His voice had gone high and steady, a man reading from a slate. "So did he. He says he'll burn ours."

*Ours.* It took her a breath. Not the pages. She felt it fall into place cold and exact: Chathu had kept the other thing. He had kept the trees.

"Thilak," she said.

"I'm to say it twice." His eyes held hers. They said what his mouth couldn't, and she took it, the only gift he had. "I've said it."

The gate wicket opened above the arch. A man came out onto the gatehouse walk, one foot uneven from an old break, one hand resting lightly on the wall as if on the head of a child.

"Seventeen," Chathu said.

He didn't raise his voice. He never had. It came down warm and soft and tired, the voice of a man calling a girl in from the rain. Even the hunters on the wall seemed to lean into it.

"I won't ask you to come in. I know you can't hear that from me. Let me say a smaller thing." He spread his hands. "The groves for the book. That is all. You keep the thirty-one names you've written on your plank, and the nine you haven't. You keep your husband. You ride home. I keep the House, and the trees stay standing, and the resin comes as it always has."

"And if I don't?"

"Then it doesn't." He said it sadly. "I have a fire in the oil store, child, and a man at the nearest grove with a torch. I'd rather not. The nightbark takes forty years to bear. But you know what a moon without resin does. They ache first. You'll ache. You've had the first of it already." His eyes went to her hands on the rein, to the nails she hadn't hidden. "Do you imagine I haven't counted yours?"

She hadn't thought he would. Her hands tightened, and she made them loosen.

"I call that a fair price," Chathu said.

He believed it. That was the worst of it. He was doing his sums, as he always had, and she was a debt on the page.

Neela sat very still. She thought of the plank in the carpentry shed and the lamp-black names, and of Twenty-two asking, *Does my roll name me?* She thought of the reading day she had put off. Until the assize. Until the right day. Until it could be done safely and with consent and by daylight, with no clerk in the room.

She had called it protection. It had been a delay, and Chathu had gone straight through it like a man through a door left on the latch. While she waited to be right, he had come back out of the wood with his nets and his poles. She had left the forty their names unread, and he had walked in and found them unowned.

*I handed him back what I'd taken.*

It was not a thought she could argue with. She looked at it plainly and it did not get smaller.

* * *

Something moved at Chathu's shoulder.

A man had come up beside him on the walk, big and bearded and slow. He wore a plain brown wrap, and he came on a short stiff stride that hitched on the left leg, a drag and a step, though he was not in his shape. He didn't look at the road. He sat on the stone coping with his back to the drop, drew a knife, and began to carve.

Vellan.

Neela had seen him from a dozen angles. In the Resin House yard when she was nine, vast and quiet. At the meeting stone in daylight with a wooden bird in his hand. In the ledger, in Chathu's neat black ink: *Hand, Thirty.* She had never seen him look so ordinary. A man whittling in the sun.

Beside her Devan went still.

She did not turn. She felt it all the same, along the length of the saddle, a change in the air at her left side. His reins stayed slack. His breath went in and did not come out for a long time.

He knew who it was. He had never seen that face by day, and he knew it, the way you know a stair in the dark.

Vellan's knife turned. A curl of pale wood fell. He lifted the thing in his hand: plum-sized, a bird, one wing folded, the head turned to listen. He blew the dust off its beak.

Then he looked down at her.

Not at Devan, who had every right to a look. Not at Bhaskaran with his spear, or the Kettamala horses. At her, only, with a flat tired steadiness that held no threat and no regret and no apology either. It was the look of a man checking that a thing he had set down was still where he'd left it.

She thought: *He's waiting to see which of us breaks.*

She would not break first. She sat the mare and looked back. In the end it was Vellan who dropped his eyes, to the bird, and went on carving.

* * *

"Seventeen," Chathu said again. "Hear the rest."

"I'm listening."

"At dusk I'll put resin on every one of them on this wall. Not the way you do it, with a hand and a choice. The way the House does it when it must." He said it without relish, the way he'd have said *the rice is late*. "They'll turn on the stroke of sunset, and it will tear them. You've felt that. Some will bleed a week. Then the gate opens, and they go into the wood. Animals with their minds whole and every hunger of the shape in them, and the nearest living things on the hill will be you and the man beside you."

A breath went along the wall. Neela saw Meenu's mouth open and shut.

"Don't think they'll refuse you the kindness," Chathu went on. "I was the first witness at every one of their turnings. I'll stand on this wall with the word in my mouth, and any of them who will not hunt you, I'll cage where he stands. And my hunters hold the wood road behind you. There is no riding home tonight, child. There is only the hill."

He leaned on the coping. "You have their pages. I know you do. They are in the bag against your knee. At dusk you can read them. One by one, in the dark, in their hearing. That is a witness's power, and it is yours." His voice softened further. "Cage them, and they stop. They'll be in the kennels with the room I have ready. Or don't, and they'll find you. That's all it is. A choice. I only describe it."

"You'd do that to them."

"I'd do it *for* them. To keep a House together. To keep them fed." He looked genuinely grieved. "You'll learn it, if you keep the book. Nobody holds a page and stays clean of it. I was twenty-six when I learned."

"Chathu."

"Until dusk," he said, and turned, and went in. The wicket shut. Vellan stayed a moment on the coping and then rose, with that hitch, and followed him.

* * *

The wall did not empty. The hunters stayed, and the thirty-nine stood where they were put, with the sun going down behind the trees.

Neela turned the mare. Her legs felt hollow. Devan fell in beside her, and they walked the horses up the slope to where Bhaskaran's men had drawn together, out of bowshot, on a bare shoulder of ground above the road.

She looked back once, down the way they had come. Two hunters stood in the road where it left the rise, nets over their shoulders, where an hour ago there had been no one. As she watched, a third stepped out beside them.

The sun was still up. The thirty-nine were within hearing. She made herself look at the plain thing: she could ride down now, break the seals, and read the pages back in daylight, as she had always meant to.

She turned it over and it did not hold. A handover was read in full, and a full reading said every night-shape aloud. Every hunter on that wall would hear them, thirty-nine words handed to men with nets and rings. And Chathu would still stand there, the first witness at every turning, with his own mouth. Pages in their hands would not unmake him. At dusk he could cage whoever he pleased. She would have made herself a master for nothing, and told his hunters the shapes besides.

So it was the binary he had given her, with the sun already going.

She waited for the other thing.

She knew exactly what it would be. *I asked you to read them the day we rode out.* He had asked, on the ridge road, without heat. *Read them now, before the assize.* She had said no, and given a good reason. He could say it now, and be right, and she would have to carry it. She wanted him to. It would have been a thing she could be angry at.

He said nothing.

He sat the horse with his hands on the pommel and his eyes on the wall, and his face was grey under the road mud. A small muscle worked at the corner of his jaw. She found she was counting again, under her breath, the hunters on the north wall, because her mind wanted something to hold. Her lips moved. She lost the number at the third pole.

"Nine along the north," Devan said, very low.

She stopped.

"Eight at the gate," he said. "Two on the corner post. That's nineteen." His eyes didn't leave the wall. He was counting for her, in her order, gate first and then the north, and he had learned the order from watching her lips. "Go on."

She went on. She named the posts and he gave the numbers back, steady as a man calling out soundings. When she reached the end of the wall she was breathing evenly, and the number she had was a real one. He turned his horse a half step, and the animal's shoulder came between her and the place where Meenu stood against the stone.

"You could say it," she said.

"I could." He said it quite evenly. "It wouldn't undo one hour."

That was all. She looked at him, at the hands loose on the pommel, and her throat closed.

"Whatever I do," she said, "it lands on you too."

"Yes."

"If I read them, I'm a master. Your wife is a master, and the crown has my name beside forty." She heard herself and made herself go on. "And if I don't, they'll hunt us, and you'll have to fight my sisters and brothers, and you have already struck a man for me this winter, and you can't tell me what you'll be after that."

"I can't."

"And you're not telling me what to do."

"No." The wind lifted the wet hair off his brow. "What you do is yours. I'll carry my half, whichever way."

She nodded, because her throat would not do anything else.

Behind the wall a bell began to ring. It was the tapping bell, thin and sweet, calling the long table to its meal. Out on the wall a hunter laughed at something, short and bored, and the thirty-nine did not move. The sun touched the tops of the nightbark.

She had less than an hour. She had a bag against her knee, with forty names in it, and a man beside her who would not tell her what to do.

*Cage them with their pages,* Chathu had said, *or be hunted by them.*

She did not yet know which of those she could survive being.
