# Chapter 23: Ten Paces

Janu stayed where she was. She stood with a lamp in each hand and looked at Neela's bare palm, still pale where it had pressed the stone.

"You're at the stair," she said.

"Yes."

"You never come to the stair." The scold was in her voice, but it had thinned. "Eravi's done your dressing? Good. Come in. The plantain will go leathery."

"I need to tell you something first." Neela's voice came out level, and she hated how it sounded, like a clerk's. "You'll want to stay where you are."

"I'll decide where I stand in my own court."

"Then stand there."

Across the flags, Devan did not move. She kept her eyes on Janu's face, on the creases at the corners of the mouth, the brass key on its cord.

"Six years ago, the night Asha died, I was here," she said. "I stood at the foot of this stair. I held a lamp."

Janu's face stayed as it was. The flames in her two lamps stood straight and still.

"A shuttered lamp," Neela said. "I was told to open it twice if anyone came into the court. No one came. Someone went up the stair past me, and I held the light. I didn't move. I didn't call out. I never went up."

"What lamp?"

"I was part of it. I'm not saying I knew her name. I'm saying I was there, and I was part of it, and it was me."

The cistern chain hung quiet. A swift went over the court, high, going home.

"Why?" Janu said.

It was a flat word. She might have been asking why the oil was short.

Neela had thought it through on the climb, on the way down, and all the way across the wet road from the wood. She had seen where she would put it. Late. She would put it last, after the plain thing was out, and she would not lean on it. Now it was asked, and she answered.

"Because I was sent. I was sold at six to a house that kills for coin. They raised me. They put a lamp in my hand and a name in my head, and I did what I was put there for. They made me, Janu." She heard it come out and stopped. "That's the true answer. It isn't a reason. I was twenty-one. I was good at my work, and I did it well, and I never once asked whose stair it was."

She stopped there. She didn't say *I was a child*, because she hadn't been. She didn't say *I didn't know*, because she had known enough.

Janu set the lamps down.

She did it carefully, one and then the other, on the flags, the way she set a full pot on the table. She straightened. Then she walked.

Neela counted without meaning to. One. Two. The small quick step she knew from the kitchen, the step that went round a house and missed nothing. Three. Four. Janu's eyes were on the stair. Five, six, seven. Eight. Nine.

On ten she stopped, an arm's length from Neela, at the foot of the stair for the first time in six years. Neela braced to be struck.

Janu's hand went past her and slapped the newel.

The flat of it, hard, on the carved bird. The sound cracked across the court. Then again, and again, with a small furious noise in her throat. Her palm must have been stinging. She struck the broken beak with the heel of her hand, as if it were a child that had hidden something.

"Stupid thing," she said. "Stupid, *stupid*."

Then her hand closed on the stone and held on. Her shoulders went down.

"I was asleep," Janu said.

Neela said nothing.

"She said keep her supper warm. She was late. She was always late. She was on the wall, or in the barracks with her slate, or down the village, and she'd come in at all hours with her cap in her hand saying she'd eat standing." Janu's eyes were on the bird. "I put the rice by the kitchen fire. I sat down beside it to wait. Only for a moment, and I slept. I woke at the cock. The pot had burnt dry and the fire was ash." She breathed. "I came out for water in the grey. I found her here."

"Janu."

"I'll not hear that it wasn't my fault." The words came sharp, and Neela closed her mouth. "I know what people say. The kitchen's across the court. The doors were shut. No one could have heard. I've said it to myself six years, and it's true, and it makes no difference. I ought to have been awake."

"I wasn't going to say it."

"Good." Janu turned her head at last and looked at her. Her eyes were red-rimmed and dry. "You'd have had a hard time saying it. Of everyone in this fort."

Neela took that. It was fair. She stood and let it land.

Ten paces. She thought of Janu's tread going round the court every dusk and stopping short of the foot of the stair. She thought of the key on its cord and the shut door at the end of the south passage. Six years of keeping the house in order so nothing else would break.

"One thing," Janu said. "I'll ask you the one thing, and you'll tell me true."

"I will."

"Was she afraid?"

The court was very quiet. Neela had known the question was coming, though not from where. She had known it from the moment her hand touched the newel, and she had no soft answer ready. She hadn't tried to build one.

"She asked who was there," she said. "From the top of the stair. She had a laugh in her voice. It was just fading, like she'd been in the middle of something. She wasn't afraid yet. She called down, *Who's there? Who is that?*"

Janu's hand tightened on the bird.

"I was holding the light," Neela said. "She could see it. I had it on the bottom step. And I didn't answer. I stood in the light with my mouth shut and she asked twice, and I said nothing."

For a long moment Janu was silent. A muscle moved in her cheek.

"She was frightened of the dark," she said at last. "From a tiny thing. I'd leave a lamp in her room and she'd count the stairs going down so she'd know where the floor was." Her voice was steady, which was terrible. "She'd have seen a light at the foot and thought it was a friend's."

"Yes," Neela said. "I think she did."

She added nothing. She had learned that much from the master's room and from the deck of a boy's barge and from eleven tins of sweets: the extra word was where the leash went in.

Janu took her hand off the newel.

"I don't forgive you," she said.

"No."

"I don't know that I can. I'll not lie to you. If I say it tonight I'll mean it for an hour, and then I'll find I don't." She wiped her palm down her wrap, once, the way she wiped it before kneading. "I'm sixty-one. I haven't the time to say what I don't mean."

"I wouldn't take it if you did."

"Good." Janu looked at her, and then past her at the dark stair, at the forty-four steps going up to a passage with a window at the end of it. Her mouth worked. Then she bent and picked up one of her lamps.

"Come and eat," she said.

Neela could not move.

"The plantain's drying. I didn't stand over that fire for a woman who'll let it go cold." Janu held out the lamp. "Take this. The court's dark. Mind the step by the well."

Neela's hands were cold. She took the lamp. The brass was warm from Janu's fingers, and the flame stood in it small and upright and steady, and she looked at it for a breath because she couldn't look at Janu.

Janu bent for the second lamp. She straightened with a hand to the small of her back.

"You'll tell the rest," she said, not turning. "Not tonight. But you'll tell me, in order, with nothing left out. I'll know when you leave something out."

"All of it."

"Hm." It was the sound she made at a pot she had decided to trust. She went away across the flags with her small quick step, and passed the corner without a pause.

* * *

Neela stood holding the lamp until the kitchen door shut.

Then she breathed in, and out, and a sound came out of her that was not a word. She set the lamp on the bottom step, next to the bird, where it laid a bar of yellow across the stone. She stared at it. She picked it up again and put it on the well curb instead, as far from the stair as the curb would let her.

Boots crossed the court. This time they didn't stop twenty paces away.

Devan stopped an arm's length off, where Janu had stood. He had said he would stay at the well post, and he had. He hadn't spoken, or moved, from the first sentence to the last. She could see what it had cost him in the set of his shoulders.

"You didn't speak," she said.

"You asked me not to."

"I know. I didn't think you could do it."

"Neither did I." His voice was rough. "I counted the court stones. There are eighty-six between the post and the kitchen door."

A laugh got halfway out of her and turned into something wetter. She pressed the back of her wrist to her mouth. He didn't reach for her. His hands were open at his sides.

She looked up and saw that he was looking at her.

It struck her how long it had been. Not the glance across a room, or the nod at the gate. He had looked at her, but the way a man reads a page with his thumb over one line, taking in everything else and keeping clear of the part that hurt. Since the ledger's last quire he had found the table to speak to, or the road, or her shoulder.

Now he was looking at her face. He wasn't hurrying to finish.

"You're looking at me," she said.

"Yes."

"You haven't, for three days. Not properly."

"I didn't know where to put my eyes." He said it plainly, as he gave the count of a patrol. "I looked at you and saw a lamp on a step. I couldn't make it stand still. I knew it was the same woman. I couldn't find how."

"And now?"

"You told her what you did before she asked what you'd done." His eyes held. "You said *they made me* only when she asked. You said it, and then you said it was no reason. I stood there and listened to you not defending yourself." He drew a breath. "I can look at that."

She wanted to say something dry. She had nothing dry in her.

"Don't thank me," she said instead. "Don't tell me it was brave. She still doesn't forgive it."

"I wasn't going to." His mouth moved, very slightly. "I was going to say you have red earth on your sleeve from the newel, and I'm going to let you keep it."

She looked down. There was a red smear on her cuff, from the bird. She rubbed at it with her thumb and made it worse, and she laughed again, shorter, and found she could stand up straight.

"I haven't got it all out," she said. "The rest of what I owe. But that was one."

"It was one."

"Janu's right about the plantain."

"She usually is."

Neela turned toward the kitchen, and her eye caught the side of the stair. A low arch of laterite where the treads rose over the wall. At the foot of the arch, half hidden by the lip of the eleventh step, was the iron grate she had seen on the day she walked round the newel.

She had meant to ask about it then. She had told no one. She had kept it behind the thing she couldn't say.

"Devan." She crouched. "Come and look at this."

He crouched beside her. She put a finger through the bars to the bolt heads. They were bright. The edges of each were still sharp, cut clean with a file, and not a flake of rust on them.

"The step rings hollow over a drain slot," she said. "I knew that. I've stood on it. But this isn't the drain slot, it's a grate in the wall under the stair. The wall is six years old, or more. This iron isn't." She turned a bolt with her thumbnail. "Rust takes a year in this damp. Two winters on laterite and these would be orange. It's been put in lately, and by someone with a file."

He said nothing. He had gone very still, the way he had in the lamplight at the Resin House with the leaf under his hand.

"Asha was found on this stair," Neela said. "A bear's wounds. Inside walls no bear can climb. Every man in this fort can tell you how she was found. I've heard it told at the well and let them tell it, and I knew what kind of bear. I didn't let myself ask how it got in." She looked up. "Someone opened a door."

"The padippura was held. Four men."

"The padippura was held that night, yes. And this is not the padippura."

He rose. He said nothing for the length of one slow breath. Her eyes went, before she could stop them, across the north court to the dispensary window, a small square of yellow where the old man worked late. She looked away. Nobody had said anything yet. She did not mean to be the one.

Devan had followed the glance. He did not follow it with a word.

"Not a shout across the court," he said, very low. "Someone in this house talks to the guild. Gopan lights the stair lamps at this hour. He'll come to us."

They did not wait long. The old keeper of the cistern came across the flags with an oil can in one hand and a bunch of wicks in the other, as he did each dusk. He had been in the post nineteen years, and he walked as if the court were a shelf he had stocked himself. He reached the stair lamps and set down the can.

Devan crouched at the grate again, so that he and Gopan were of a height and the court saw two men at a lamp.

"Gopan," he said, barely above the oil's gurgle. "This grate. How long has it been iron?"

Gopan squinted. He grunted down onto one knee and laid two knuckles against the bars.

"That's not mine," he said slowly.

"Whose is it?"

"I couldn't say. The old gate was wood, strapped with iron, and rusted to lace. It's the slop-gate, the old postern. Opens on the outside path, under the wall." He rapped a bar. "Never opened in my time. I used to go round by the outside path in the damp months and feel it over for swelling." He frowned at the bolts. "Wood's gone, I see. New iron. Somebody's had a smith up."

"I gave no order for it."

"No, Warden. Nor your father." Gopan said it with the plain honesty of a man who had never been asked to guess. "But I'd not have seen a smith. The lip of that step hides the grate from above unless you kneel, and outside there's a bank of lantana grown over the path, thick as a hedge. I've been meaning to cut it these ten years. And the fever year I kept the chain from a cot, and the sergeant's lads walked my rounds. Before the lantana closed the path, I put my hand through to the lock, felt it hanging where it always hung, and thought no more."

"The lock?" Neela said.

"Big as my fist. Same one." He felt past the bars for it, and his knuckles knocked iron. "There. Old lock, moved to the new door. Same key still fits it, I'd wager. Why would a man buy iron and keep the old lock?"

"Because he wanted nobody to look twice," she said. She spoke to Devan, but her eyes were on the bolts. "The postern was there six years ago. It was there that night. New iron means someone still uses it, and has kept it oiled and quiet, and kept the same key."

"Then who has the key?" Devan said.

Gopan looked up at him, and then at her. His old eyes crinkled with something like surprise at being asked at all.

"I haven't," he said. "Nor the sergeant. Nor Janu, for all she keeps every other." He pulled himself up by the arch with a creak. "There's only the one that ever carried it. Only the physician keeps that key."

The chain clanked above them, the hour, once and then again.

Neela didn't look at Devan. She saw the dispensary, the bark on its beam, eleven bundles and nine of them willow. She saw the satchel on the stool with its flap hanging open on the dark. A pair of green-stained cuffs, wrapping a dressing. A kind old voice asking which day.

She straightened, and the bright bolts were at the height of her hand. Across the court, the dispensary window burned small and yellow.
