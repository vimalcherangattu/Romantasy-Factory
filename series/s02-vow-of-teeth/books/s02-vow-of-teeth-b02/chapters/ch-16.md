# Chapter 16: Lookout: Seventeen

They bowed.

Nobody gave the word. Pavi went first, the young woman with the white scar across her chin. She folded from the waist and made her curled right hand lie flat on her thigh beside the left. The row behind her followed, then the next. The bow ran back across the grey yard like wind through young rice, until Neela was looking at forty partings and no faces.

It was the bow they gave Chathu at the long table. She knew it in her knees. She had given it every night since she was six, until she learned to wait for his back to turn.

The black book lay against her chest. Greased cloth, scorched tamarind. Its corner pressed the claw lines along her flank, and each breath found the place.

*Which am I?*

A sister would bow back. An owner would let them stay down. She stood between the two and could not feel which one her body meant to be.

"Up," she said.

They did not move. Her voice was hoarse from the turning. Perhaps it hadn't carried.

"Up. Please."

They rose together, all forty, in one breath.

That was the answer, and she hated it. No one rises together for *please*. They rose for the weight in her arms.

"Pavi," she said. "Is the cook-house open?"

"Barred from inside, Seventeen. After the table."

The number landed like a hand on a bruise. Neela kept her face still. "It's Neela. You can use that." She looked along the rows. "Open it. Eat what there is. Everyone."

Nobody moved until Pavi did. Then they all went, quietly, the way they had been taught to go.

Devan had not stirred from her shoulder. He stood with his arms at his sides and his shirt dried stiff across the back where the welts had wept. He watched the last of them cross the yard. Then he gave Bhaskaran two low orders about the gate and the wall. When he turned back, his face held nothing but the question.

"Where would he keep what I can't read?"

"His room," she said.

* * *

She had been called through that door twelve times, once before each contract of her own. She had never once been called for anything good. The lookout night had come with no summons at all, only a hand on her shoulder in the dark and a cloth tied over her eyes.

By daylight it was only a room. Scrubbed boards. A low desk with an abacus on it. A shelf of small, neat account books, one for each year, each spine lettered in the fine hand she knew. A tin sat on the end of the shelf with its lid pressed down.

She did not look at the tin twice.

Devan took the stool, stood up from it again, and sat on the boards with his back to the wall. He did it without a word. She had seen him sleep that way and had not asked.

Rain started on the tiles, a few fat drops and then a steady drumming.

She laid the book on the desk and turned it so the back half, the resin-ink pages, faced the wall. She did not open it. Then she unwrapped the front. The contract leaves came loose from their stitching in her hands, as she had known they would. Plain ink, in hooks and half-moons like bird tracks in wet sand.

"Daylight," she said. "Aloud. In front of you. Every word, in order, and nothing I skip without saying so."

"In order," said Devan.

She had learned this cipher at the long table, before she had a name to write in it. She found the first leaf. It was an index, in Chathu's hand, the kind a man makes when he cannot bear a thing he owns to be mislaid. Year, place, leaf. She read it aloud as it came, flat, one line after the next, and Devan listened with his head against the wall.

The first heading was *Released.*

"I'll start there," she said. "It's first in the index, and it's mine to check. Ten breaths. Then I'm yours."

He nodded once.

The leaf was short. Twelve names in a column, each with a date, and beside each a tick and the word *released* in Chathu's best script. She read them to him. Her voice stayed steady until the ninth.

"Kirri. Twelfth contract closed two years back. Released."

In the margin beside it, in a smaller hand, ran two words. *Kennel, nine.*

She stopped.

She had seen that numeral. In the dark, on the third cage from the end, she had looked at the civet's face and nothing else. But there had been a tag wired to the bars, with a figure painted on it in white. A nine. She had seen it and not read it.

"Every name has a number," she said. "Beside it. The kennel doors are numbered. I saw Kirri's last night. It's nine." She went down the column with her finger. Twelve names, twelve numbers. "Released means a door. All of them. Twelve."

Devan said nothing for a long moment. Then he said, "Twelve is the number that earns it."

"Yes."

"And they let them out into cages."

"They let them out of the contract," she said. "It's very tidy."

His jaw moved. That was all. Under her ribs a cold place she had been keeping shut for weeks gave way, and she let it. Ammu had asked after Kirri for years. She had written four times and been told Kirri was where she wished to be.

She turned the leaf over.

Below the twelve, set apart by a ruled line, stood one more name and a tally. *Thirty. Thirty-six contracts.* No tick. No *released.* Every few lines the tally had stepped up by one, and nothing was ever written beside it.

"That's Vellan's number," she said. "Thirty. He earned it three times over. He's never been let out. He's still useful." She heard how that sounded and did not soften it. "His contracts run on for leaves and leaves, after this. I'm going to turn past them. I will read them if you ask. Not now."

"Not now," Devan said. He was looking at the name, not her.

She turned past them. They made a thick wad of paper, a man's whole working life. She did not read a line, and she felt every leaf go by under her thumb.

After them the stitching changed. A separate quire, sewn tight, headed with the hooked mark Chathu put in a corner for the accounts. *The Keeper's.* She said so aloud. Then she said, "Tell me the year. Tell me the season."

"Six years back," Devan said. "The cold month."

"Then it's here." Her mouth was dry. "Before I read it, I'll tell you how. I'll cover everything below the line I'm on, with my hand, and slide it down as I go. I won't read ahead. If I read ahead I could change it. This way I can't."

"All right," said Devan.

He did not say *I trust you.* He watched her hand.

She laid her left palm flat over the leaf, uncovering the first line. Rain came harder on the tiles. She began.

"The cold month, six years gone."

She slid her hand down.

"Kettamala, the warden's daughter, the west stair."

She heard him go still. It was not a movement. It was the stopping of one.

"My father was warden then," Devan said, to no one.

"Buyer, the Keeper."

Her hand slid.

"Hand, Thirty."

A long breath, from him or from her. She could not tell. She slid her hand one more line.

The numeral stood there in the plain cipher, as ordinary as her own hand. She knew it the way she knew the scars on her wrists. Her mouth had said it for twenty-one years before she was told to stop.

"Lookout," she read. "Seventeen."

The rain went on. She did not move her hand.

There was one line left under it, and she had promised every word. She slid her palm down and read it, in a voice that did not belong to her.

"Closed. Paid in full."

* * *

Nobody spoke.

She had read it. Every word, in order, with her own hand covering the next. There was nothing to take back and nothing to soften. The honesty she had sworn to was a clean floor, and it held, and she stood on it with her hand flat on the leaf.

"Seventeen," Devan said.

"Yes."

"You."

"Yes."

He did not stand. He sat against the wall with his hands loose on his knees, and she could not read his face at all. It had gone as still as it did in the kalari when a drill ended.

"Thirty is Vellan," he said.

"Yes."

"Say what you did."

She said it plainly, because he had asked it plainly. "I was brought in blindfolded. They took it off at the foot of a stair. Carved post, a bird. I had a shuttered lamp. I was to open it twice if anyone came into the court below." She counted the next part in her head and said it anyway. "No one came. I didn't go up. I heard it. I counted to three hundred and ten. Then I was blindfolded again and walked out the way I came in. They never told me the name of the fort. They never told me the name of the girl."

"Asha," he said.

"I know that now."

She stopped. There was nothing else that was true.

*I would have told you,* she wanted to say. It was true, and it was useless, and it sounded like every excuse ever made by a person who had stood at the foot of something. She said it anyway.

"I would have told you. If I had known which fort."

"I know," Devan said. He said it evenly, the way he gave facts. It was not forgiveness, and it was not the opposite. It was only a thing he knew.

She could not look at him. She looked at the leaf instead. The rereading had begun in her whether she wished it or not, the way a wall will show the damp through new plaster.

The parrot newel, smooth as a river stone under a palm that had touched it once, at twenty-one, in the dark. The eleventh step that rang hollow under her boot and made her stop and breathe. Her long way round, eighty-one steps by the east stair, so that her feet would not pass that post. Janu, keeping ten paces from the foot of the stair without knowing she did it. And herself, a bride on her second day, walking wide of the bird without stating a reason. She had thought it a nerve in her. It had been memory, only without a name.

She had held a lamp at the foot of his sister's death. On the first night in his house, she had been afraid of the very place.

And he had asked her once, in a lamplit room, why she took the turn on the eleventh step. *I don't know,* she had said. It was true. She had known only how her body walked.

"The shutters," she said, before she could stop. "Everything you did. You closed them for me, and I thought it was —" She shook her head. The words were no use to either of them.

"Leave it," said Devan quietly.

She left it.

She did the only thing left to her hands, which was to turn the pages. The Keeper's quire ended. Behind it lay one more, the last in the front half, and it was not sewn like the rest. It was stitched in green thread, tight and even, round all four edges. Over the stitching, pressed into a lump of wax the colour of new leaves, a bird sat with its wings spread.

A sunbird. She knew that bird from a wedding gift and a cut seal in a man's sleeve.

*The queen's.* The hook mark in the corner of the first page was the same hook. She did not need to read a word to know what the Keeper's money had been for, or whose hand was above his.

She slid her thumbnail under the edge of the wax.

"Leave it whole," Devan said.

Her thumb stopped.

"It says who sealed it. If we break it, it says nothing, and anyone can say we wrote what we found." He still sat against the wall. His voice was low and exact. "Rema can break it. The high court can. Not us."

"You don't want to know what's in it?"

"I want to know." His hands went flat on his knees and stayed there. "Leave it whole."

She took her thumb from the wax.

He got up. It cost him; she saw the welts pull across his back under the stiff shirt. He crossed to the desk and took the leaves from her one at a time. He tapped their edges true against the wood, the way he squared the gate roll at a change of watch, and set them down on the black cover with both hands. Then he stood a moment with his palms flat on either side.

"I need to walk," he said.

"Devan."

"Not far." He did not look at her. "I'll be inside the wall."

"It's raining."

"Yes."

His cloak hung on the peg by the door, where he had put it that morning. He went past it. The door opened on grey water coming down in sheets, and he walked out into it with his back straight as a gatepost. She watched the dark spread through the back of his shirt, across the claw lines she had given him.

She did not follow. She had learned that much about him. A man who answers questions with facts and does kind things unmentioned will also ask, once, for the thing he needs, and say no more about it.

She counted his boots across the yard. Eleven steps. Twelve. Then the rain took the sound, and the door stood open on the empty court.

Neela stood in Chathu's room with the book, and the green seal, and the tin on the shelf, and laid her palm flat on the leaf again. Paper. It was only paper. It said, in a clerk's tidy cipher, what she was.
