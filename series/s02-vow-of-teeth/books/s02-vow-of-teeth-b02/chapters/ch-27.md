# Chapter 27: Before Dawn

Neela ran with a lamp in one hand and forty names strapped against her ribs.

Devan had pushed the lamp at her in the first twenty strides. "I need both hands," he said, and she understood what the hands were for. She held it by the ring now, unlit. The oilcloth packet shifted under her wrap at every step. She did not touch it. She ran upslope between black trunks with his breath at her shoulder and counted: strides, roots, the slap of the pack on his back.

The wood filled up behind them like a bowl.

They came in ones and twos. A long spotted back slid between two trees on her left, belly low, and was gone. Something heavy broke through the ferns. Eyes showed green at knee height, paired, then paired again. They did not rush. They closed wide and patient, the way hounds do when the slope will do the work for them.

Every one of them knew her. That was the horror of it. The mind sat whole behind each eye, and the hunger belonged to the animal, and the legs belonged to whoever had been smeared and sent.

"Thilak," she said. It came out ragged. "Thilak, it's Neela."

A heavy shape checked at the edge of the dark.

"You brought me tea every evening I held the House. You never asked me for a thing." She ran on and kept talking, because the other choice was to go quiet and be caught. "Madhavi. Kuttu. Meenu, I heard you on the wall. I'm sorry. I'm here."

Nothing answered. Nothing could. The shape stood a breath longer than a hunter should, then turned and went crashing back downhill.

She nearly stopped. Devan's hand caught her elbow and kept her moving.

"Again," he said.

"He can't hear you out here!" She flung it at the trees. "Chathu's on the wall. His word doesn't reach this far. Nobody is caging you tonight."

In the House she had been Seventeen, and they had all been numbers. Now she dragged names up out of the plank in the carpentry shed, lamp-black on pale wood, and threw them into the dark one at a time. "Thulasi. Kuttu. Madhavi."

They broke off by ones. A low shape veered away. Then a pair, side by side, as if they had been waiting for permission. Others came on with their heads down, and she saw it in how they ran. It was not hunger. It was fear of a man on a wall with a word in his mouth. She could not be angry at that.

"Twenty-two." A pale narrow shape paced her on the right, close enough to touch. "Your roll names you. I won't read it in the dark. At noon, in the sun, if you want it. You and me and whoever you choose."

The shape ran beside her for ten strides. Then it dropped back, and the dark where it had been felt wider.

The old tappers' verse came to her unasked, the line Ammu had said was a door. *The name given back at the closing of day.* She had heard it as a thing between two people at a dusk turning. She had never thought it could be done with her mouth, to a wood full of strangers, with no one answering.

Then the root. She never saw it. Her foot went, the ground came up, and the lamp rang off a stone. She rolled, and something took her calf.

Teeth, quick and narrow. It let go as soon as it had tasted. Blood ran warm into her wrap, and the pain came after, hot and bright.

Devan grunted mid-stride and slapped his own left calf. Under his palm a welt was rising, hot and shallow, the skin lifted in a weeping line that did not open.

"That's mine," he said.

"I know." She tried to rise and the leg folded. "Go on. I'll—"

"No."

He had the pack swung to his chest and the buckles snapped before she finished. Then his arms were under her, and he heaved her onto his back as if she were one more bundle. "Hold the lamp. Hold me."

She hooked her arms round his neck, the lamp ring biting her fingers. He ran. She called names over his shoulder into the dark.

A heavy brindled shape came out of the ferns on the left. She knew the gait. She had called its name three times.

"Madhavi," she said. "Madhavi, it's me."

It did not break off. It closed low and fast, and Devan turned his body to take it on the side away from her, and the jaws fastened high on his thigh. He went down on one knee with a sound she felt through her own ribs. Cloth tore. Blood ran down his leg, a real wound, deep and wet. A welt came up hot on her own thigh, and she gasped at it, and the blood was all his.

The teeth did not let go.

Her free hand found the packet under her wrap. Oilcloth, the stiff edges of forty pages, and in them Madhavi's whole night-shape in a witness's hand. One sentence, aloud, in the beast's hearing, and the jaws would stop for good. It sat against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

She took her hand away.

"Madhavi," she said, and her voice shook. "I'm not going to do it. I won't. Not to you. Let go."

A breath. Two. The brindled head jerked, as though something behind the eyes had pulled hard. The jaws opened. The shape fell back with his blood on its muzzle and stood a moment in the ferns, and then it turned and went.

"Up," Devan said through his teeth. He was already getting up.

* * *

She lost the hours after that.

She remembered his shoulders working under her, and the pack knocking the ledger against his ribs, and her cheek wet with his sweat. She remembered calling names until her voice went to a rasp and then calling them in a whisper, because he could run and she could still do that. Twice he stumbled. Once he went to one knee, and a long cold breath of fur passed within a hand of her face. He got up, and it did not follow.

When he finally set her down, his hands were shaking.

"I can walk," she said, and found she could, if she leaned. They went on together, two bleeding people on a hill, his arm round her and hers round him. His thigh still bled through the torn cloth, and the blood ran down his leg and over her welted calf where she pressed against him. The welt on her thigh wept clear.

The dark was thinning. It was not light. It was a greyness between the trunks, as though the night had worn through at the elbow.

She knew the tree before she knew where she was.

It stood alone in a ring of trampled fern, blackbark, old as the House, its trunk split at the base into roots like knuckles. Something pale lay among them. A rag of blue cotton, rained on and dried and rained on again, with a cord pouch beside it, held down by a flat stone.

Ammu's Valli cloth. Muthu had laid it there.

"This is where she—"

"I know," Devan said.

They had both stopped. Neither had chosen it. She thought, with the part of her that still counted: *The wood brought us here.* Then she heard the hitch.

A drag and a step. Slow, unhurried, from behind the trunk.

The bear came round the tree without a sound beyond that. He filled the gap between two roots. His coat was matted with the night, and in the grey his small eyes held nothing at all, neither hate nor pity, only a patient weariness that she knew. It was the look from the wall.

She had time to say, "Devan, go—" and then the forepaw swept her off her feet.

She hit the roots on her back where Ammu had died. The air left her. A weight came down on her breastbone, and claws pricked through the wrap, and the smell of him closed over her like a wet wool blanket. Scorched tamarind. Old blood. A man's sweat under an animal's musk.

He did not bite. She lay under his paw and understood, all at once and very coldly, why he did not.

Devan stood over them.

He had put the pack down. His right hand was inside his shirt, and it came out holding a folded strip of tacky paper. The lamp had rolled against a root. He snatched it up, struck flint twice with fingers that would not hold still, and the wick caught. The little flame put gold on his face and a shaking gold on the page.

He opened it. He held it to the light.

"Off her," he said.

The bear did not move. The paw pressed and the claws went in a hair deeper.

She could see the page from below, upside down: a name at its head in Chathu's neat black hand, a line beneath it, a number. One sentence, aloud, in the bear's hearing, before the dawn. She watched Devan's lips shape the heading. He had not said anything yet, but he was reading. He had begun.

Six years, she thought. Six years of Asha's notes tied in red thread, and a stair he had sworn on. The oldest claim on the hill.

She had stood at the foot of that stair with a shuttered lamp. She had counted to three hundred and ten.

"Devan."

He didn't look at her.

"You haven't struck first in eight years." She said it between breaths, because the paw left her little else. "You swore it to me twice, in our bed at Kettamala, that if you began to read I'd take it, and you'd let go. It's not a throat. It's a year. A year of his mind going, in a cage behind the cook-house, and you'd be the one who put him there."

"He killed her." His voice was very low and perfectly even, the voice he used for a charge. "He's standing on you. He could finish it now."

"He hasn't. Because you've got the page lit. That's the only reason I'm breathing, and I'm still asking."

"Neela—"

"You said if you begin to read, I take it." Her hand was free. She had known it was free since she fell. "I said I'd hold on till you let go."

The flame leaned in a draught. The bear's breath rolled over them both. Somewhere out in the grey, something moved and was still.

She reached up. It was a long way. Every claw lifted and settled with the stretch of her arm, and she did not look at them. She closed two fingers on the edge of the page, and then her whole hand.

His grip was iron for a moment, a man holding a sentence the way a drowning man holds a rope.

Then it opened.

He did not speak. He stood with his empty hand in the lamplight and let her have it. Through the tacky paper she felt a fine tremor that was not hers.

"Thank you," she said, and found her voice had gone.

"Don't." He knelt. His face looked scraped to the bone. "Tell me what to do with my hands."

"The book. Open the book. Hold the light."

* * *

He dragged the pack to him and unbuckled it one-handed, the lamp in the other. The ledger came out in its greased cloth. He laid it open on the roots beside her head, front half up, the loose contract leaves lifting in the draught. She turned the leaves with the hand that wasn't holding Vellan's page, and the bear's weight rode her breath.

She knew where it was. She had turned past it once, in the Resin House, unread.

*Thirty.* Chathu's cipher, small and tidy, plain ink on a plain leaf. No resin in it, no witness's hand. It couldn't cage a mouse. It was only what the House had written about a man.

She read it aloud in the plain tongue, slowly, so that every word would arrive.

"Thirty. Release not to be granted. Sell to the crown when spent."

Nothing happened.

She said it again, because it was a short line and she wanted the bear to have it twice. "Thirty. Release not to be granted. Sell to the crown when spent. That's in his hand, Vellan. It's been in his hand since your third contract. He wrote it in ordinary ink, so he'd never have to say it."

The paw stopped pressing. It did not lift, not at once. It rested on her, a weight with the intent drained out.

*Three times over,* she thought. *He earned it three times over.* A man who believed the blade was never guilty, only the hand that threw it, and who had waited years to be set down. The hand had written his price in advance.

The small eyes closed. When they opened they were looking at the page in her fist.

The paw lifted away.

The bear backed one step, then another. He lowered himself among the roots as if his legs had been cut. He put his chin on his forearms, six feet from her face, and did not move again.

She lay where she was. She did not trust herself to get up.

"Is he—" Devan began.

"Waiting."

"For what?"

"Light."

* * *

The grey went to pearl. A bird started up somewhere, and then another. The trunks came out of the dark one at a time like men stepping from a crowd.

Round the edge of the clearing she saw them. Low shapes at the fern line, a dozen, then more, standing in the paling dark and not coming nearer. The thin-flanked ones, the antlered, the spotted. A pale narrow one who held her eyes a moment too long. They had run all night on a hill with their minds whole, and now they stood and watched what a man and a woman and a bear were doing under a tree.

Then Vellan began to change.

She had seen it a hundred times from the other side. She had never watched from outside, with a lamp on it. The coat drew in like a tide going out. Bone slid under skin with a sound like green wood. The great shoulders narrowed, the muzzle drew back, and a big bearded man lay shuddering among the roots, bare to the waist, the old resin scars pale at his throat and wrists. He coughed. He got his hands under him.

Devan had not looked away. He had not been able to. The lamp was in his hand, and his eyes were on it all, the whole slow wrench of it, and she saw the instant he understood what he was seeing. His shoulders went still.

*Witness,* she thought. *Oh, Devan.*

Vellan raised his head. He was grey under the beard. He looked at Devan's face, then at the lamp, then at the page in her fist. His voice, when it came, was the low grumble of the yard.

"Well," he said. "You saw."

"Yes," Devan said.

"Then you've got it. Any dark you like."

"I know what I have."

Vellan's mouth moved. It might have been a joke. He didn't finish it, and he didn't ask for anything. He sat back on his heels with one hand flat on the root where a blue rag lay, and for the first time since she had known him he looked at no one.

All round them the light was coming. Along the fern line the shapes began to stir, then to go, one by one and then in a rush, loping downhill toward the House and the sheds behind the wall, where the dawn would take them out of sight of every eye. She counted them out. She could not help it. Thirty-one, thirty-two. Behind the trunks, far off, the first of the cries began, the thin ragged turning-back that always came with the sun.

None of them was caged. She had not said a word of any page. She said it again to herself, slowly, to be sure it was true.

Devan sank down beside her. He still held the lamp, and the flame had gone pale in the daylight. He set it on the root, and it ran with him, smoking, a small stubborn light. Blood had dried black down his leg.

"I stood there," he said. "With it open."

"I know."

"Another breath—"

"You didn't."

"You took it." His eyes closed. "I let go."

"You did."

She lay with her head on the root and looked up through the black branches at a sky going pink. Forty pages sat flat and warm against her ribs. One more, tacky and creased, lay curled in her fist. In the clearing behind her a man who had killed Asha knelt in the dirt and did not run.

The night was over. Nothing in the wood had been caged.

She had until the House woke to decide what she was going to do with the forty pages and with Vellan.
