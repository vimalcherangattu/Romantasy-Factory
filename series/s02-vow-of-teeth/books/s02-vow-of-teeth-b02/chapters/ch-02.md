# Chapter 2: The Justice at the Gate

Bhaskaran's horn cut across the kalari in the middle of the third form, and Devan stopped with one foot lifted.

One long note. Two short. Strangers at the padippura, unarmed, not many. He had taught the signal himself, and in five years it had never been blown on a day like this one.

He stood in the red earth with sesame smoke in his nose and rain running off his shoulders. The pass was a river. The road below the fort had been empty all night; he had watched it until the dark took it, and nothing had moved on it. So they had not used the road.

He took the north steps two at a time.

He had left Neela asleep. When the langurs went silent at dusk she had spent the first smear anyway, as she had said she would, and run the road as the other one to see who walked it. She had come back to the ledge at grey light with nothing but wet fur: no scent on the road, no track, nobody. She had lain down at last like a woman who'd been told she could sleep. He did not like leaving her. He liked less the thought of waking her to this before he knew what it was.

At the gate the rain hung off the roof edge in a bead curtain. Bhaskaran stood under it with the horn still in his fist. Unni stood behind him, staring at the five figures in the arch as if they might be a joke someone had told badly.

Four of them stood in a line. The fifth stood in front.

She was a lean woman of fifty-eight, soaked from hem to hip, with a grey plait dripping down her cloak. Her boots were caked to the shin in red scree. She looked like someone who had spent the night climbing something she disliked.

"Warden of Kettamala," she said. Her voice was dry as a split pot. "I am Rema, justice of the high court at Kodiyur. I regret the hour."

"You walked."

"We climbed. Your goat path is not fit for goats." She pushed back her hood. "I bring the queen's warrant. May I bring it out of the wet?"

He should have said yes at once. He looked at her escort instead.

Four, in oiled cloaks, each with a green sunbird sash worn across the chest. Their packs were oilcloth, tied tight, and dry. A dull sheen showed on them, not a bead of water standing. Their boots were stiff and square at the heel. Mud reached the ankle and no higher, and hardly a scuff marked any sole.

He had walked that goat path first at nineteen, on a dare. It had taken the skin off his knees.

"Bring it in," he said.

* * *

The warrant came out of a sleeve of oilcloth, folded in three. Rema handed it across without ceremony and watched him read.

*Councillor Ittan, Keeper of the Passes, charged with treason under the wartime charter. To be delivered into the keeping of the crown at Kodiyur.* Below it, in a clerk's round hand, ran a list of Ittan's offices. And below that, the wax.

Green. Crushed leaf, not clove. A sunbird with wings spread.

He did not move his thumb. He let his eye run down the left wing to the tip, and there it was: a chip no bigger than a grain of rice, the same notch as the seal on the writ that lay in a hollow behind the third slat of his chamber.

Of course it was the same. It was one seal. He told himself so twice, and kept his face where it was.

"The charter was invoked when the spring raids began," Rema said. "You were sent notice."

"I read it."

"Then you know the warrant outranks the warden's hold. I am to carry him to Kodiyur."

"On what road?"

For the first time she smiled. It was a small, tired thing. "That is the difficulty, yes."

"I have a request," he said. He made it flat, because flat was the only voice he trusted. "I mean to put Ittan before the court. Not as an accused man. As a witness to my sister's murder, six years ago, on the west stair of this fort. He sold the passes. I think the same hand bought her death."

Bhaskaran, behind him, went very still.

"You think," said Rema.

"I can show you the tallies. I can show you his chest of waxed blanks, his book, the sums. I cannot yet show you that the hand is one hand. That's what a hearing is for."

She studied him for the length of a slow breath. He had faced raiders with less sense of being weighed.

"The charter lets a warden hold a prisoner until assize when the roads are shut," she said at last. "It lets a justice sit assize where she stands. I stand here." She folded the oilcloth over her knuckles. "Three days, Warden. You will show me your proof. Ittan will answer. At the end of it I decide whether I carry a traitor out, or a witness, or no one."

"Three days."

"You have kept him well?"

"He has a cot and a lamp. He has asked for one thing."

"What?"

"That his wife's willow bark be boiled slowly."

Something moved in her face and was gone. "I shall want my First Guard at his door. A courtesy to the crown."

"Bhaskaran's men hold that door."

"Then both," Rema said pleasantly, "and you will not mind the company."

He minded it very much. He had a whole fort, sixty soldiers and the only wall for a hundred miles, and he understood now that all of it had been quietly put to one side. A woman with a plait and a sleeve of paper had walked into his gate, and the gate had opened because it was meant to.

"Both," he said.

He turned to the four. "Your names, for the garrison roll?"

"Crown escort does not give them, Warden." Rema was already shaking rain from her cloak. "They go by place. First, Second, and so on. It saves the clerks a great deal of ink."

"First," said the nearest. A woman, close to his own age, built like a bowstring. Her voice had no colour in it.

"Second."

"Third."

"Fourth."

Numbers. He had heard a woman say *Seventeen* in the dark a month ago, and it had settled in his ribs like a stone. This one fit the same hole too neatly.

First Guard shrugged her pack higher on her shoulder. Her cuff rode up. On the inside of her left wrist lay a single pale ridge, the length of a finger, faintly raised and shiny in the grey light. One clean line, flat to the skin. Nothing like the thick ridges that lay layer over layer on his wife's wrists, ten years of burning laid down on each other. Her collar was buttoned to the chin and did not gape, even when she stooped under the pack.

Lamp-burn, he thought. Or a rope. A night guard carries a lamp too long, wraps the cloth wrong. Half his own garrison wore marks like it, on hands and forearms. She tugged the sleeve down, and he looked away, and the thought went into the place where he kept things that were not yet facts.

* * *

Janu fed them in the hall, because Janu would have fed the flood itself. She scolded the mud and the hour and the queen's justice, in that order, and set a bowl of rice and fish in front of Rema twice.

Rema ate like a person who had been hungry on principle. The escort did not eat in the hall. They stood by the pillars, two and two, and Janu's girls carried their bowls to them, and the bowls came back full.

"Your goat path has a drop at the boulder," Rema said between mouthfuls. "A fall I would not want my enemies to take. My escort strung ropes at the three worst places. In the dark, mind. I would have turned back at the first."

"Good soldiers."

"The best I have ever travelled with." She wiped her fingers. "I do not ask how they do it."

She said it lightly, as praise.

He said, "I'll have the south range made ready for them."

"Kind."

He said no more. When she looked toward Janu's steaming pot, he glanced across at the First Guard against her pillar. She was looking at nothing, at the middle of the air, her hands loose at her sides. Water had pooled under every other cloak in the hall. Her hem had dried to the knee.

He picked up his bowl and did not taste it.

* * *

Neela was sitting on the edge of the bed with her hair half braided when he came in. She had heard the horn. He could see she had counted the doors.

"Before you ask," he said.

Her fingers stopped.

"A justice of the high court. Rema. Fifty-eight, tired, no fool. She brings a warrant for Ittan under the wartime charter, sealed in the queen's green wax. I asked for an assize, with Ittan as witness to Asha. She gave me three days. She sits here. Her escort stand at Ittan's door beside Bhaskaran's men. I agreed to that."

"You agreed."

"I had no way not to." He sat on his heels so she would not have to look up. "Four escort. They gave numbers, not names. They came by the goat path in a night of rain, and their packs are dry and their boots are barely worn. Rema says they fixed ropes at the bad places in the dark."

"Dry," said Neela.

"That is what I thought."

"What else?"

"The seal on the warrant has a chip at the left wing tip. It's the same as the writ." He moved his hand once, flat. "And the First Guard, a woman, has a mark on the inside of her left wrist. One pale line, faintly raised, about so long. Not like yours. I took it for a lamp-burn."

Neela said, "Which wrist?"

"Left. Inside."

"Mm," said Neela. She looked at her own right wrist, with its binding, and then at the shutters. Her next breath came a moment late. He saw it, and kept it.

"There's more," he said, because he had told himself in the passage that he would not stop at the easy end. "When I came up the stair I stopped at the corridor and told Raiu's relief, the new one, that you were not to be woken, and that if the justice's people asked, you were unwell. I was going to say the same to Janu. I was going to put you in the south range behind a bar. I thought, if she doesn't see you, she can't ask."

He heard how it sounded. The silence after it was not angry. It was attentive.

"But you came up here instead," she said.

"I came up to say it to you first. You have been shut behind my fences for a month, with good reason. I do not think it is a good reason now. I do not want you behind one that you did not choose."

She put her hands flat on her knees. "Do you know what it looks like," she said, "when a warden's wife is ill the morning a crown justice arrives? After the lord of the house has spent a month doing nothing but bar his shutters?"

"Like guilt."

"Like the only story that needs no help from anyone." She shook out the rest of her hair. "Hide me three days and she finds out anyway. Then she asks every servant who carried my tray. If I stand in the hall and Janu complains about my cooking, I am only a wife. A wife with a name on a crown writ, who ought to be proud of it."

"She will ask questions."

"She will." Neela met his eyes. "I'll answer them. I'll say only true things. It is more than most people do, and Chathu spent twenty years teaching me which ones to choose."

He had wanted to say *no*. He had been holding it since the gate, in the part of him that had learned at twenty-six what happens to the people behind a locked door. He turned it over and found, to his surprise, that she was right, and that the wanting was only fear.

"True things," he said.

"And you tell me everything she says. Everything. Before I ask."

"I've done that once this morning."

"You did well." Her mouth tilted. "Do it again."

"I will."

She stood. She caught his wrist as she passed, quick, the way she did at the kalari when she was about to correct his guard, and the bracelets of scar on her skin pressed hot against the pulse in his. Then she let go. "I'll go down by the west stair," she said.

He looked at her.

"I've used the east stair every day since the vow. A watcher counts habits. Today I break one."

He had seen her keep wide of the west stair since the first week. He had never asked why, and he did not ask now. He only nodded, and she was out of the door before he could say anything else, with the third slat of the shutter complaining behind her in the draught.

* * *

Rema had moved to the warden's chair. Devan saw it as he came down the east steps: not a challenge, a convenience, the chair nearest the brazier. Janu had put a cushion on it. He found that he had no objection to the cushion.

His objection was to the man beside the chair. Muthu. Cheerful, loud, rain still on his collar, telling the justice that the fort had never seen so honoured a guest in his time and the south range had the best roof in the hill country. He laughed at his own words. He stopped laughing when Rema looked at him, and for the length of a breath his face was very quiet.

Devan marked it, and let it lie.

"A sentence of business, Warden, and then I shall sleep like your garrison," Rema said, as he came up. "The closing writ you sent down to Kodiyur with the last rider. It bore a household witness. A woman's name, written in a clerk's hand and a fresh hand beside it. *Neela, of Kettamala*."

"My wife."

"I know whose wife." She folded her hands. "I should like to meet her. By name. In my experience a writ is only as honest as the person who signed it, and I would like to look at her while I read."

He had meant to say something. Some courteous, unhelpful, dilatory thing.

"She is here," said Neela from the foot of the west stair.

She stood with one hand on the newel, on the parrot's smooth broken beak, and a face as unremarkable as a wall.
