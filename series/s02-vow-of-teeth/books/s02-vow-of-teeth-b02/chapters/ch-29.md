# Chapter 29: The Assize

Rema sat in his father's chair with the tally chest open at her feet, and Devan stood where the accused would have stood and thought: *eight pillars, and not one of them will hold me up.*

His thigh had stiffened on the ride. Every step across the hall flagstones tugged at a welt that wasn't his, a hot line on his left calf, and it did not matter. He had come in with a canvas pack and the court's whole attention, and he meant to spend it well.

The hall was full. Janu stood on the hearth step with her hands wrapped in her apron. Muthu had the door, and Kandan of Mathoor stood three men down from him with the scar through his brow drawn tight. Bhaskaran had put the garrison along the wall in their grey, and two of his men held the dais step. Rema's escort was long gone down the goat path. She sat alone with her pen.

Neela sat on the household bench, bound hand in her lap. She had washed. She had put on the day kasavu with the torn border, mended. Her eyes stayed off him, and he understood why. Last night he had told her what he would and would not do.

"The leaf names a lookout," he had said, in the dark, with the pebble in his cheek and no reason for it. "I'll put it before Rema. I'll say whose number it is. You needn't stand. I'll say it, and the cost is mine."

She had said nothing. Her breathing had been very even, and he'd known that breath.

Rema laid a board across her knees. Fifty-eight years old, grey at the temples, plain brown wrap. She wrote like a clerk who loved her ink.

"This is an assize of the queen's border, sitting at Kettamala for the high court's record," she said. "I write what is said and what is shown. I decide nothing today but what I will carry south. Warden. Begin."

He opened the pack.

Asha's token came first. The split-ash stick lay on the table in its oilcloth, the red thread still twisted round it, council wax gone dull. Beside it he set the beeswax impression he'd lifted from Bhaskaran's tally. Dot and split, at the sprig's stem. Then the scouts' satchel tally, with its clove-sweet wax. Then thirty-six blanks from Ittan's chest, in a row, each wrapped, each sealed in the same mark.

"Asha, my sister, found the first of these six years ago," he said. "She wrote on a note beside it that she needed a second to be sure. She was dead within the month. Last spring a second came off a raider's body in the lower narrows. It matches. Today I give you thirty-six more, found in a false-bottomed chest on the Keeper's own cart, with no clerk's number on any."

"And what would they have done?" Rema asked, writing.

"Opened the pass. Not to a gate clerk. A gate would never stamp them. To a widow holding the seal in trust, with the Keeper at her elbow to advise." He kept his voice flat. "My marriage writ names her. The same clause. The Keeper proposed the match."

He set Ittan's tally book beside the blanks. Forty leaves, dated sums, pass names. He did not open it for her. Rema had spent two days in it already, alone in the south range, and her pen told him she knew where it would read.

"And the guild contract," she said.

He took the leaf from its fold. It was cut from the Keeper's quire, hooked in the margin, written in Chathu's tidy hand. It was not the other quire, the one stitched in green thread that he had carried in under his flap. That one was a different matter, and he left it buckled. He laid the leaf on the table face down.

"I'll have the man who wrote it speak first," he said. "By daylight, as the charter requires."

* * *

They brought Chathu in from the south range with his hands tied before him and the gag cloth hanging at his neck. It was an hour past noon. Devan had counted every tick of the sun to be sure.

The old man walked with his uneven foot on the flagstones and stopped at the foot of the dais. He looked at Rema's board, and at the false-bottomed chest, and last at the hall. His gaze went along the grey line of the garrison without hurry, taking inventory.

"Master of the Resin House," Rema said. "You are not charged here. You are heard."

"I thank the court." The fatherly voice. Soft, unhurried, a man at a ledger by lamplight. "I will speak in what I know, which is accounts. A man who keeps accounts keeps them true. It is the only mercy in my trade."

"Then give me the account," said Rema.

"The cold month, six years gone, by the Keeper's hand." He did not say *Ittan*. He said the office, as if the man had already ceased to be a person. "Four slashes paid, one hook delivered. The buyer was the Keeper of the Passes. The subject was the warden's sister of Kettamala. The service was a bear's."

The hall made a sound. It was not a gasp. It was lower, the noise of sixty men shifting their weight.

"She was found by the stair," Chathu went on, "as the contract required. Closed. Paid in full. I have never in forty years carried a balance past the day it fell due, and the Keeper was as exact."

"Why?" That was Janu, from the hearth step. Her voice cracked in the middle. "Why her?"

Rema lifted a hand. Chathu turned his head toward the old woman and answered her anyway.

"Because the girl had found a tally, mistress. A little thing. The Keeper's trade was in little things." He folded his bound hands. "She cost the House nothing to feed. It was a short account. I wept, when it was closed. I do, always."

Devan's nails were in his palm. At the end of the household bench a hand tightened on the wood rail. He felt it as a drag at his own right wrist, and he did not look.

"Enter it," Rema said, to no one, and wrote.

* * *

"The leaf," she said.

He turned it over. He had turned it over in his head a hundred times on the road, and the wording had not changed. He drew breath to read, and a bench scraped.

Neela had stood.

She still hadn't looked at him. She stood with her bandaged hand against her skirt and the other lifted, the way a child lifts a hand in a pit when she wants the instructor's eye. The hall stilled. He had seen her walk into a Resin House and take it. He had never seen her stand so carefully, like a person carrying a full cup.

"Madam," she said. "I'll read the lookout."

"Neela." He said it very low. "I told you—"

"You did." She had not turned. "You said you'd carry it, and the cost would be yours. I know." Her eyes flicked to him then, once, very bright. "It was never only yours."

Rema watched her over the board. "You are the warden's wife and the household witness to the closing writ. You're not sworn today to speak to this."

"Then swear me after." Neela crossed the flagstones. Her calf dragged. Muthu's hand twitched on the door and stayed where it was. She stopped at the table beside Devan, close enough that he could smell the tamarind of the Resin House salve, and took the leaf from the cloth. She did not read yet. She held it a moment against her palm.

"Asha, the warden's sister, was killed on the west stair of this fort six years ago," she said. "I know where the stair is. I walked round its foot for weeks before I let myself say why. I stood at that foot once. I stood at it with a shuttered lamp and a blindfold just off my eyes, and counted to three hundred and ten, and nobody came."

The hall did not breathe. Kandan's scarred brow had gone white.

She read then, in the plain flat voice she'd used for forty pages in the sun. "The cold month, six years gone. Kettamala, the warden's daughter, the west stair. Buyer, the Keeper. Hand, Thirty. Lookout, Seventeen. Closed. Paid in full."

She lowered the leaf.

"Seventeen is a number. It was mine for twenty-one years." She turned and looked straight at Rema. "My name is Neela. I'm the lookout. I was told to open a lamp twice if anyone came into the court. I wasn't told what the lamp was for. I didn't ask. I do not offer that as a reason."

Devan stood very still.

*My father was warden then*, he had said at the Resin House, with the stolen ledger open between them. The leaf said *daughter*, as if the girl on the stair were only her father's. It gave the lookout a number, and nobody who wrote that line had thought she might carry a name of her own. He had told himself for two nights how this would sound. He had not been ready to hear it in her voice, level and without excuse, a woman handing over the one thing she could have kept.

Beneath it lay the quiet fact that Asha would have done the same. *Say it anyway*, her last note had said. *The truth protects whoever finds it.* He had carried that line since the night he found it under the camphor block, not knowing it would be spoken in this hall by this mouth.

"I asked you not to," he said, so low only she heard.

"I know." She didn't turn her head. "You asked me nothing. You told me what you'd do. I decided the rest."

He had won. He knew it the way you know a gate has fallen. The proof would hold, and Rema would carry it south. And the price was this woman standing in the open with her own name on a record, and he had not been the one to pay it. He did not know what to do with the gladness or the grief of that. When she held out the leaf, he took it, and his fingers were steady. Then he laid his hand flat over hers on the table's edge, where the whole hall could see, and she let it stay.

Muthu, at the door, had closed his eyes.

* * *

Rema wrote for some time.

The garrison did not move. Somewhere in the courtyard the cistern chain clanked the hour, and every head in the hall took it in. Janu had pressed her apron to her mouth. She had kept to the hearth step all morning, as if the flagstones beyond it were the stair. Now Devan saw her step down and cross the floor toward the household bench, her face wet. She passed Neela without a word. She sat down beside her empty place and put her hands in her lap.

At last Rema laid down her pen.

"I will record the finding for the high court at Kodiyur," she said. Her voice had a clerk's dryness, and under it something he could not have named. "That the killing of Asha, second of Kettamala, on the west stair six years since, and the sale of forged pass-tokens to Thurai raiders, were one conspiracy. That the Keeper of the Passes bought both. That the Resin House executed the first at his purchase. That he is dead by the queen's warrant and cannot answer, and that I record his death with my objection."

She dipped her pen and wrote a moment longer.

"Entered in the record by name," she said. "Asha, daughter of the late warden of Kettamala, officer of the border, aged twenty-two. Killed under contract. Her name is written."

Devan had thought, for six years, about that sentence in different shapes. In none of them had it come so quietly.

He nodded once. It was all he had. Along the wall a few of the garrison dipped their heads, and one man at the back let out a sound that he tried to cover with a cough.

"Warden," Rema said. "Is there more?"

"There is." He had known the question was coming. "I hold one document more. I will not lay it before this assize. It's for the high court in full session, and no bench below it."

The hall rustled. Rema did not move.

"What does it concern?"

"Who paid for the contract on my own death," he said. "The rest I will say where it can be answered."

For a long time she looked at him. Her eyes went to the sealed case at her feet, the second one, her own, tied in green thread, which she had not opened all morning. Then, quickly, to Neela. The look was nothing. A glance at a name on a board.

"Noted," Rema said. "Entered as held, and not shown." She wrote it. "I will not press it, Warden. That is for those with the right to press."

It struck him that she'd said it like a woman taking a very small step back from a very large drop.

* * *

"One matter remains," Rema said, "and I will ask it now, in the hall, with the garrison to hear and my pen to take it down."

Devan felt the room sharpen. He had half known. He had not wanted to.

"Neela, wife of the warden. You have named yourself the lookout. That is a guild number, from a guild's book, and a guild's book names no one who is not of its kind." She laid the board flat. "You will be sworn as household witness to the closing writ. In that oath I ask you now, once, plainly, and I will have no answer but the true one. Are you an odiyan?"

Muthu's hand tightened on the door. Kandan did not blink. Along the grey wall sixty men waited, and Devan saw faces he had trained, men who had learned her name from her own mouth, who would sit through this oath and then eat or not eat with her at noon.

He could stop it. He could say *I won't answer that* for her, as he had for himself a hundred times. He could say he held a writ and the hall had no right. Rema might even allow it.

He looked at Neela instead.

She had gone quite white, and her hands were steady. She did not search for him. That was how he knew she had made up her mind before she stood. She stood in the clean light from the west shutters and put her bound hand over her heart, the way a witness does, and when she raised her eyes they were not afraid. They were counting. Doors, steps, breaths. And then they were not counting at all.

"Yes," Neela said.
