# Chapter 9: The Seventh Day

Devan said yes in the grey before dawn, standing at the lip of the pit with the red earth cold under his bare feet, and Muthu's face did something he had never seen it do in twenty-three years.

It came apart. Then it put itself back together, badly, and Muthu went down the north steps without a word.

The sesame lamps burned in their niches. Above them on the east wall the paired sentries stood with their faces to the hill, as they always did, and did not turn. Muthu stripped to his mundu and rolled his shoulders. He did not make a joke about it. That was how Devan knew the round was not for sport.

"No marma," Devan said.

"No marma." Muthu came in low.

Devan let him. Bar one blow in the Resin House yard and two touches with his wife, he had struck at air for eight years, and the habit of it held him: take the first blow, strike no one first. Muthu's forearm cracked into his ribs, over the old white seam from the ford, and the breath went out of him in a grunt. Somewhere in the hall above, a woman he had left sleeping would be feeling the same blow bloom along her side. He hoped she'd stay asleep.

Then his hands remembered. He turned the next strike off his wrist, stepped inside, and put the heel of his palm into Muthu's shoulder hard enough to move him two paces.

"There he is," Muthu said, and his voice shook. "There's the lump."

"Talk or fight."

"Both. I'm a clever man."

They circled. Muthu feinted at the knee and Devan read it from the hip, the way he had read it at fourteen. His body was a stranger to him and also exactly his own.

"The winter Asha died," Muthu said, and came in. Devan blocked. "A man came up from Kodiyur. Household man. Polite. He had a grey cloak." Another strike, flat to the ribs, and Devan folded round it. "Said the queen grieved. Said she wanted you kept safe. Said the safest way was for someone who loved you to tell the household how you did."

"Keep going."

"I'm going." Muthu swept low, and Devan jumped it and came down, and the earth shuddered. "*To keep the warden safe.* That was the whole of the asking. A letter a month. What you ate. Whether you slept. Who came and went at the gate."

"Whether I sparred."

Muthu's guard dropped for half a breath. "Yes."

Devan hit him. A clean knuckle strike under the arm, no weight behind it, and Muthu staggered and came back wheezing.

"Six years," Devan said.

"Six years. My mother wasn't in it. She's alive, she writes to me on the ninth-day post, that part was a lie, and I'm sorry for it more than the rest." He lunged, got a hand on Devan's wrist and twisted. They went down together in the red dust, Devan on top, his forearm across the captain's collarbone. Muthu looked up at him with his teeth showing, and tears ran sideways into his ears.

"I thought it was the same thing," he said. "The crown and you. I thought if I told them everything, nobody could ever hurt you without it being seen."

Devan did not move his arm. The tell was not there. Muthu's voice had been loud, ragged, whole. Devan had stood in this pit and watched this man lie, and the silence always came first. It was not here. It meant less than it should. A man who has told one lie for six years has had time to learn the shape of the truth.

"The grey packet," Devan said. "The one you took into Ittan's cell."

Muthu closed his eyes. "A rider brought the slip off post day, a week and more before the sealing. Lathered, from the household. He gave it to me with an order that's stood ever since: if the Keeper is ever held, put it in his hand and nobody else's." He swallowed. "I never thought I'd use it. Then you held him. I read it first, because I'd never carried one I couldn't read. It was a single line. *Say nothing, and your wife is kept.*"

Devan let his weight off the arm.

"I thought it was a mercy." Muthu's chest heaved. "I thought it was a pardon on terms. His wife's an invalid, Devan. They'd keep her fed and housed so long as he kept quiet. I thought, that's a kindness. I carried it in like a kindness. I didn't know the queen would send a justice with a block. I didn't know." He opened his eyes. "I'd have thrown the packet in the cistern."

"And his wife?"

"I don't know." His mouth worked. "I don't know. Ittan read the slip and gave it back to me. I'd no one to ask what to do with it, so I put it in with my own letter and sealed the lot again in the guardroom, with the grey stick I carry."

Devan sat back on his heels. The pit smelled of sesame oil and earth, and dawn was coming grey over the wall. The cistern chain began to clank the hour, and the sentries above did not turn round.

"Get up," he said.

* * *

The hall was empty, and Janu's girls had not yet come with the brooms. Devan lifted the cord from his neck, and the strongbox key came with it. The packet lay on top of the rest of the post, grey wax whole, thick as a man's thumb.

He set it on the long table, between the cups left from last night. Muthu stood on his right, close enough that Devan could hear him breathe. He did not tell the man to step back.

"I had this out of your hand openly," Devan said. "In front of the yard, so you'd think it was routine. I haven't broken it. It's lain in that box since the rains began." He looked at the seal. "Today I read you, with you standing here. That's the only way I know how to do it that isn't a theft."

"Read," Muthu said.

Devan broke the wax with his thumb. Two leaves fell out, and a slip no bigger than his palm.

He took the slip first. It was in a clerk's round hand, black ink on cheap paper, with no name and no seal: *Say nothing, and your wife is kept.* He read it twice, and put it down very carefully.

The other leaves were Muthu's, square and large, in the hand of a man who writes as he talks. Devan read them aloud, because he had decided he would.

It was all there. The rain. The garrison's rice. The warden's appetite, which was *better, my lady, since the wedding.* A line about sealing day: the Keeper's five carts searched under the padippura, the Keeper held in the guardroom, the warden's wife signing the closing writ as household witness. Then the morning of the vow, in Muthu's own words: *The warden's wife spoke before the household at noon. She said she came to this house sold. She named Raiu, and the odd girl they call Ammu who died under the blackbark, and every servant down to the goat-boy. She told me, to my face, to spell her name right.*

Devan stopped. Muthu was looking at the floor.

*I do not know what she is,* the letter went on. *But I saw her bind her hand on the warden's wrist, and he did not flinch. I think she loves him. I think he is happier than I have ever seen him. I ask the household to remember that.*

There was nothing else. No mention of the Resin House, the kennels, the book, or what she was at night. No one called Chathu appeared at all. Every line was what Muthu had told him in the pit, and nothing more.

Devan laid the leaves down on top of the slip.

"I didn't know what she was," Muthu said. "I still don't. I never wrote to any guild. I don't know what a guild would want with me, or with this house. I told nobody her cart order, either. The one she gave me the night she rode out. She gave it to me alone, in the passage, and I carried it out in my own head and I never said it to a living soul." His voice cracked. "Say you believe me."

"I won't answer whether I believe that."

It landed. Devan watched it land. Muthu took it the way a man takes a hit he has been waiting for, standing, with his chin up.

"Fair," Muthu said.

"Give me the post-key."

Muthu had it on a cord at his belt, the small brass key to the gate's post chest, the one thing in the fort that was entirely his. He worked the cord over his head and put it in Devan's palm. His hand was shaking. "Do you want me gone?"

"No."

"Devan—"

"You're my captain." He closed his fingers on the key. "You'll draw the watch at the sixth clank and eat in the hall, and Gopan will see you at the chain as he does every day. Nothing changes that anyone can see. The post goes through my hand." He met the man's eyes. "A captain I know is the queen's is worth more to me than a captain I don't."

Muthu looked at him for a long moment. His mouth opened. What he said, in the end, was "Salt and ash, you're cold."

"Yes," Devan said. "Go and eat."

He listened to the footsteps go. They were uneven, and then they weren't.

* * *

The tally board went up again at dusk. Devan had sent the others away, so it was only the two of them in the tack room, with the rain ticking into the buckets and the lamp low. Neela sat on the pail with her bandaged hand turned palm up. Across her ribs, under the cloth, a bruise had come up the colour of a plum. He had counted it himself in the lamplight and said nothing. She had touched his side, in the same place, and said nothing back.

"Muthu," he said.

"I heard in the yard. Everyone did."

"What did they hear?"

"That you sparred." She almost smiled. "That you hit him once and he laughed like a drain. Unni says the whole east wall turned round to look."

"They didn't turn."

"They wanted to."

He sat down on the trestle's edge. The charcoal roof lay on the board in front of him, a long loaf with two doors. He had not meant to ask. He had meant to decide it himself, as he had decided everything for eight years, alone in the dark with a lamp. But he found he was already speaking.

"I've kept a man in office who has sent six years of letters about me to the queen. I've told him I won't say whether I believe him. I don't know how to hold a man like that." He looked at his scarred knuckles. "I've never asked anyone how to hold a man."

She did not move. He felt her go very still, the way she did at the edge of a thing she had been waiting for.

"You're asking me about command," she said.

"I'm asking you."

"I've commanded no one. I've been a number."

"You know the guild from inside. You know how a house leashes a man." He kept his voice flat. "I know it from a class and a letter. Tell me what you'd do."

She thought. He had learned that her thinking had a sound, her thumbnail going along the nail, a slow dry rasp. It stopped.

"Chathu never struck a girl who cried," she said. "He left her sweets and let her be fed and gentle until she'd learned to be grateful. It worked for years. It worked because he was always the one who decided what she was worth." She put the bandaged hand on her knee. "So don't hand Muthu a kindness. And don't hand him a cage. Either one, he'd know how to wear."

"Then what?"

"Give him a job he can't fake."

He turned that over. "Say more."

"A thing that's done in front of everyone, where the work is the whole proof. A man can lie in a letter. He can't lie in a gate that's holding or isn't." She looked at the board. "He chose a side without noticing he was choosing, six years ago. Let him choose out loud. Where it costs him, and where you can see him do it."

The rain ticked. Devan took up the charcoal, and for the first time in the whole planning he drew something with confidence: a square with a hill on it, and a gate.

"The raid goes in on the dark of the moon," he said. "I take the ridge party and Bhaskaran's best. Muthu holds Kettamala."

She said nothing for a count of three.

"With Rema inside the walls," he went on. "The queen's justice, sitting in my hall with her tally chest and the gate log. She won't be watching his loyalty. She'll be reading who goes through that gate, and when, in his own hand."

"Rema is the crown's," Neela said. "The crown sealed your death. Why would her log protect you?"

"Because she wrote against the crown." He did not look up from the board. "At the block, in her own hand, she entered her objection to Ittan's execution. Clerk's copy and all. A woman who'll put that on a roll will put a bad gate on one." He drew a bar across the square. "I don't trust her. I trust that she records."

"If he sells you, it's in the log."

"If he sells me, it's in the log, and she reads it before I'm back. He'll know that when he writes the first line. If he holds it, I'll know what he is, and so will he."

"And if it falls?"

"Then we'll both have our answer, and I'll have an hour to learn it." He set down the charcoal. "That's the cost. I'm paying it."

She leaned over the board and put one finger on the little square gate. It left a smudge. "You asked me."

"I did."

"You'll ask again."

"Probably." It came out drier than he meant. "Don't make a habit of being right."

The corner of her mouth moved. Then it went away, and her thumbnail started again along her forefinger, and he knew before she spoke that she was going back to the thing they had both walked round all day.

"The sword," she said.

"I know."

"Vellan said the watcher told him you lay it hilt toward me. A man on the hill who reads scent-posts can't see inside a barred room." Her voice was very low. "You told me what was in the packet. Muthu's letters say you eat better and you smile. They don't say where you lay your sword."

"No."

"He doesn't sleep in your chamber. Never has."

"No." Devan looked at the gate on the board, and the hall behind it, and the long passage to the south range, and the west stair, and every door in the house with a name on it. "Someone has stood where I lay it, Neela. Someone inside these walls who isn't Muthu."

The chain struck the hour. Neither of them counted it.
