# Chapter 4: Ask Your Captain

For the first time since the gate, no green sash stood at Ittan's door.

Devan saw it from the far end of the passage. Rema had called her escort to the hall to carry the tally chest. Bhaskaran's men held the guardroom alone, and the man holding it was Unni, thin as a spear shaft, with his mother's salt-fish smell still in his jerkin.

"Sir. The justice said two of hers at the door at all times."

"The justice is counting wax," Devan said. "Shut this behind me. Knock when the chain strikes."

Unni looked at the iron cistern chain through the passage slit, then back at him. He was not much past twenty and he had a good face for being afraid in. "Yes, sir."

The guardroom smelled of wet wool and lamp oil. Ittan sat on the cot with his writing case on his knees and his hands folded on the lid, like a man waiting for a ferry. He had been a round man. Since the sealing he had lost something from the cheeks.

"My friend," he said. "You look worse than I do."

"I haven't been given a cot."

"No. They only give those to traitors." The smile came and went. "You want something. You have your sister's face on. It is a very good face, it has never once learned to hide."

Devan took the stool by the door and kept the lamp between them.

"Rema sits assize for you. You will be heard as witness. If you swear before her that the hand that sold the passes also bought Asha's death, she has to carry you out as a witness, not a traitor. I need you sworn. I need names."

"Do you?"

"A witness gets a hearing in Kodiyur. A traitor gets a rope on the road."

Ittan looked at his hands. "Do you know what an oath is, Warden? It is a man choosing which person to be afraid of. I am a long way past choosing." He lifted his eyes. The lamp stood in them, bright and full, and nothing fell. "I will swear to nothing. Not the day of the week. I ask only one thing."

"Ask."

"My wife has a cough. It's bad in the damp, and this is the damp season." The voice was still genial. The words came slow, one stone at a time. "There is a brown jar in my case. The physicians make it, honey and root, the sweet kind, to take after the willow bark. She won't take the bark without it. If you would send it on with the next rider, and not forget that she likes it warmed."

Devan had expected a bargain. A name for a name, or a door left unlocked. He had braced for that all morning.

"I'll send it," he said.

"You will. You're a man who does." Ittan shut his eyes. "That is why they—" He stopped himself.

"Give me the names of her physicians," Devan said. "I'll write to them myself, in my own hand, with my seal. Tell them where she's to be looked after, what dose, who pays. The bill will be Kettamala's. She'll not be left."

He had not planned to say it. It came out as plain as a ration count, and he saw it land.

Ittan's mouth worked. His fingers closed on the case so hard the knuckles went the colour of tallow. For a moment the whole round, easy face was gone, and what looked out was a frightened man of forty-nine who had a wife.

"You would," he whispered. "Salt and pepper, you would. That is the trouble with you."

"Ittan."

"Ask your captain." The words fell out low and fast. "Ask your captain what he brought me."

Devan did not breathe. "When? The morning after the vow?"

Ittan opened his mouth. Then he closed it, and Devan watched him fold himself away. The hands settled on the lid. The eyes went to the lamp and stayed there. He sat four feet away, and he was not in the room.

"Ittan."

Nothing. A drip in the corner. The chain struck the hour, iron on iron, and Unni's knuckles rapped twice on the door.

* * *

Muthu was at the padippura, laughing.

He stood with Bhaskaran under the gate roof, arguing the price of a bad axle with a pepper carter who had walked up in the rain to complain. He had one arm round the carter's shoulders. The carter, who had come to shout, was grinning. That was Muthu's gift. People gave him their anger and walked away holding something warmer.

Devan stood in the arch until the carter went. Then he said, "Ittan says you brought him something."

"Brought him?" The laugh came up, huge and rolling, the one that turned heads in the lower market. "Warden, I brought him the fort's whole patience. Two meals a day and a blanket, and a lecture on the price of cloves from Bhaskaran, who'll—"

He stopped.

It was not a pause. A pause had breath in it. This was the stillness of a man set down in a room with no air, the big hands hanging, the face gone smooth. Devan had known that stillness since they were boys in the red earth, when Muthu had sworn he hadn't touched the sweets.

"That's all," Muthu said quietly. "Ask Bhaskaran. It's on the roll."

Bhaskaran, who had been studying the rain, said nothing. He had the sense not to.

Devan could have spoken then. He could have said *the packet*, *the grey wax*, *what you carried into that room at first light*. He weighed it, with the justice's escort a hundred paces off and the hearing a day away.

If he named it now, he lost Muthu. A man named stops being a captain. He becomes a prisoner, or a stranger, and either way he leaves the gate. And Devan needed sixty soldiers who believed their warden trusted every one of them. He needed the padippura held by a man the garrison loved, with Rema's eyes on it, until the hearing had been heard.

A man you suspect is safest on the wall, where you can see his hands.

"Hold the gate through the hearing," he said. "Nothing in or out without your mark."

Muthu's face came back, in pieces, the grin first. "As if I'd be anywhere else."

"No," Devan said. "You wouldn't."

He walked back through the rain with his shoulders square. Behind him Muthu's laugh started up again, loud and warm, and Devan thought it the worst sound he had heard in years.

* * *

"He told you to ask your captain," said Neela. "In those words."

"In those words. Then he shut like a door."

The lamp was low. They had not lit the second. The shutters were closed, the cotton curtain behind them hung still, and Devan sat on the edge of the bed with his sword across his knees because he had nothing else to do with his hands. Neela stood by the window with her arms folded. She had not slept. It showed in the way she held her left hand away from her body, the three bandaged fingers curled.

She had taken his report in order, as she'd asked in the morning. He had told her about Ittan before she could ask. It was the first thing out of his mouth in the door.

"Muthu is the guild's man," she said.

"Muthu is the queen's."

"You've decided."

"I've watched him write grey-wax letters for six years. They go with the palace rider." He turned the sword. "The queen's household. Rema looked at him in the hall and he went quiet for a breath. He did not do that for anyone else. He knows what she is."

"He goes quiet when he lies. You told me. He'd go quiet in front of any justice who looked at him straight." She came away from the window. "Think of the cart. I gave Muthu an order out of your hearing, and by that dusk Chathu knew to have Vellan at his gate. Who else knew?"

"You were followed."

"On a grey mare, a night and a day ahead of any hunter? No. Someone told them." Her eyes went to the floor. "A house pays its debts in news. Chathu said it to me with his sweets in front of me. And Ittan bought my contract through that house. A man who brings Ittan things is a man who answers to Ittan's creditor."

"Or the packet came from the palace and Ittan was its last stop."

"Then why would Ittan point at him?"

"To spite the one who sent it." Devan heard it as he said it, and did not like it. "A man who's lost everything tells you who to hate. It does not have to be true."

"No." She sat on the sill and the wood creaked. "Nor does yours." She looked at him. "The packet is in the hall strongbox. One cut under the wax and we'd know whose it is. Why don't we break it?"

"Because Muthu would know he is read." He had turned that over on the stair. "The day before the hearing. He'd see the seal was lifted, and he'd know by whom, and the gate goes with him. Sixty men watch the man who holds it. Whichever of us is right, I'd lose the only place I can see his hands."

"So the wax stays whole."

"The wax stays whole. We argue it out the long way."

"Then it's a choice, not a proof." She nodded slowly. "I'll take that."

"He held the gatehouse arch for me at the Resin House," Devan said. "He took spears for that."

"A man can hold a gate for you and write to someone who wants you dead. I did both for a month." The words were quiet. "He may love you. Most people who sell you do."

He had no answer for that. He looked at his hands. He had known Muthu since he was nine and Muthu twelve. He knew how the man took his tea, and which knee ached in the rains, and how he had sat up three nights after Asha with a lantern, saying nothing, drinking Janu's bitter tonic without complaint. If Muthu was lying, it was a thing built so long and so well that Devan would have to unbuild a good part of himself to see it.

"You think I'm blind," he said.

"I think you're a man who has kept one friend for more than twenty years and doesn't mean to lose him in a night." She did not smile. "I'm not arguing so I'm right. If I'm wrong, I wish to be. Muthu laughs at my jokes."

"Everyone laughs at your jokes."

"Not Rema's guards."

It almost made him smile. It did not reach his mouth.

"We have one night of it," he said. "Tomorrow, the hearing. Rema wants the tallies, the satchel, the chest, Ittan. And you sworn. What else?" He did not need to say *what else do I have that she does not.* Behind the third slat, in the hollow of the shutter frame, lay an oilcloth bundle tied with red thread. He had not looked at it in three days. He knew its weight exactly.

Neela turned her head toward the slat, and then did not.

"The payment writ," she said.

"Yes."

"Seven weeks before the wedding. The queen's green wax, with the chip."

"Yes."

"If you hand it to Rema, she reads it."

"She is a justice of the high court. It's her office to read it. The charter says a warden shall lay all evidence before a justice sitting assize." He kept his voice flat, because flat was the only way he could say it. "It's the law I've held for five years."

"And she serves the queen."

"She does."

"Then what does she do," Neela said slowly, "with a writ that proves her mistress paid for your death?"

He had asked himself that on the stair, in the passage, in the rain at the gate. He had not liked any of the answers, so he had gone on asking.

"She reads it," he said. "She goes the colour of ash. She says it's a forgery. Or she says it's real and she's grateful, and she carries it to Kodiyur in the sleeve of her coat, with Ittan in front of her on a rope." He let the sword lie. "A justice of the queen's court can try Ittan. She can try me. She cannot try the queen. There's no bench in Varanad above the queen's bench, and if there were, who would sit it?"

"So the only proof you have goes to the one person it can hurt."

"And never comes back." He stopped. "Neela, if I withhold it, I break the charter. A warden who sits on evidence at assize is a warden the queen can strip, with the law on her side. I have never broken it. Not once, in five years. It's the only wall I've built that I'm proud of."

She was quiet for a time. She did not tell him it didn't matter. He was grateful for that.

"I'm sworn at the hearing," she said at last. "As household witness. She asks me what I know. I tell her true things." Her mouth moved. "I've told you what I'll say to a justice. A true sentence with room around it. But the room is yours, Devan. If she asks me what's behind the slat, I won't know what you want me to say."

He had been about to say *I'll decide it*. He heard himself, and did not say it.

"Tell me what you want," he said. "Not what you'd bear. What you'd do."

"I'd keep it." No hesitation. "I'd keep it behind the slat until we've found a bench above hers. I don't know where one is. I'd keep looking."

"It's my neck."

"It's my name on the closing writ. If you break the charter, I'm the witness who stood beside you. I'll share that." She put one hand over her bandaged fingers. "We could be wrong. She could be better than I think. I've known three justices, and none of them was good."

"And if she asks me whether I hold such a writ?"

"Then you'll say what you say."

"I'll say I won't answer."

"She'll know there's something."

"She will know there's something," he agreed. "That's different from knowing what." He looked at the shutter, at the third slat with its small, complaining creak that he had never fixed. "I won't lie to her. I won't lie to you. I can refuse."

"Then refuse." Her voice was dry. "I'll be beside you, being a wife."

He set the sword on the floor. He stood, and she stood, and for a moment neither of them touched the other. It was not the kind of moment that wanted touching. They were two people who had weighed a thing and put it in the same pan.

"Together," he said.

"Together." She held out her good hand, and he took it as one takes the hand of a man who has agreed to a contract, quickly and hard. Her palm was cold. Then, because there was no one to see it, she did not let go for a breath.

* * *

Neither of them slept. In the last dark hour the lamp burned to a thread, the cistern chain struck the turn of the watch, and the thin rain seemed to hold its breath against the shutters.

Neela lay on her side with her eyes open. He lay on his back. She had told him once that she counted exits, steps and breaths when she was afraid. He listened, and could hear it, the small dry sound of a lip moving on a number.

He was thinking of a brown jar, and a woman he had never met, who liked her medicine warmed.

The knock was soft. Two knuckles, then a third, then nothing, as though the hand behind it was ashamed.

Devan was off the bed with his sword before he knew he had risen. Neela was beside him with her feet bare. He opened the door a hand's width.

Unni stood in the corridor with no lamp. His jerkin was wet to the collar. He had run the whole of the west stair, and he was breathing in short pieces, like a boy who has been told to carry bad water and not spill.

"Sir." His voice cracked. "The justice. She's moved it."

"Moved what?"

"The execution. Ittan." Unni swallowed. "She has found a clause in the wartime charter, sir. A prisoner whose keeping endangers the pass. Dawn. This dawn. She says the hearing can sit without him." He licked his lips. "She says she'll hold it all the same, for the record."

Behind Devan, very quietly, Neela let out a breath.

"The escort," Devan said.

"They're already in the court. All four, in green sashes. First Guard has a block." Unni's eyes were enormous. "She had Bhaskaran's night men told to stand down, sir. Sixty of us, and they walked past us to the guardroom and nobody stopped them. They said it was the queen's sash."

He hesitated, and then it came out of him all at once. "Do we stop them, sir? Bhaskaran has the lads half up. They're only waiting on you."

Devan did not answer.

He saw it whole. One word, and sixty spears would go down the west stair and take the court from four people. And by noon every man who followed him would be a mutineer against the queen's own sash, with a rope in Kodiyur waiting for each. Neela's eyes were on his face. She did not tell him what to say.

Unni waited. The rain ticked on the shutters.

Outside, over the wet roofs, the sky had not yet begun to grey.
