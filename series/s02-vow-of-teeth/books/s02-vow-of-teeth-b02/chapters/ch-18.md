# Chapter 18: Forty Names

She told him the second contract at first light, with the book open on the desk and her hand covering the line below.

"A magistrate in a river town. Bad knee. He sold verdicts, and somebody who couldn't afford the price wanted him quiet. He took the stair slowly, so I waited on the landing." She slid her palm down. "One strike. He looked surprised. The buyer paid in full."

Devan sat against the jamb with his arms across his knees. He did not nod, and he did not look away.

"The third."

"Two sisters in a coast house. They owned a share of a boat that someone else wanted. I went in by the cook's door." She read the leaf again, because she had promised every word. "Twelve nights I watched them before I went. The elder one kept bees."

That was the fact she had not meant to say, and it was out. She went on.

She gave him the fourth, a boy on a river barge, and then the buyer, who had paid for the boy and been paid for in turn two years later. She gave him the fifth through the ninth. Some had names. Some had only a trade and a town. Her voice stayed flat, because flat was the only way she knew to carry weight without spilling it. She had once seen a girl carry a full water pot the length of the yard by never looking down.

The rain stopped at midmorning. The tiles dripped. Pavi passed the door with a basket of tapped bark on her hip, the old-broken hand curled round the rim, and Neela lost a line and found it again.

By the eleventh leaf Devan had said four words in four hours. *Go on. And then?* Each time, she had gone on.

She shut the book. The room smelled of wet laterite and the bitter edge of scorched tamarind. She put both hands flat on the cover.

"Now I'll argue it," she said.

"Argue it."

"I was twenty-one." She kept her eyes on the cover. "I was brought blindfolded. I didn't pick the fort or the night. I didn't pick the girl. Chathu chose. He took the money and wrote the leaf. Vellan struck. All I did was hold a lamp and open it twice if anyone came, and no one came." She heard her own voice rise a little and brought it down. "If a lamp-holder is guilty, so is the lamp-maker, and the road, and the stair. I won't carry what was put in my hands in the dark. It isn't mine."

It was a good argument. She had made it to herself on the east stair at night for a week, in the voice of someone she would have believed.

"I know," Devan said.

She waited for the rest. He let the silence sit.

"I know it isn't yours the way the strike was Vellan's," he said. His voice was even, the way he gave facts. "I have read the leaf. You've read it to me twice. You were a child on a stair, and a man had your eyes covered." He turned his hand over on his knee. "She's still dead."

Neela opened her mouth. There was an answer in it, and the answer was the argument again, with the same words in a different order.

She shut her mouth.

He did not press. He rose, slowly, with a hand on the doorframe, and stood a moment with his back stiff under the dried shirt. "I'll be at the gate," he said.

She watched him go. Her hand had tightened on the cover until the edge of the board bit her palm. It had not been an accusation. She could not put that down.

* * *

The forty were waiting in the yard, as if someone had called a count.

Nobody had. They stood in their rows on the churned mud, hands at their sides, faces turned toward the master's door. They had stood that way when Chathu came out to read the day's work. She walked into the middle of the ground and watched their spines straighten, and she stopped there.

"I'm going to ask your names," she said. "All of you. One at a time. Say what you answer to."

A girl in the front row said, "Twenty-two."

"That's a number. What did you answer to before?"

The girl looked at her. "Before what?"

It went down the rows like that. Most gave a number. A few gave a name, and it was a shed name or a nickname, *Sparrow* or *Long Mani*, the kind one child gives another across a sleeping mat. A thin boy of about fifteen gave her three, one after another, and said, "Whichever you like." Pavi, in the first row, her old-broken hand curled against her hip, said only, "Pavi. Because you braided my hair."

Neela wrote them. She had no slate, so she took a plank from the carpentry shed and a stub of lamp-black and wrote each name down the board with the number beside it. The letters went crooked. Nobody told her so. Forty heads stayed turned to her the whole time, like a field following the sun.

When she finished, she had thirty-one names that meant anything and nine blanks. She stood a long while over the blanks.

The back half of the book lay open on a mat beside her, with Pavi guarding it. It was the resin-ink pages, in Chathu's careful hand. Each leaf had a slip stitched to its corner, thick paper, a different hand on each. She turned them one by one, careful not to read the entries. She looked only at the slips.

Names. Real ones. A village, a river, a father's trade. *Thulasi, daughter of a salt-boiler, Pazhayangadi.* *Kumaran, called Kuttu, a ferryman's son.* The slip behind Twenty-two said a name in four syllables she had never heard anyone speak. It was lovely.

The girl had come up behind her. She said, very quietly, "Does it say mine?"

Neela put her thumb over the slip.

She knew the rule. The slip was fastened to the page, and the page was the leash. To hand the page back, she had to read the entry aloud, by daylight, in the girl's hearing, and anyone standing near would hear what shape the girl took. To burn it was to burn the slip with it, and the name would be ash.

But the name was not the page. It was a few words on thick paper. She could bend and say it into the girl's ear, here, now, and the entry would stay shut under her hand.

She saw that. Then she saw what came after. A girl with a name would want the rest. She would ask which page was hers, and why Neela held it, and she would ask in front of thirty-nine others. Or she would hear *Pazhayangadi* in her own mouth and think of a road, and run for it before the groves were held, past the hunters in the wood. Neela weighed it against a girl's face and chose.

It was a Chathu kind of choice, made for someone else's good while she watched. She made it anyway.

"Yes," she said. "It does."

"What is it?"

"I can't give it to you yet." She heard how thin it sounded. It sounded like a tin of sweets. "When it's safe, I'll hand it to you whole."

The girl searched her face. Then she bowed, a small and perfect dip from the waist, and said, "Yes, Master Seventeen."

"Not —" Neela said, and the girl was already gone.

* * *

She told Devan her reasons in the gate passage, with the rain-dark wall at her back. She told him fast, as if speed would excuse it.

"Burning a page frees her and marks her rogue. A rogue is hunted by the guild and the crown. Forty rogues in a yard is a hunt with a map." She counted on her fingers. "Handing a page back means reading the entry aloud, so the room hears every shape. I can't do that to forty people in one yard with Chathu's hunters in the wood. They'd hear it. Someone would say a word in a fever or a quarrel, and a girl would be a bear in a kennel by morning."

"Rema is a day's ride," Devan said.

"Rema is a crown officer. She'd take the book to a bench. The bench would take the pages." She was not sure whether she believed it. She believed it enough to say it. "The groves have to be held. Without resin, the ache takes half of them in a month. Without a watch, Vellan walks in at dusk."

"Yes," said Devan. "Those are all true."

"Then I hold the pages. Not for me. Until it's safe."

He looked at her for a time. He did not say what he thought.

"If Rema asks who these forty are," she said, "they're the Resin House. I vouch for them, under my own name. A house with a master isn't rogue. The crown can hunt a rogue but not a vouched house."

"A vouched house has a master," Devan said. "The crown will write your name beside forty."

It was a fact, set down flat, the way he set down all of them. She felt it land.

"Then it writes it," she said. "Forty rogues have no name at all. Mine is already on a crown writ. Let it be beside theirs, where I can see what it costs."

"It will cost more than that."

"I know." She did not look away. "Say it again when it does."

He took that, as he took everything, and set it somewhere she could not see. "Then do it," he said. "I'll hold the gate."

She did it.

It took one morning. She sent six to the groves with spears from the Kettamala men's spare stack, in pairs, with a rule that no pair stood where the wind carried scent to the wood. She sent four more to the wall. She told the cook-house to feed all forty twice a day, whatever was left of the stores, and the cook-house did. She said the kennels were to be cleaned, the straw burned, every cage opened at the front and scrubbed, and eleven people took buckets and went and did it. Nobody asked how many buckets. Nobody asked why.

She had expected to argue. She had braced her shoulders for it, the way she did at a gate.

It was easy. She stood in the sun with her hands empty, and she could not stop turning it over. She said a sentence, and the yard moved. She had never in her life said a sentence that large.

At noon she said, "Someone should wash Kirri," and two girls went quietly to the third cage from the end, one with a rag and one with a basin and a cake of coarse soap. They talked to the civet in low voices. The civet, pressed against the bars, stayed very still and let them wash her face. The silver anklet with the bells cut off lay dull against one thin paw. Neela watched from the end of the aisle, and her chest hurt, and she let it.

*It is not a cage if the door is open*, she told herself. But the doors were not open. She had written no sentence for that.

* * *

Devan's horse stood saddled at the gate by the second hour past noon. He had split the Kettamala men, four to go with him, six to stay. He told the six their orders, and Bhaskaran, who was among them, took his with a face she could not read and a quick, almost privately bitter nod. Devan did not look at the forty. He looked at her.

"Rema will be at the fort by now, asking where the warden rides," he said. "I'll tell her the truth. Not all of it."

"Tell her what you can."

"I will." He pulled the strap of the girth. His hands were steadier than they had been. "Neela."

"Yes."

He didn't turn from the horse. "Chathu thought the ledger was mercy too."

He said it as he had put the brass pot by her mat, with the spout turned toward her left hand. If he had said it hard, she could have answered hard.

She stood very still. The wind moved the loose end of her wrap.

"I'm not Chathu," she said.

"No."

"I haven't kept a tin of sweets for anyone."

"No," Devan said. He put his foot in the stirrup and rose into the saddle with a small tightening about the mouth, where the welts pulled. He gathered the reins. Then he looked down at her and did not look away. "I didn't say you were. I said what he thought."

He rode out through the gate at a walk. The four went after him, mud flying behind. She watched until the wood closed over the last of the Kettamala coats.

Under her ribs the claw lines pulled. They would pull for days, and he would feel each pull on his back all the way up the wood road. She had not asked him whether it was a comfort or an insult to be so close.

*I'm not Chathu.* She said it again, inside, and it came out the same and meant a little less.

* * *

Dusk found her in the master's room, which was not a thing she had decided. The mat was by the wall, as Devan had moved it. The desk held the black book, and the loose contract leaves, and the plank of crooked names propped against the shelf. The tin was still on the end of the shelf. She had not looked at it twice.

She sat with her back to the wall and counted the doors. One. The hearth, if it counted, was two.

The cook-house had sent bread. A bowl of lentils steamed beside it. Somewhere beyond the wall the hounds were settling, and the wind off the groves brought the smell of tapped bark, bitter and sweet. Forty people slept inside that wall because of what she had said. She turned the thought over like a coin and found no obvious flaw in it.

A knock came at the frame, light, two knuckles.

"Come," she said.

The man who came in was older than anyone she had counted that morning. He was forty-one, he had told her at the names, in a voice with no hurry in it. Thilak. He had been a tapper before he was a member, he said, and a member for twenty-two years. His hands were scarred past the wrists. He carried a brass tray in both of them, the way a man carries something he is afraid to spill, and on it stood a small covered cup.

"Your tea, Master," he said.

He set it on the desk, beside the black book, without looking at the book. Steam rose from under the lid, sharp with ginger and something earthy that she knew from the long table. It was Chathu's blend. She would have known it blind.

She waited for the edge in his voice, a tilt that would let her say *don't*. There was none. He said it as he might have said *good evening*. He believed it, plainly and without effort, and so did the room.

Her mouth was open to correct him. She had the whole sentence ready: *Neela. It's Neela. Please.*

She looked at the cup.

"Thank you, Thilak," she said.

He bowed, small and exact, and went out, and drew the door half shut behind him as the master liked it.

She sat for a while without touching the tea. Then she lifted the lid and drank, and it was hot and sweet and tasted of every night of her childhood. She set the cup down beside the black book, and did not know how long she had been sitting there with her mouth still shaped around her own name.
