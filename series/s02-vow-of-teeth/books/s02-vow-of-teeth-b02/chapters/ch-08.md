# Chapter 8: Six Years of Letters

Everything on the board was in its place except one roof, and the only person who knew where it stood would not draw it.

The tack room beside the kalari had two leaks and two buckets. Devan had set the tally board on a trestle between them, and rain ticked through the thatch into tin while Bhaskaran bent over it with a lump of charcoal. Neela sat on an upturned pail with her bandaged right hand in her lap. The left did the work. In four strokes it had made the Resin House wall, crooked as a dog's hind leg.

"North side bows in," she said. "Where the drain runs. Don't straighten it. A man who thinks that wall is straight walks into it at night."

Bhaskaran grunted and did not straighten it.

"Main gate. Two hunters. Drain, two. Four under the wall, here, here, and the two corners." She tapped each place with the charcoal. "Forty in the yards by day, and they're all out of the sheds by first light. Cook-house door is barred from inside once the long table has eaten. The well house." She drew a small square, and a bar across it. "Hands and dark. We haven't solved that."

"We have not," said Devan.

Muthu was on the gate, where Devan had put him that morning. Devan had not asked himself why.

The charcoal came to the black-tiled roof behind the cook-house. It stopped.

She laid it on the trestle. She put her hand flat on her knee, and her thumbnail began to drag along her forefinger, slow, the way it did when someone asked her a question she had decided to count instead of answer.

"Roof," she said. "Long. Behind the well."

"How long?" said Bhaskaran.

"Long."

"My lads said forty-odd cages in a row. They carried water to it, after. Iron, a door at each end."

"Forty-one." Her voice was perfectly level. "Draw it as they told you."

"I'd sooner have it from the one who—"

"Sergeant." Devan did not raise his voice. "Give me the charcoal."

Bhaskaran gave it. Devan drew a roof, long and low, with a door at each end and nothing under it. Then he laid the charcoal down on the far side of the trestle, away from her hand.

"A roof is a roof," he said.

Neela looked at the board, not at him. After a moment the thumb stopped. "Thank you."

"For what? I can't draw."

The roof looked like a loaf. But Bhaskaran, who had a wife and four daughters and had learned what to leave alone in a marriage, studied the walls with great attention and asked nothing.

The well house sat in the middle of the board with its bar across it, and no arrow Devan could draw would reach it.

* * *

At dusk she sat on the window ledge in the warden's chamber with the shutters wide and the flask uncorked in her lap.

"Four people walked out of Kettamala on foot," she said. "A justice's escort. Horses at the lower stable, a cart road a day's ride from the gate, and they took the goat path." She turned the cork in her fingers. "Four of them smelled of old nightbark. One put her hand to her cuff when she saw my wrist."

"I saw the cuff."

"If they went to the coast, they're nothing to me. If they went north, to the wood, they're telling Chathu that the warden's wife is alive and has friends." She said it as a sum. "I want to know which."

"Go as far as the scree."

"As far as I can read." She looked at him. "You'll be bored."

"I've been called worse."

She smeared the resin on her throat and wrists. The burn took her at once, as it always did; he saw her teeth set. Then she did what she had done only twice before, and not once in front of anyone but him. She did not turn away. She kept her eyes on his face for the whole minute while the rest of her went out of true. He watched all of it, because she had asked him to, and a thing watched on request is not the same as a thing stolen.

The cat went through the window like water. The third slat creaked behind her.

Devan took the pebble from the sill. It was Kannan's, the white one, which she had pressed into his hand the first pebble night and never asked for back. He set it in his cheek and sat on the edge of the bed with the shutter open and the lamp unlit.

The rule had changed in the Resin House yard. A caging needed two voices now, and one of them was hers. He could have slept with his mouth open in a room full of Rema's guards and nothing would have come of it.

He kept the pebble anyway. Nobody was at his door. Rema's escort was gone, Raiu was dead, and his own men slept. If he spat the stone into his palm, no one in Varanad would know.

The chain struck the hour. He counted it, and at the last stroke his tongue pushed the stone to his teeth. He held it there a moment, then set it back in his cheek.

Near the middle watch a thin heat went down his left shin, a line as fine as a thread drawn through the skin, and stopped. He sat very still. A second one followed, shorter. After that came a long nothing. He could not tell whether the nothing was a good sign. The cistern chain clanked twice more. He held the stone, and he did not stand, because there was nowhere to stand to that would help her.

* * *

She came up the ledge at the grey of dawn with her cloth round her shoulders and her knees shaking. The third slat creaked again, like a door in a house that was glad to see her.

Devan spat the pebble into his palm and gave her the dish he had kept warm at the brazier. She ate rice and curd on the floor with her back against the bed, in the fast, ugly way of someone paying the morning bill. He sat on the boards a pace away and said nothing until she had finished it.

On her left shin was a long scrape, dark at the edges. His own shin carried its ghost, a thin red line.

"Scree," she said. "I went down it on my side."

"You lost the trail."

"In the rain. Three days of it. On the scree everything runs the same colour: water, stone, a boot, a paw." She wiped her mouth with the back of her wrist. "They went down. That much I had before the rock took it. Toward the lightning boulder and the cart road, not north. I'd have said the coast."

"Would have said."

"Because of the other thing." She put the dish down. "Something large went up the scree in the night. Up, toward the shoulder. Stones turned out of their beds, a long drag through the gravel. It was fresh. The rain hadn't filled it yet."

"Large how?"

"Heavier than I am." She did not look at him. "I followed it a hundred paces and then my nerve went. I'm not proud of it. A cat doesn't follow what it can't put a name to."

He knew what the name was in his own head. A stair, forty-four steps, four furrows across a boy's chest. He did not say it, and he watched her not say it. Below the window the cistern chain began to clank the hour, and her thumbnail dragged along her forefinger, and neither of them spoke until the last stroke had died.

"Don't ask me what I think," she said.

"I won't."

"You're allowed to."

"I know." He got up and set the cloth round her shoulders again where it had slipped. "I'll have Bhaskaran put a pair on the shoulder."

"He'll ask why."

"I'll tell him something large went up it." He let it be the whole of the answer. "That's true."

Her mouth moved. It was the nearest thing to a smile she had managed since the groves.

* * *

That evening the hall was loud.

Muthu was the reason. He was halfway down the long table with a leg of fowl in one fist, telling the story of the mule and the cistern wheel, which grew in the telling every year. Gopan had once, in the fever year, been carried a hundred paces by a mule on the chain. Gopan said it was forty. Muthu said it was a hundred and ten, and slapped the board with the flat of his hand, and the whole hall laughed with him, because it was impossible not to. His laugh came out of his chest like a dropped tray.

Devan sat at Rema's right. She had the warden's chair and he had the bench beside it, which he had not minded. Neela sat at his other side with her good hand round a cup. Janu moved up and down behind them with the ladle, scolding two guards for no reason and feeding them anyway.

Rema had said little all evening. She ate carefully, in small bites, and she watched the room the way she had watched the tallies.

When Muthu reached the mule's last moment and the hall broke into laughter again, she put down her spoon and rose. The noise did not stop at once. It thinned, the way noise does when a person of rank stands, and settled into a courteous quiet.

"I have been a guest at Kettamala for some days," said Rema. "I should like to thank one man in this hall, whose duty brought him no honour, and who deserves some."

Muthu's fist was still raised, with the fowl in it. He lowered it.

"Captain Muthu." She turned her cup in her fingers. "Six years of faithful letters to the queen's household. Not one month missed, I'm told. The household asked me to say so to your face, and I am very glad to." She inclined her head to him, a small and entirely sincere courtesy. "A garrison is fortunate in an officer who writes so well."

Nobody moved.

Devan had known Muthu since Devan was nine and Muthu twelve, in the pit, with sesame oil on their hands. In all that time he had seen the man go quiet exactly when he lied. He waited for the quiet now, and for the other thing that always came after it, the laugh that rode over the silence like a man jumping a fence.

The laugh did not come.

Muthu's mouth was open. He had a face the colour of tallow. Down the long table Bhaskaran stared into his bowl as if he had lost something in it. Janu's ladle hung over a guard's cup and did not tip. Unni, at the foot, looked from the captain to the warden and back, and understood less than anyone and more than he wanted to.

The cistern chain struck the hour. It was the only sound in the hall.

Six years. Devan did the sum without wanting to. Six years ago Asha had been found on the west stair with Janu holding the lamp. Muthu had begun to write that winter. Muthu had told him the grey-sealed letters went to his mother in Kodiyur, and Devan had believed it, and then he had stopped believing it and had thought *Ittan*. There was a sealed packet in the hall strongbox that he had not broken. He had thought he knew what kind of man had written it.

He had not thought of the house with the green wax, and the sunbird, and a payment writ dated seven weeks before his wedding.

Under the table's edge a hand closed over his.

He had not known his right fist was clenched on his knee until her palm covered it. It was her left, the good one, and it came across without a glance from her, her face turned politely to the queen's justice. The welt in his palm, the twin of the cut under her bandage, pressed against his own knuckles, and her thumb found the pulse at his wrist. Until the chain's next clank it was the one place on his body where he could feel her heartbeat. Rema's eyes passed over the gesture and moved on, as one looks away from a bride's hand on a husband's.

He opened his fingers. Nothing cracked.

"Captain Muthu," Devan said, "has stood at my left for as long as I've held this fort."

It was true. It was also all he could find that was.

"So I understand," said Rema, and sat.

The hall started again, falsely, in pieces. A guard coughed and another laughed too loud. Janu's ladle came down at last into the cup it had been hanging over. Muthu sat with his hands flat on the board and his fowl forgotten, and he looked at no one.

* * *

The hall emptied slowly, the way a hall empties after a death. Devan stayed by the cold hearth with Neela at his shoulder. She said nothing. Her thumb had found her forefinger again, and he understood that she was counting the pillars.

Muthu crossed the floor to him with the walk of a man crossing a ford.

Close up he looked ten years older. Sweat stood at his hairline, though the hall was cool. He glanced once at Neela, and she looked back at him, level and without anger, and he looked away.

"Seventh day tomorrow," said Muthu.

Every seventh day for eight years he had asked it, in the yard, in the passage, once over a corpse at the ford. *Give me a round, Devan. Come on, you great lump.* Every seventh day Devan had said no. It had become their joke, a thing a man says to a friend he is certain will refuse. Devan had never once heard what was under it.

There was no joke in it tonight. Muthu's voice came out thin and steady, like a rope pulled tight.

"Give me a round."
