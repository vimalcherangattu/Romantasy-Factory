# Chapter 30: The Sealed Writ

Nobody in the hall moved. Neela counted three doors, sixty men and one held breath, and not one of them let go of it.

"Yes," she had said, and the word stayed in the rafters like smoke.

Kandan of Mathoor stared at her from the wall. His scarred brow had gone the colour of tallow. Janu sat with her hands open in her lap as if someone had taken something out of them. Muthu had stopped being a man at a door. He was a post.

Rema nodded once and wrote it down. Then she bent and set her fingers to the green thread on the case at her feet.

"I carried this from Kodiyur," she said, "with an instruction. I was to open it if, and only if, a sworn answer to that question was yes." She drew a small knife from her sleeve and cut the thread. "I would have preferred to be told no. Sit down, Warden. It's a long sentence."

Devan did not sit. Neela stood beside him with her bound hand against her skirt, and she felt his shoulder an inch from hers, solid as a wall.

Inside the case lay one folded sheet. Rema broke the wax with her thumbnail. Green wax, crushed-leaf smell, a sunbird with its wings spread. Neela knew the chip at the left wing tip before it came clear. She had seen that wing on a payment writ under a loose slat. Beside her, Devan's breath went in and out once, very evenly.

Rema read it aloud in her clerk's dry voice.

"By the hand of Nirmala, queen of Varanad, to the border at Kettamala. Should the warden of that fort harbour, shelter, or keep as household any odiyan not vouched by a master's page, he shall be stripped of Kettamala and of every office of the border. He shall leave the fort within three days of the finding. The seal of the pass shall pass in trust to the captain of the fort, Muthu, who holds it for the crown."

She turned the sheet toward the hall so the garrison could see the date.

"Written eleven days before the Keeper's execution," she said. "Before I left Kodiyur. Before any of us had heard the word *rogue* in this hall."

"I didn't know," Muthu said. It came out of him hoarse, with no laugh anywhere in it. "Warden, I swear it. I didn't know my name was on it."

"I know," Devan said.

Neela looked at the date and did the sum. The queen had sealed Devan's death, and then, when that plan slipped, sealed his exile. She had done it with the same bird, in the same ink, before the closing writ with Neela's name on it could have reached Kodiyur. The queen had not needed a name. She had made room for one.

*Unvouched.* The word sat in her like a swallowed stone. A master's page vouched an odiyan, and Neela's page was ash. She had done that herself, at dusk, with her own mouth. The same act that made her uncageable had made her a rogue in the crown's eyes. Nobody could vouch for her now, because nobody held anything to vouch with.

She could not undo it. She could do one thing.

* * *

"Madam," she said. "May I speak to the court?"

Rema's pen paused. "You are sworn. Speak."

"The writ says *harbour*." Neela kept her voice level. She had used it on forty pages. "A man can't harbour what isn't under his roof. I'll go today, alone, over the east wall, before the sun is down. He turned me from his door. That's what you can write."

She meant it. She felt how much she meant it, in her calf and in the soles of her feet. It would hurt like a limb coming off, and it would be cleaner than anything else on offer.

"I can write that," Rema said slowly. She was looking at Devan. "If she is gone by sunset, and I see her go, I can set down that on the day of the finding the warden of Kettamala did not hold an unvouched odiyan. The writ names harbouring. I only record what I've seen." She laid down the pen. "I tell you this plainly, Warden, because it is a way out, and I will not have it said I hid one."

"No," Devan said.

He said it before she finished. He didn't turn to Neela. He stepped out from the table and faced the hall instead, so that sixty pairs of eyes went to him and left her alone. Her count, which had reached sixty-one, let go. She found herself starting again at one, and her shoulders came down from around her ears.

"Write this down," he said to Rema. "The warden of Kettamala harbours his wife."

The garrison made a sound like a long breath through a door.

"She is an odiyan. I have known it since the night we married. I shut her shutters and I kept my mouth shut, and when a man asked why, I said rain." His voice stayed flat and exact, the way he gave the guard rota. "I'd do it again tomorrow. The writ takes a fort and an office for that. Then it takes them. I held this pass for five years, and I'll hold nothing at the price of sending her over a wall in the dark."

He stopped. His jaw worked once.

"I won't answer for it to anyone but her. She doesn't get to leave alone, because I won't be left. That's all. Enter it."

Rema wrote. The scratch of her pen was the only sound in the hall.

Neela's eyes stung. She had stood in the kennel aisle at the Resin House and not wept, and she was not going to weep here. She stepped up to stand beside him, facing the garrison, near enough that her sleeve touched his.

"Listen to me," she said.

Sixty men listened. She could feel it, a hot, bright pressure.

"I came into this house with a number and a lie. I was sold. You gave me your names anyway. Vasu, whose mother keeps ducks. Kuttan, who can't write. Raiu, who is dead because of the people I came from." She did not look away from Kandan. "You can hate me for that. You have the right. I'd think less of you if you didn't."

Kandan did not move.

"He knew what I was before I told him. He kept it. In all my life nobody has kept anything of mine." She heard her voice shake and let it. "He's losing this fort because he won't let me carry that for him. So hear it from me, too. I don't go alone. I'm his wife. I chose it once because I was told to, and I'm choosing it again, now, with all of you watching. Wherever he walks out of this hall, I walk out beside him. Not behind. Beside."

She stopped. There was no more.

Devan did not look at her. His hand found hers at her side, the one without the bandage, and closed. It was not the hand of a man making a show. It shook, a little, and she held on to it harder.

* * *

Rema laid down her pen and let the ink dry.

"The finding is made," she said. "The writ stands, and I'll carry it. You have three days from this hour." She said it without pleasure. "Captain Muthu. Come forward."

He came the way a man walks onto a rope bridge. His big hands hung at his sides.

"The writ names you to hold the seal in trust for the crown," Rema said. "I'll have it put into your hand, in front of the garrison."

"I'll do it," Devan said.

He reached to his belt. The iron disc hung there on a worn cord, cut with a hill and a gate, the seal that eleven wardens had worn. He worked the hook loose. It took him two tries, which Neela had never seen. Then he laid it in Muthu's palm and folded the fingers over it with his own.

"Hold the pass," he said. "Not for the crown. For the villages below it. Parukutty's, and Kesu's, and every roof between here and the sea that can't shut a gate behind the rains. The raiders won't wait for spring because the council's changed. They'll look for the soft place."

Muthu stood with the disc in his fist. His face worked, once, ugly and honest.

"I'll hold it," he said. "I'll hold it for the border." He turned, and for the first time since Neela had met him he spoke without being loud, and the whole hall leaned in. It was not the quiet he wore for lies. She knew that one, and this was its opposite. "Not for any household. Not the queen's. Not this house. Not mine. If one of them writes to me, I'll answer it with the gate."

"That's all I'd ask," said Devan.

Muthu saluted him, fist to breast, with the iron held hard between.

* * *

They walked out down the middle of the hall.

Nobody had told them to. It simply was the way to the door, and Devan took it, with Neela on his left, on the side of her bad calf, at a pace slower than his own. She saw him do it.

The garrison stood along the wall in their grey. Their faces went by her as she walked. Vasu. Sankaran. Unni, wet-eyed and furious about it. Bhaskaran, with his jaw shut hard on four daughters' worth of worry.

Kandan of Mathoor stood three men from the door, and he was the one who had called her a witch. He had said it flat at the barracks door the week Raiu burned, with the scar white through his brow and his mother's lame leg in his voice. She had not argued. She had learned his mother's name instead, and he had hated her for it.

He stepped off the wall.

He did not look at her. He looked at the man beside her, and he brought his fist to his chest hard enough to ring on the buckle. He held it there.

"Warden," Kandan said.

It was all he said. After a moment Vasu's fist came up, and Sankaran's, and then a ripple ran down the whole grey wall, fist after fist, like a field under wind. It was not for her. She felt that, and was glad of it. She had not asked for it, and she did not want it.

Devan stopped. His face was bare as he looked at the man. Then he returned the salute, fist to breast, slow, the way one pays a debt.

They went out under the arch into the wet light of the court, and the hall closed behind them.

* * *

Rema sent for them an hour later. She was in the south range, at the long table, with the tally chest shut and a single lamp lit in the middle of the day.

She had taken off the brown wrap and sat in plain linen, and she looked older than fifty-eight. She did not rise. The door shut on the three of them.

"I want to say something," she said, "and I would like it not to be written down."

Devan stood with his back to the wall, hands folded, as if he were on guard. Neela stayed near the door and counted the shutters. Two. Barred.

"The queen does nothing by accident," Rema said.

"I've begun to notice," said Devan.

"No, Warden. Listen." She turned her pen in her fingers. "A writ like that isn't made in a bad hour. It's made in a good one, with the ink steady and the question already answered. I carried it five days down a road and opened it on my own knees. It was ready before your wife's name reached her desk, and it was ready for you. I serve her. I'll serve her again." She put the pen down. "I'll write that you left within the three days. That's the law. I won't write where you went."

"We'll not tell you," Neela said.

"Good. I'll not ask. I'd rather not lie in the record." Rema's eyes came up. They were not unkind. "But she will remember where you go. Understand me. She keeps every road a person's ever taken, and every door she's had closed on her. She will know your road, if not from me. There is always a letter. Someone always writes."

Neela thought of grey wax curling over coals, and of Ittan asking, of all things, for his wife's cough syrup. She thought of a physician, a key, and a drained lamp. Muthu had burned his. But a house that had one pair of ears had many.

"It's not Muthu," Devan said.

"No." Rema folded her hands. "I think not. I don't say it is anyone." She stood at last, the board tucked under her arm, and for a moment she looked at Neela alone. "You read the lookout leaf in a hall that could have turned on you. I've been thirty years a clerk, and I don't see that often. Whatever the queen has written about you, I've written that."

"Thank you," Neela said. It was an awkward word, and she meant it.

Rema went to the door, and stopped with her hand on the bar.

"Three days," she said. "And a closed country."

She went out.

The lamp guttered in the draught. Neela listened to the cistern chain clank the hour outside, and thought of the pass. Monkey Pass was shut for the rains, four months of it, with Devan's own seal and her name beside it on the writ that had closed the road. The hill roads were drowned. Nothing came in and nothing went out.

She had counted the exits of every room she had ever slept in. She had never before stood in one that had no way out at all.

Devan's hand found her wrist, over the wound that was not his, and held it.

"Three days," he said. He didn't say it was enough, and he didn't say it wasn't. He never lied to her.

"Where?" she said.

"I don't know yet."

It was the most honest thing anyone had ever said to her, and it frightened her more than the writ.
