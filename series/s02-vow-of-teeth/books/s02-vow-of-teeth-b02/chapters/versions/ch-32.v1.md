# Chapter 32: Sold to the Crown

They climbed for two days with the rain on their backs.

Neela rode the grey mare, who favoured her off hind and told the whole hill about it. Devan rode beside her. Behind them came forty people on borrowed horses, strung out along a goat track that no cart had ever used. Nobody spoke much. Every few hundred paces someone looked back down the slope, and then looked forward again, as if the road might change its mind.

On the second afternoon the trees gave out.

The last of the wet forest dropped behind them. Ahead lay grass and grey rock and cloud, and a wind that smelled of nothing at all. After months of lantana and laterite and resin, nothing was a strange thing to breathe.

"There," Devan said.

The shelter sat in a fold of the ridge, built of stacked stone with a turf roof and a doorway no taller than Neela's shoulder. Kesu's grandson had drawn it for them on the back of a tally stick. Pepper hunters used it in the dry months, when they came up to cut wild vine from the high gullies. There was a hearth, a stack of damp dung-cake fuel, and a long, low room that would hold perhaps half of them lying down.

The rest would sleep in the lee of the wall. Nobody complained. Neela counted the doors anyway. One. It had one door, and she felt the old fear rise and fall away, because for the first time in her life she was standing in a room with one door and wanted all forty of them inside it.

* * *

Inventory was Thilak's idea. He had kept the tapping sheds' accounts in his head for twenty-two years, and he laid it out on a flat rock in the last of the light with a care that made her throat tight.

"Flasks," he said. "One season each, no more. Forty-one, counting the spare Pavi carried." He tapped them in a row. "Tapping knives, nine. Hooked, for the bark. A grove map, Pachan's, copied small. It shows every tree on the north slope, and the three that run best at noon." He looked up. "That's what we own, Master."

"Don't call me that."

"What should I call you?"

She opened her mouth and found she did not have the word. "Neela," she said. "For now."

"Neela." He tried it as if it were a new tool. Then his eyes went to her hands, and the pale ridges at her wrists, and her split nails, and he said carefully, "You've not taken a flask."

"I gave mine up."

"Ach." Thilak looked at the row of clay flasks. "There's one spare."

"It's the spare. It stays the spare." She heard her own voice and softened it. "The ache is mine to carry, and I know the road. Nine more days, maybe ten. You'll need that flask when somebody's is lost, or someone's too sick to walk to the groves. If I ration the House by one flask for myself, I'm not a different thing from Chathu."

He said nothing. Then he nodded once, slowly, and laid the spare flask at the end of the row where everyone could see it.

Devan had been listening from the doorway. He didn't say a word. He crossed the grass, took the dung-cake basket from Kuttu, who was struggling with it, and went in to light the fire, and she felt the care in that as plainly as a hand on her back.

The ache waited. She could feel it in her nails and in the new heat behind her eyes. Forty other people in this place would feel it one day too, as soon as the flasks ran out. A season was a season. After it came a moon without resin, and after that a question nobody had answered: who in all Varanad was going to give them more?

* * *

Dusk came slowly at that height. The cloud pulled apart, and for a quarter hour the whole sky above the pass lay open, pale gold on the edges and violet in the middle.

The forty did not take resin. Thilak had asked them to wait one night, and nobody had argued. They sat on the grass in twos and threes, human and tired, and ate Janu's rice cakes out of banana leaf. Pavi was telling Kamalakshi something with her hands, and Kamalakshi was laughing. Neela had not heard her laugh before. She was twenty-two years old and had a name with four syllables, and she was eating a sweet rice cake with both hands.

Neela took her own bag of rice and went in to the fire.

Devan was sitting cross-legged by the hearth with his pack open. He didn't say anything. He took the green-stitched quire out and laid it on his knee, and they looked at it together.

It was thin. Stitched round all four edges in green thread, sealed under a thumb of green wax. A sunbird with its wings spread, and a chip at the left tip.

"Rema left it with us," Neela said.

"Rema said a seal the queen laid on was not hers to break."

"Ours, then."

"If we want." He turned it over. "We could carry it unopened. We could throw it down a gully."

"We could." She watched the firelight slide on the wax. "I'd think about it every night for the rest of my life."

"Yes," Devan said. "So would I."

They both reached for it. His hand closed on one corner and hers on the other, and for a moment neither moved. She had read eighty-two pages aloud in a day. This one was thinner than any of them, and it frightened her more.

"On three," she said.

"Whose three?"

"Yours. You count slower."

"One," said Devan. "Two."

They tore it together.

The wax cracked like old bark. The thread gave a little at a time, and then all at once. Inside lay a single sheet, folded once, and behind it the ragged stubs of leaves that had been cut away. She smelled it before she saw it: scorched tamarind, pine sap, resin-ink. Chathu's hand.

She unfolded the sheet and read the heading aloud, because that was how she read now.

"Sold to the crown."

Devan's breath changed beside her.

Under it ran a column of numbers, each with a year at its side and a sum in panam. She counted them with her finger. She counted them again, because the first time she did not believe it.

"Thirty," she said.

"Thirty what?"

"Thirty members. Thirty ledger numbers." She put her finger on the first one. It was a low one, from before her own intake. "Chathu sold them one at a time, over twenty years. Look at the sums. They go up." Her voice had gone flat. She let it. "Every one has the little bird in the margin. The one with the folded wings."

"The stubs," Devan said.

She turned the sheet and laid her hand on the cut edges behind it. A clean blade had gone through each leaf close to the stitching. Thirty pages, not burned, not lost. Taken out, as a man takes a loaf from a tin.

"They're not gone," she said. "They went north. In a green-sealed box."

"The crown leaf."

"The queen's own." Her hand shook. She laid it flat on her knee to stop it. "She holds their pages. She is the master of thirty odiyans, and she has been since before you were warden. I think she may be master of more than that."

From somewhere far below them, out of the dusk, a langur barked.

Once. Then again, farther down the slope. Then the sound ran along the hillside like a rope being drawn through a ring, and stopped.

It stopped completely. The wind went on. The grass went on. But something under the world had gone quiet, and all of them heard it.

Neela was on her feet. Devan had the knife out of his belt and the quire in the pack in one movement. She went to the doorway, and the whole camp was already rising. Forty faces turned downhill. Thilak had a tapping knife in his fist. Pavi had stepped in front of Kamalakshi without seeming to think about it.

"Nobody turns," Neela said, low. "Nobody takes resin. Stay human."

Below them, at the edge of the grass where the last stunted trees gave out, a figure was walking up the path.

He was big. He walked with a hitch on the left leg, a drag and a step, and he carried nothing in his hands. He came on slowly into the light of the fire and stopped at the edge of it, at the exact distance where a man could not be accused of coming closer.

It was Vellan.

* * *

Forty heads turned to him. She heard the sound they made: not a word, a breath drawn in through teeth. Several of them had been in the yard at the Resin House the day he came for Devan. Several of them had walked past the kennels where he put girls. Thilak's knife came up, and Neela put out a hand without looking and laid it across Thilak's wrist.

"He has no resin on him," she said.

She knew it. She could smell the clean air round him, no scorched tamarind, nothing. He stood in the dusk with bare wrists and a bare throat, and the pale scars there were the oldest she had ever seen. He was a man. He was only a man, and he had walked up a mountain alone, at dusk, on a night when every witness he had ever made was awake and listening.

"I've a page," he said. His voice was a low grumble, the same as ever. "You read it me yourself. It's in my coat. I'm nobody's but mine." He looked at Devan. "I thought you'd rather know that before you decided what to do with the rest of it."

Devan said nothing. His knife hung at his side. He was breathing slowly, very evenly, the way he did on the kalari floor, and she knew exactly what it cost him.

"Why are you here?" she said.

"The watcher at the pass village logs scent-posts. He logged a forty-strong trail going up the hills. I'm the only man in Varanad who'd walk it." Vellan's mouth moved, not quite a smile. "I'm told I'm good at following. Also I have one more thing, and I'd sooner say it to your face than leave it on a mat."

"Say it."

"Chathu's list." He nodded at the pack. "You've broken the green seal."

"How do you know it was green?"

"He told me when I was nine. The day he bought me." Vellan looked at the fire, not at her. "He said the odiyans who go up the green road never come back to the yard, and that I should be glad it wasn't me. I was glad for thirty years." His jaw worked. "I'm not glad now."

Nobody moved.

"Say it," Devan said, and it was the voice he used for the guard rota.

"The queen's guard. Her night-guard." Vellan's eyes lifted and held on him. "Her own people, the ones who stand outside her door in green. Those are odiyans. All of them. Thirty-one, when I last counted. The four who walked into Kettamala with that clerk, with her sashes on, and crossed a pass the rains had shut." He drew one breath. "They crossed it on foot because they knew the goat path. I taught two of them the goat path. They're not hunters, and they're not soldiers. They're mine, in the way anyone's mine. Sold."

"Their pages," Neela said.

"Are in her leaf. In her chest, under her hand." Vellan's voice stayed level and very low. "She keeps them as a man keeps a knife in his sleeve. And she is a witness to every one of their turnings, for Chathu always brought her to see the first. Nobody told you that. Or if they did, you didn't hear."

He did not say the shapes. She noticed that. He stood there with the whole camp watching, and he did not say one of them, not the First Guard's, not his own, not hers. He had learned to be quiet about that the hard way, and he was giving them the gift of it.

"I'd have been the thirty-first," he said. "That's how I know. 'Sell to the crown when spent.' You can read it in my own leaf. I've been spent for a while." He turned his hands palm up. "I have no master. I've nothing to offer you. But I carved this."

He took a small thing from his coat and held it out toward the fire. It was a bird, pale wood, wing folded, head turned, plum-sized.

"For the youngest," he said. "I don't know who's the youngest."

Kamalakshi was staring at it. After a moment she took one step forward. Pavi caught her sleeve, and she shook her head without looking round and walked to the edge of the light. She took the bird from his hand and turned it over. Its beak was whole.

"It smells of the sheds," she said.

"Yes."

"I'll keep it." She looked up at him. She was twenty-two, and her voice shook. "I haven't forgiven you."

"I'd think less of you if you had," Vellan said.

He didn't ask for a place by the fire. He lowered himself to the ground where he stood, the bad leg out straight, and sat there in the cold at the very edge of the light. Devan looked at him a long time. Then he took one of Janu's cloths from his pack, folded it twice, and set it on the grass beside him without a word. Vellan looked at it. He did not touch it, but he did not push it away.

* * *

Later, when the fire had burned down and the forty had gone to the long room and the lee of the wall, Neela sat outside by herself.

The cloud had closed again. A few stars showed through a rent, very white. Beyond the ridge the land fell away in black folds toward the coast, toward the pass she had crossed twice as a child, once in each direction and neither time by choice.

She took a breath. She said the first word quietly, into the wind, as if testing a stone.

"Indu."

It was a small name, a village name. A girl who had lived in a house with a lantana hedge and had hated the smell. She tried to see her. She saw a flicker, a hem, a hand in someone else's hand, and then nothing.

"Neela," she said then, and that name came easily, as easy as an old knife. It was the name Ammu had called across a courtyard. It was the name forty people had said that evening and meant. It was the name of a woman who had been counted, and had learned how to count back.

She let both names lie in her mouth. Neither one belonged to the guild or the crown. They belonged to her and to the man now coming across the grass with two cups of Janu's hot water between his hands.

He sat down beside her. They did not speak for a time.

"Thirty," she said at last.

"Yes."

"And forty here. And forty-one in the cages, and nothing I can do for them but wash their straw."

"Yes."

"The queen holds thirty pages, and she's a witness to every one. If I read them back, I give each of the thirty a dozen new witnesses. If I don't, she can cage any of them with one sentence." She pressed her hands against her knees. "Rema said she keeps every road anyone's ever taken. She'll know where we are by the new moon."

"Probably."

"You're not going to tell me it'll be all right."

"I never have." He passed her a cup. The steam rose between them. "I'll tell you what I know. We have forty people who trust us and a season of resin. We have a grove map and nine knives. We have a thing in my pack that the queen did not want read, and we've read it." He drank. "We've no hall, no seal, and no road. We'll need all of those before spring."

"We have a plank," she said. "With thirty-one real names on it and nine blanks."

"We'll need a bigger plank." His mouth twitched. "And a bigger hall. Forty won't fit in a hunters' hut for four months. Seventy-one won't fit in anything we own."

"Seventy-one?"

"Forty, and the thirty in the leaf. I'm counting ahead." He looked at her sidelong. "You taught me that."

She laughed. It surprised her, and it cracked in the middle, and she let it. She leaned her head against his shoulder, and his arm came round her, and they sat there while the wind went over the grass and the langurs, far below, began one by one to bark again.

Thirty more pages sat in a chest in Kodiyur, under the hand of the one master they could not reach. The queen knew every road and every door. She had a sunbird with a chip in its wing, and a courteous voice, and an unfinished sentence she had once written about a warden's death.

Neela thought of Janu's leopard, thin from a long way, looking down at the lit door.

Nobody was going to leave a lamp burning for thirty people in a green sash. They would have to carry it down there themselves.

She finished the water. It was still warm.

"Come on," she said. "We'll need to start the plank."
