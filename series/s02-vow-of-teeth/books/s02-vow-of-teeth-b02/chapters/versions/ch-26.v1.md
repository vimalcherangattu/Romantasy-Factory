# Chapter 26: Human at Dusk

Neela's hand went to her belt for the flask, and found nothing.

Devan saw it from a horse's length away. She did it the way a man pats a pocket he knows is empty. Her fingers closed on cloth and stayed there.

They had climbed the bare shoulder above the road, and the sun was a hand and a half above the nightbark. Bhaskaran's men sat their horses in a tight ring behind them with their spears grounded. Down in the clearing the tapping bell had stopped.

"It's in the shed," she said.

"Which shed?"

"The store, behind the tapping floor. I put it in the week I took the House." She was looking at her own fingers. "Everyone's flask went in the same box, Thilak's and Pavi's and mine, with the ration written on a tag. So nobody could say I kept more than they did." She laughed once, short. "It seemed clean at the time."

"You have the thumb of Ammu's resin."

"I spent it. The last smear went on the ridge to scout the well house." She turned her wrist and showed him the inner side, the old ridged scars pale in the failing light. "I'm a woman tonight, Devan. Thirty-nine of them will be animals, and I'll be a woman on a hill with a bad ankle and no claws."

He had known it for a little while. He had worked it out when she stopped at the rim of bowshot and did not reach for anything. Knowing it did not make it lighter.

"Yes," he said.

She said nothing else for a time. The wind brought the smell of scorched tamarind up from the clearing, and her nostrils flared at it as if it were a hand on her throat.

"I did this," she said.

"Chathu did."

"I put the flask in the box. I put off the reading. I put their names on a plank in lamp-black and told them it was safe." She still hadn't looked up. "I've become him, Devan. I kept them and I called it care, and the day it counted I wasn't there to read the pages, because I wanted to be right. That is what he does. He is always about to be right."

Devan thought about arguing. He had the facts for it, and they were good ones. She had stood on a wall under hunters' arrows and carried a ledger on a bad leg. She had asked thirty-one people their names.

He put them away unspoken. She hadn't asked for comfort. She had said a thing aloud, which was the hardest sort of work she did, and the worst answer was to take it from her.

"Maybe," he said. "That's yours to carry or put down. What you do next is still yours. I won't tell you it isn't."

She breathed in, slowly. The tension in her shoulders did not go, but it moved.

"You're not going to say I'm not like him."

"I don't know that. I know you counted the hunters out loud for an hour so you could breathe, and he never needed to."

Her mouth twitched. It wasn't a smile, but it was near one.

* * *

She went to the saddlebag with the look of a woman going to a door.

The ledger came out in its greased cloth, the black book with the contract leaves loose at the stitching and the resin-ink pages sewn in behind. She unwrapped it on a flat rock, in what light was left. Bhaskaran's men turned their heads away without being told. She turned the pages with her thumbs. They were stiff and tacky and wrong to the touch, like skin that had been wet and dried.

She stopped near the front of the back half. She did not need to read the name. She knew the place by the number: *Thirty*.

"Vellan's," she said.

She took the page between two fingers at the stitching and tore it, slowly, so the thread gave in a line. It made a small dry sound. The oilcloth flapped. She laid the page flat on her palm and held it out to him.

He did not take it.

"Chathu says I'll cage the forty tonight," she said. "He's not sure which one I'll choose, and I won't be sure either, so I may as well be honest about it. I might. If the House comes down the hill at me and I'm out of ground, I don't know what my hand does." She kept it steady. "I don't want this one in my hand. Vellan will come. He'll come for me, or for you, because he's the one thing Chathu can still point. If it's his page or one of our lives, I don't want to be the one who decides."

"Neela."

"It's yours." She said it quickly, as if speed would make it cheaper. "He killed your sister. Whatever's written there, that's the oldest right on this hill. If he comes tonight, the choice belongs to the man who has most claim to it. I'll not take it from you and I'll not make it for you." Her eyes finally came up. "And I trust you not to read it. Not until you choose."

He looked at the page. A strip of tacky paper with a name at the head, a single line of black ink and a number. A bear, written plain, and a thin hand's weight of someone else's life.

It was the cruelest thing she had ever given him. It was also the most trusting. He saw that she knew both.

"The bargain stays," he said. "You told me, if I begin to read, you take it. I told you the same. That holds."

"It holds."

"Then I'll hold it." He took the page by its edge. It was warm from her palm. He folded it once, along the tear, and put it inside his shirt against his ribs, over the old white seam, where he'd feel it with every breath.

She let her hand drop. It shook, and she pressed it flat against her thigh until it stilled.

"That's the most I've ever given anyone," she said.

"I know what it cost."

"No." She almost laughed. "You don't. But thank you for saying it."

He took Kannan's white pebble from his belt pouch and put it in his cheek. He had no resin to guard against and no sleeper's mouth to fear. But the page lay against his ribs, and he had sworn on a stair never to be the man who held a throat. A pebble slowed words. It was the only lock he trusted tonight.

* * *

Bhaskaran took it badly, and then he took it.

"You want us to ride *off*," he said, low. "With the wall full."

"East along the ridge to the cairn, where the camp was. Horses and spears." Devan took the pebble out to say it and set it back. "Face the way you ride. Nobody looks at the wall after the sun touches the trees."

"They're not our enemies."

"They'll turn. Whoever sees it becomes a witness, and carries a word in his mouth for the rest of his life. Every one of them knows exactly who you are." He held the sergeant's eyes. "I won't hand eight good men that. If you hear a thing you can't help, you hear it. You don't turn round."

Bhaskaran's jaw worked. He looked past Devan to Neela, who stood by the rock with the ledger in her arms, and some of the hardness went out of him.

"The hunters hold the road," he said. "Mine are on the ridge track, then."

"Yes. Keep the six in the sheds in sight of the ridge if you can reach them." Devan did not say *if they live*. "Go."

They went in a clatter of wet harness. Bhaskaran was the last to turn his horse, and he said nothing, only touched two fingers to his brow, as he had at the pass the morning Raiu died.

Then the hill was theirs. Neela, himself, the rock. The long gold light went red along the ridge.

* * *

It began before the sun went. He heard it before he saw anything.

He had set himself with his back to the Resin House and his face to the wood. Neela stood at his shoulder facing the same way, with her arms wrapped round the ledger and her eyes fixed on the trunks. He could see, without looking, that she was counting them.

The first cry came thin and high. It might have been a girl's. Then a second, lower, ripped off short. Then another and another, from every part of the wall, a rolling chorus that was not a battle cry. He had heard those. This was the sound a body makes when it is turned inside out against its will.

"Don't," Devan said, as she flinched toward it. "Don't turn round."

"I know."

"I'm not saying it for them. I'm saying it for you."

"I *know*." Her voice cracked on it. "I can hear Meenu."

He could hear her too. A thin shriek, and a breath, and a longer one. Meenu, who had cried at the roll leaf. A name, now, as well as a number.

He stood and listened to it, because there was nothing else to do. The pebble was hard against his teeth. After the first sixty heartbeats the cries changed. They went down into something worse, grunts and a wet tearing, claws on stone, a hunter shouting a short sharp order and getting no answer. Over it all a bitter wind off the nightbark carried the smell of fresh blood.

"A forced turning tears them," she said. Her teeth were set. "The scars at the throat and wrists. It opens them all, and they'll bleed for days."

He took the pebble from his cheek.

"This is cruelty," he said, to the dark, to the trees, to nobody. His voice was quite level. It was the voice he used for reading a charge. "I want that said, once, in the open. It isn't discipline, or mercy, or a hard sum. Chathu knows what it is. Thirty-nine people are bleeding on a wall to make them dangerous. They didn't agree to it, and he's lying to himself if he calls it a kindness. I won't call it anything else, and neither will you."

She turned her head and looked at him. He hadn't thought he had said it for her. But he saw, with the last of the light on her face, that she had needed to hear a man say it who had a right to.

"Thank you," she said.

"It's only the word for it."

"Yes," she said. "I haven't had one."

He put the pebble back.

* * *

When the cries had thinned to scattered whimpering, he turned and unslung his pack.

It was an old canvas thing, still smelling of the kalari's sesame oil. He had packed it at Kettamala without thinking, with a flint and a water skin and a twist of rice. He knelt on the wet ground now and unbuckled the flap.

"What are you doing?"

"You'll be running."

"I'll be—"

"Fast. If your ankle holds. Which it won't, for long." He took the ledger from her arms and set it in the pack, the black book wrapped in its greased cloth, and buckled the straps over it. "You don't carry the weight of other people's names on a hill. I do. I'm bigger."

"You'll be slower."

"Yes."

"Then they'll catch you."

"They'll catch both of us, one way or another." He stood and shouldered the pack. It was heavy, as it ought to be. He took the lamp from the saddle and trimmed it with his thumb, and the oil smell was sesame, the kalari, the early mornings. He hung it from the strap at his chest, unlit. "I'll light it when you say. A lamp makes a witness of the person who holds it. Mine, not yours."

She stared at him. In the grey light her face was bare, with all the guarding gone.

"The pages?" he said. "The forty?"

"Here." She touched her ribs. She had folded them flat in oilcloth and bound them with a strip of her own wrap. "Where I can reach them. Not Vellan's." She swallowed. "I won't read them, Devan. I mean it. Not unless there's nothing left."

"I heard."

"I still want to be able to."

"Then keep them," he said. "A thing you can't reach isn't a choice."

She nodded, jerkily, and dragged her sleeve across her face.

He should have said something then. There was a kind of private face a man wears, when the work is done and nobody is looking, and he found he'd put it on without thinking. His hand had gone to the page inside his shirt, to the folded edge against his ribs. It pressed there, warm. Six years he had wanted a sentence, and now it lay a finger's breadth from his heart, and he did not know if he'd have the strength to leave it folded.

*Asha,* he thought. Only that. He did not say it. His lips moved around the shape of it behind the pebble, and he hoped she didn't see.

She did. Her hand came down over his, flat, over the page.

"I'm here," she said.

"I know."

"If you start to read," she said, "I'll take it."

"And if I don't let go?"

"Then I'll hold on till you do."

It was not an answer he could improve on.

* * *

The gate opened.

They heard it before they saw it: the bar lifting, the long drag of wood across stone, and then the hush that comes before a flood. Down in the clearing the lamps along the wall had gone out. In the dusk the gateway was a black mouth.

"Wood," Devan said. "Not the road."

"The road's held."

"Then upslope. Keep the trees close. Let them come to us through the trunks, not the open." He felt her take her hand away. She had her feet planted, and he had never seen her so entirely alone in her own body. "You'll feel it first. Anything that bites you opens on me. I'll feel it a breath behind. Don't spare yourself on my account."

"I won't. I'll spare you on mine."

"Neela—"

"Don't tell me what it costs. I've counted." She was almost smiling, and it made the rest of her face worse. "Every bite on me, a welt on you. Every scratch. A mortal wound drags you down beside me. I've known it since the yard, and I'm going to bleed on you anyway, because I've nothing else to run on."

"Then bleed," he said. "I'm here."

They came out of the gate in a rush.

First the sound, a pouring of padded feet on packed clay. Then the shapes, low and quick and many, spilling past the hunters as water goes round stones: a sleek otter-dark one, a long spotted back, a jackal's thin legs, things with horns and things with teeth and things he had no name for. Their eyes caught the last red light. Thirty-nine, he counted, because she had taught him to count. And behind them the great slow shadow with a hitch in its stride, coming through the gate last of all.

Neela's breath went in.

"Run," Devan said.
