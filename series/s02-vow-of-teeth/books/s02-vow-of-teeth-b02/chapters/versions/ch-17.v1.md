# Chapter 17: The Broken Beak

The rain made the yard a bad place to be fast, so Devan went slow.

He stood in the middle of the Resin House training ground with the mud sucking at his bare feet. Eight men with spears stood at the gate and kept their faces to it, as he had told them. He raised his right hand to the level of his eyes. He held it there until it stopped trembling, and then he started the first form.

It was the drill he had made for himself at twenty-five, the winter after the ford. Open hand, slow. Palm out, turn, drop. Two fingers to the post and stop a hair short of the wood. Never touch it. A hundred times, until the hand did what it was told and nothing more.

The welts across his back stretched at every reach. Four lines, shoulder to hip, and the fourth burned like a coal. They were hers. She had earned them under a bear, and he wore them in the rain like a borrowed coat.

Palm. Turn. Drop.

The post was grey pala wood, scarred by years of other people's fists. He set his two fingers an inch from the notch below its crown, where a man's ear would be.

He had put them in that notch once. The captain at the lower ford had knelt in the ash of a burned village, with his hands open and his helmet off, and said a word in his own tongue that Devan did not know. Muthu had been holding his own opened arm. There had been a knife-cut already in Devan's ribs, from before the kneeling. Two fingers, one stroke, and the man had folded like a coat dropped from a peg. Devan was twenty-four. He had not slept properly since.

Last night he had not needed fingers. A pry bar, an old seam in a shoulder, a roar. He had known whose shoulder it was before the iron landed.

That was what he made himself look at, standing in the rain with his hand an inch from the wood. He had not hated the swing. He had waited for the arms to come down so that the blow would be fair, and it had felt like a locked door giving. He knew now he could do it again. Not as a man in a fury. As a man with a tally, who had found the last line.

Palm. Turn. Drop.

His hand shook on the fortieth stroke, and he started the count over.

*If you're reading this, I was right, and it's ugly. Say it anyway. The truth protects whoever finds it.*

The note lay in a carved box under his bed at Kettamala. He had read it so often the creases had gone soft. Asha had written it knowing she might not come back to read it herself. *Say it anyway.*

So he could say it. He could take the Keeper's quire to Rema on the day of the assize, and the leaf of Asha's death, and read it out in the hall with the court at his back. *Hand, Thirty. Lookout, Seventeen.* The court would ask who Seventeen was. The crown already held her name on a closing writ. It would not take a clerk long.

Or he could cut one line from the leaf and say nothing. That was the other road, and it was open and well-lit. He looked at it as he had looked at the notch in the post. He would not take it. He would not lie to Rema, and he would not lie to his wife by keeping quiet, and he could see no way to do neither without breaking something. Both were true. Both pulled.

Palm. Turn. Drop.

The rain came down harder, and his fingers stopped a hair short of the wood. He held them there and breathed.

"You'll take cold."

He did not turn. He had heard her on the step of the long shed, the way she set her left foot down a little short because of the claw lines. He felt it as well, a small echo along his own side.

"I'm wet already," he said.

* * *

Neela came across the yard with a grey wrap pulled over her head. She stopped at the edge of the drill ground, where the beaten earth gave way to puddles. She was too far to touch and too near to ignore. He had watched her keep that exact distance from a cornered man once, in a lane in Kodiyur, in a story she had told him. She kept it now with him.

"I'm not going to say I'm sorry for the House," she said.

He lowered his hand.

"It isn't mine to give. You'd know it for a cheap coin if I did." Her voice was still rough from the turning. "I won't say sorry for the stair either, not yet. I don't know yet what I owe there. When I do, I'll say it, and it will be the right size."

It was an honest thing to say. He noticed that it was honest, and noticed he could not lean on it. He stood in the mud and let the rain run into his collar.

"What do you need?" she asked.

Not *are you angry.* Not *forgive me.* He had expected one of those, and he had had an answer ready for each. For this one he had nothing.

"Can you look at me?" she said. "I'm only asking. If it's no, say no, and I'll talk to your shoulder."

He had been looking at the post. He turned his head and looked at her, and that was a mistake.

She stood in her wet wrap with her left arm held clamped against her flank. Her hair was dripping off her chin. Her thumbnail dragged along her forefinger and stopped, and dragged, and stopped. She looked like what she was, a woman who had read her own name aloud as the lookout at his sister's death, and then waited, with her hand on the page, to be sentenced.

He found he did not know the answer. He knew only that one true answer would be cruel and the other would be a lie.

"I won't answer that," he said.

She took it. He watched it land and watched her not flinch. Nothing in her face closed, and he was glad of it and sorry for it at once.

"All right," she said.

"I'm not leaving," Devan said. He said it quickly, before she could build anything on the silence. "I am not going back to the fort without you. I'm not going to the horses. I'll be in this yard, or the gate, or across the sill of whatever room you sleep in. I need a day. One."

"A day to do what?"

"To not say anything I can't take back."

She was quiet for a time. The rain ticked on the hood of her wrap.

"Have you eaten?" she said.

"No."

"Nor have I. They gave me rice with some kind of tamarind in it. It was too sour." She turned toward the long shed, and then said, still not looking at him, "The master's room has a mat behind the desk. I don't want it. I'll take the floor by the shelves."

"I'll have it moved to the wall. Away from the door."

She did look at him then, a short look, sharp. He had given her the number of doors in the room without being asked. He had guessed it from the way she counted them. She said nothing, and he did not wait for her to.

* * *

He did small things that day, because he could do those with his hands.

He told Bhaskaran to leave both ends of the passage open, front and rear, and to put a man at neither. He found a brass pot in the cook-house and filled it with water from the rain barrel, and set it by the mat with the spout toward the left, because she reached with that hand when she woke. He sent a boy to the sheds for the clay pot of salve the otter-girl Pavi had offered, the green one that smelled of scorched tamarind, and left it on the desk without a word. He did not stay to see her pick it up.

He stood the afternoon watch himself at the gate, with the spearmen, and let the mud take the knees of his breeches.

Forty came past him in ones and twos. They went to the sheds, the kennels, the cook-house. They did not look at him, and he did not look for their names. Once, at the kennel end, a small man came out carrying a bucket of water for the cages. He looked at Devan's bare, shaking hand, nodded as if he understood something, and went on. Devan watched the bucket go and thought: *No one told him to.*

Nobody had asked him anything about the book. They did not ask where it was. He understood then that they did not need to. They knew the room, and the door, and who had gone in.

At the dusk change he walked through the wet to the long passage behind the master's room. He did not go in. He stood where the light from her lamp fell through the door.

Neela was at the desk, in a dry wrap, with the green salve open beside her. Her sleeves were rolled and she was binding her left wrist, as she did when she wanted her hands busy. She had not touched the resin. The flask sat corked on the shelf, next to the tin she had refused to look at. The sun was gone below the wall, and she had not so much as glanced at the window.

It was dusk and she was a woman. She had chosen that, and he understood it was not for his sake. It was a night she wanted to spend as herself.

He reached into his belt pouch and took out the pebble.

It was Kannan's, small and white and cold. Gopan had taught him at sixteen to keep one in his cheek on long cistern watches, so that a man falling asleep could not shape a clean word. He had not needed it for any watch tonight. It was not for the dark. Nothing in the dusk was going to cage her, not with her own name already in his hearing.

It was for his mouth. He was tired and hurt and angrier than he could place. Words were a thing he did badly when they came quickly. Behind the white stone they came slowly, and a sentence that comes slowly can be stopped before it cuts.

He put it between his teeth and the soft of his cheek and felt it settle.

She looked up.

She saw it at once. Her eyes went to his jaw, then to the lamp, then to the unstoppered window. She had counted his sleeping breath, and she counted this too. He watched her work it out. Her fingers stopped on the wrapping.

"I took no resin," she said.

"Mm."

It came out as nothing, a hum, and he almost laughed. It was a poor laugh and he kept it behind the stone.

Her face went through three things. Her lips parted, and he thought she would say *you don't need it* or *take it out.* Then she looked at his hands, at the knuckles skinned from a drill that had touched nothing, and her mouth closed again.

"All right," Neela said quietly. She went back to the wrapping.

He stood in the doorway until the lamp burned low, and neither of them said another word.

* * *

He slept across the sill.

He took Bhaskaran's spare blanket and laid it lengthwise over the threshold of the master's room, from jamb to jamb, and lay on it on his side with his spine against the wall. His sword he laid along his front, hilt toward the room. The floor was cold, and the claw lines pulled at every breath, and he did not move.

Through the open door he could hear her. She was not asleep. He knew it by the tiny things, a rustle, the soft knock of a nail against wood, the count of her breathing. In the third hour she turned over, and the pot of water clinked against the floor, and she drank. Then she lay still for a long time, and he knew she was watching the ceiling.

He did not speak. The pebble lay against his cheek, and he thought about Asha.

*Say it anyway.*

He would. He had not decided how, or in what order, or to whom first. But there was a thing he had been taught at the border school and had never once doubted, and it was this: a decision made on one page is a decision made in the dark. He had a leaf that said *Lookout, Seventeen.* He knew what she had done in one night, one lamp, three hundred and ten counts. He did not know the other ten times, or the faces, or what she had been told. He would not judge a woman, or a court, or himself, by one leaf.

Rain eased. Somewhere in the yard a hound coughed, and a cistern chain that wasn't there clanked in his head on the hour.

He slept at last, near dawn, and woke to grey light and the creak of a board.

She was sitting up on the mat, with her arms around her knees. She was dressed, and she had been awake a long time. The black book lay on the desk with the leaves squared on top of it, as he had left them.

He took the pebble out of his cheek. His jaw ached. He held it in his palm, and it was warm now.

"Neela."

"I'm here."

"I want the second contract." His voice was rough with the night and came out clean. "And the third."

She did not answer for a moment.

"Not Vellan's," Devan said. "Yours. The next, in the order you took them. Then the fourth, and every one after, to the eleventh. I want all eleven before I decide anything."

"Decide what?"

"I don't know yet." It was the truest thing he had said since the yard. "That's why I want them."

Something went across her face that he could not read, and he did not try. She looked at the book and then at him on the threshold, where he had slept across the one door she could not otherwise count.

"They're not like the first," she said. "The first I told you. I was sick in a drain and ate after. The rest —" She stopped. "I didn't get sick after the rest."

"I know," Devan said.

"You don't."

"No. I want to."

She was quiet. Then she rose, and crossed the room, and stood with one hand on the cover of the black book. He sat up against the jamb, with the blanket round his knees and the pebble in his fist, and watched her draw out the loose leaves one by one.

"Daylight," she said.

"It's nearly daylight."

She turned the first leaf toward the window, where the grey was thinning to white, and drew a breath. He sat very still, and waited to hear what else she had been.
