# Chapter 20: Nellimala

The arrow came out of the grey with no sound before it, and it opened her across the ribs.

Neela went down between two nightbark trunks and counted. One, for the burn along her left side. Two, for the thorn scrub on the wood side, where a bowstring had spoken once and then kept quiet. Three, for the pair at her back, Vasu and Kuttan, who were already shouting and had their spears up at nothing.

"Stay on the trees," she said. "He's gone."

He was. It was a hunter's way: one shot, then away through the scrub before the light was good enough to give him a face. She pressed a hand to her side and it came back red to the second knuckle.

It was not deep. It was the second arrow of the night. The first had come at lamp-lighting from the same wood and only scored her, a line she had wiped and forgotten. She had read it as a test of the pairs, and she had been right. Then she had left the east pair standing through the dawn change, when men have stood longest and see worst. She had been awake for a day and a night, and she had counted wrong.

"Hold this." Vasu thrust a strip of his own sleeve at her. She folded it and pushed it under her wrap.

Hooves came on the wood road below, slow and uneven. Kuttan swung his spear toward the sound.

"Down," Neela said. "That's the grey mare. She favours the off hind."

Devan came up the grove path on foot, leading her. He was mud to the thighs. The road had been running water for a day and he had ridden all night through it, and his face was the colour of the clay. He stopped ten paces off. His left hand was flat against his own ribs, in exactly the place hers was.

For a moment neither of them said anything. The grove smelled of wet bark and bitter sap.

"Show me," he said.

"It's a scrape."

"Then it's a short look."

* * *

She took him to the tapping shed, because it had a door she could see from any corner. Pavi brought warm water without being asked, and the green salve in its clay pot, and went away again with her curled hand held close to her chest.

Devan had stopped her at the threshold. "Which side do you want me?"

It was a strange thing to be asked. She looked at the room. The door, the shuttered window, the drying racks. "Right. So I can see the door."

He took her right. He did not ask her to strip. He lifted the hem of her wrap the width of the wound and no more, and held it there with two fingers while he worked. The water was warm because he had had it warmed. She had once told him cold water on open skin made her teeth hurt. She had said it in the dark, in the middle of something else.

"Breathe out," he said, and pressed the cloth.

It stung. Under her ribs the claw lines from the bear pulled, and she heard her own breath go thin. His hand paused.

"Go on," she said.

He went on. His shirt had ridden up where he leaned. Across his left ribs, over the old white seam from the ford, lay a raw red line that matched hers to the finger's width, a half-breath behind. He had a mark that was cleaner than hers and did not bleed. He cleaned hers.

"That's mine," she said.

"It's ours." He turned the cloth. "I felt the first one at lamp-lighting, in front of a crown officer. This one I felt coming up the hill."

"Rema?"

"Is counting." He said nothing more about it.

She watched his hands. There were seven pale scars across the right knuckles and a thin line at the thumb. They moved with the same care he gave to hurt horses. Eleven leaves lay between them, all of them read aloud and all of them still on the desk across the yard. Neither of them said her name for the stair. Neither of them said his sister's. It sat in the shed with them like a third person who had been asked to wait outside and had not gone.

He pressed the salve on with his thumb, light as breath, and laid a clean pad over it. "Hold," he said. She held. He wound the long cloth round, twice, snug and not tight, and tied it on the side where it would not rub her arm.

"You rode all night for a scratch," she said.

"I rode all night for a woman who counts the doors in a room." He rinsed the cloth. "I wasn't asking what it was worth."

She found she had nothing dry to answer with. She said, "Thank you," and it came out plain.

* * *

Pavi came back to the shed door in the sun, not quite in it.

"Master. There's a thing on the gatepost."

"Neela," she said, from habit.

"Neela." Pavi tried it as if it might not hold her weight. "The hunters have put it there. It was warm when I found it. Nobody saw who."

It was a flat tin, round, no longer than her hand, wrapped in a cloth against the damp. She knew it before the cloth was off. She knew the dent in the lid. Eleven times before eleven contracts it had come to her mat, and each time she had learned a little more of what it was for.

She lifted the lid. Sweets lay in rows, dark jaggery gone glossy, sesame seeds pale on top. Heat still came off them. Whoever had shot at her in the dark had stood at that gate and set this down while her hands were busy with a wound.

A gift is the start of a bill. She had understood that by the fifth tin. She had not guessed the bill might be tucked under the sweets.

A folded square of thick paper lay beneath them, and a note beside it in Chathu's fine hand. The note was short.

*Child. The book is not yours to carry. Bring it home, and the account closes. What was always yours is under the sweets. Come home. C.*

Devan had come to the door behind her. He did not read over her shoulder. He stood where she could feel him, a pace off and to her right.

She unfolded the paper.

It was her slip. She knew it by the fastening-holes along one edge, where it had been stitched to a page now ash. She had seen it once in the mud of the yard, four lines, two of them in a plain hand she could read and two in the Resin House cipher she could not. Chathu had picked it up and covered it with his thumb. He had kept it three weeks.

She did not have to ask for the key. She had found it at the back of the contract leaves two nights ago, a grid of cipher letters set against plain ones, with a leaf of masters' marks below it. It was the thing the clerks had used. She carried it in her head now, from reading eleven contracts aloud. She ran her finger along the cipher lines and the letters gave themselves up one at a time.

*Intake. Indu, of Nellimala, coast side of the Monkey Pass. Village burned the second harvest. Sold at six years.*

She read it twice. Then she read it a third time, with her lips moving, and heard what her mouth did with the first word.

"Indu," she said.

* * *

It did not feel like anything. That was the first surprise. She had expected a door to open, and it was a word. It sat in her mouth like a pebble from somewhere she had never walked.

The second surprise was that the rest came after it, quietly, and fell into place.

"The goat path," she said. "Left of the split boulder. I walked it without a map." She was speaking to Devan and to the tin. "I told you I couldn't say how. My feet knew it. They'd known it since before I had a number."

"Below Kettamala," he said.

"A day below, on the coast side. Nellimala." She turned the slip over and the back was empty. "And lantana. All my life I have hated the smell of lantana. I used to cross a yard to be upwind of a hedge. The village is under it. It must be." She laughed once, short. "A hedge. It was a hedge."

"Neela."

"Indu." She said it to see if it would stay said. "Chathu says it now. After twenty-one years in his drawer."

Devan's face had gone still, the way it did when he was working out a cost. "He kept it back until he could spend it."

"He kept it for the twelfth contract. Release means the page burned and the birth name returned. That was the promise." She set the note on the tin. "It was never going to come. Now he's offering it. Not release, just the name. If I carry the book home." She could hear how level her voice was. "He's put my name in the pan of the scale, and he knows precisely what it weighs."

"Yes."

"Because he's the one who weighed it."

Devan did not say *I'm sorry*. He said, "What do you want to do with it?"

It was a question, and he waited for the answer as if there might be one. She stood there with a hot tin in her hands and thought about what a name was for.

"Read," she said. "I want to read something."

* * *

They went to the master's room because it had a table. She laid out the black book, and the key, and from her wrap she drew Ittan's tally book in its oilcloth. It was the size of her hand, forty leaves, and it had been in her wrap since the night they took the ledger.

"I've read this twice," she said. "I read the place names, and I read the sums. I can't read the marks beside them. Those are the masters' marks, and they don't live in the receipts."

"They live in the key."

"They live in the key's last leaf." She turned it over, and laid her finger beneath a small inked bird with folded wings. She had passed it a dozen times in the margin of Ittan's book, a doodle, a clerk's habit. The mark leaf had a line for it in the Resin House cipher. She read it slowly. "A sunbird alone. By leave."

"By whose leave?"

"The masters' receipts use it for the party that grants the passage." Her finger moved down the page. "A hook for a thing delivered. A slash for coin paid. And the bird, for permission given. Ittan copied his shorthand from ours, so his columns are ours." She opened the tally book flat. "He keeps two columns. On the left, his own passes, the extras, with the hook. Those I've read. On the right, a second column. Every line in it carries the bird."

Devan sat down beside her. She did not notice at first that he was not across the table. He had pulled the stool to her left and put the book between them, and he looked at it as if it might bite.

She read down the right column. Dates, boats, a count of men, a place. *Three boats of Thurai men, the long coast road, by leave.* *Eleven boats, the lower ford, by leave.* The sums beside them were small and tidy. The oldest leaves were written in an ink gone brown, copied from some earlier book in a younger hand. She turned back toward the front and the years dropped away.

"Twenty-one years," she said. "Count with me. The slip says sold at six. I was twenty-seven this spring."

Devan counted on his fingers. "I was eleven," he said, softly, to himself.

Her finger stopped.

*Nellimala. Second harvest. Two boats of Thurai men, the coast road to the foot of the pass. By leave.*

It stood in the right column, in the brown ink, under its bird. It had been there when she read it the first time. She had read the name and gone on to the sums, because it was a name like any other, and nothing in her had so much as flinched.

She said it aloud. Devan said nothing. She read the line again, and then the slip, and put them side by side so the dates touched.

"The village was burned by raiders," she said. "That's what I thought. That's what I told you. The raiders burned it."

"They did."

"By leave." Her throat was dry. "Someone gave a bird to two boats and let them up the coast road to the foot of the pass. And a little girl in Nellimala was six."

The fire spat in the hearth. Outside, someone dragged a bucket across the yard. Devan sat with his eyes fixed on the small ink bird, and she saw him go the way she had gone, one step at a time, down a road with no turning.

"The crown permits raids," he said at last. His voice was even. It was what he did with facts that cut. "Not Ittan's extras. These are the other thing. The ones he didn't sell."

"My village was in the other thing."

"Yes."

"It wasn't a failure of the border. It wasn't the cost of a bad year." She turned her hand over on the table, palm up, and looked at it. "It was agreed."

He did not touch her. He moved his own hand to the table's edge, near hers, with the palm open as it had been in the yard. She looked at it. After a moment she laid two fingers across his, no more than that, and felt the old scars under them.

They sat there on the same side of the table, with the book between them. It was the first time since the stair-leaf that neither of them was waiting for the other to be sentenced. The same bird had marked them both. He had lost a sister to its paperwork, and she a village. She did not say it, and he did not. She thought they both knew.

* * *

"Say it," she said.

"What?"

"The name. In daylight."

He lifted his eyes from the page. The sun came through the shutter slats in bars, and the bars lay across the ledger and the slip and the open tin.

She knew why she was asking. Everything at the Resin House that mattered was said in the dark. Shapes were whispered at dusk, pages read at night, the word kept in a mouth like a knife. Her name had lain in a drawer for twenty-one years because no one in that house said anything useful in the light. She wanted one word of hers said with the shutters open, by a man who could have used it and had not.

"Indu," Devan said.

He said it carefully, as if he was afraid of putting weight on a thing so new. It was not a leash. It was not a cage. It was a name, said in the clean light with the door open, and the sound of it went through her and left nothing behind but the sound.

"Indu, of Nellimala," he said, and then waited.

"Again."

"Indu of Nellimala."

She closed her eyes and let it sit. When she opened them, he was still looking at her, steady, and had not moved his hand.

"Both," she said.

"Both?"

"I'm going to keep both. Indu was what the village called a girl who is gone. Neela is what I did with the years after." She breathed. "I'll answer to either. I'll not answer to Seventeen."

"Neela," Devan said. A pause, quite small. "Indu."

"Yes."

She stood and picked up the tin. It was no longer warm. She held it a moment, the dented lid, the dark sweets in their rows, and thought of eleven tins and eleven stairs and a man who had wept at her mat and kept her page in a drawer. A kind hand had written *come home*. It was the kindest bill she had ever been sent.

She crossed to the hearth and tipped the tin.

The sweets slid out in a sticky dark rush. The fire took them with a spit and a hiss. Jaggery bubbled, the sesame seeds jumped, and a thick sweet smoke rose into the flue, so rich it caught in her throat. It smelled of every night of her childhood. She stood in it and did not step back.

When the smoke thinned, she dropped the tin in after them, and then Chathu's note on top.

Behind her, Devan had not spoken. She turned. He was looking past her, at the tally book, at the small ink bird folded in the margin of a line she could not stop seeing.

"Everything I meant to carry to the high court," he said, very quietly, "has the queen's bird on it."
