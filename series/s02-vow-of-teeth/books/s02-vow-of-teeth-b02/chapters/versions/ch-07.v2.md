# Chapter 7: Blackbark in the Rain

The knife slipped on the third cut and went into her palm instead of the bark.

Neela did not swear. She counted. One for the sting, two for the heat, three for the bright line that welled across the heel of her right hand and ran down to the wrist. The tree went on dripping black into the flask below it, unbothered.

Thirty paces back, behind the dead stump where she had told him to wait, someone sat down hard in the wet fern.

They had ridden since full dark. The wood road ran under blackbark and wild jackfruit, red mud to the horses' fetlocks, and the rain came in sheets and stopped as if someone had shut a door on it. By the time grey light showed through the canopy she had stopped feeling her feet. By noon they had left the horses tied in a ravine and climbed the ridge above the Resin House groves.

This tree was a stray. The wind had seeded it on the high ground, outside Chathu's fenced rows and his count. Its trunk was black and plated like dried mud. Nightbark can be tapped at noon, and she had known that since she was nine. A tapper's wife had taught her on a slope like this one, closing her hands over Neela's and cutting for her, singing while she did it.

She was humming the tune now without noticing. The words had never come back to her whole, only the first verses and the last, which Ammu had sung her once.

The knife had gone because of the tune. She had been listening for the next note.

A shadow fell across the fern. Devan had crossed the thirty paces without a sound. He took a square of linen from inside his shirt, already folded, and held it out.

She stared at the cloth. Then at his right hand, held a little out from his side, palm up. Across the heel of it ran a thin red line the length of hers. The welt stood up as she watched, hot and weeping at one edge, though it did not open.

"I said behind a tree," she said.

"It was a tree. It was four paces further than the one you meant." He still did not look at his hand. "Take the cloth."

She pressed it into her palm. He closed his own hand around nothing, and for a moment they stood there, two people holding the same wound. She had cut herself a hundred times. She had never had a witness to it.

"Don't wipe the flask rim with that," he said. "Tamarind and pine. It will burn the cut."

"I know." She did not know. A black drop on the edge of her palm was already burning like a spark, and she shook it into the fern with a hiss. "Now I know."

"Does it hurt on you?"

"It hurts on both of us." She nearly smiled. "That's the arrangement."

He looked at his palm at last. "Then it's a good thing I don't need it for anything."

He went back to his stump. She wrapped the linen under her bangle, tied it with her teeth, and finished the cut one-handed, slow and careful. When the flask was full to the neck, the weight of it in her left hand was so strange that she held it a moment before corking it. Twenty-seven years old, and she had never once held a full flask that was hers.

* * *

The ridge looked down on the Resin House like a hand on a map.

They lay flat on wet leaf-mould under a bank of fern. Devan watched with his chin on his folded arms, the way he watched the pass from the wall. She gave him the counts without being asked, low, as she would have to Ammu.

"Forty in the yards. Mostly women, and men of every age down to boys. The dust-coloured wraps are first-year. The ones in grey have a mark, a shape and a number." She knew where each shed door opened. "Eight hunters on the walls. Hooked poles, nets, iron rings on chains. Two at the main gate, two at the drain, and four under the wall."

"Under it?"

"The wall tells you where a man is not."

He gave a small grunt. It was the nearest he came to praise.

On the practice ground a girl of perhaps fourteen was being taught to fall. Her teacher folded her wrist into her chest and made her do it again. Two women ran the length of the east wall and back with stones in their cloths, quick and silent.

"Twenty years ago that was me," she said. "I thought it was a school."

Below them a man limped across the yard, big-shouldered, bear-slow, dragging his left leg a half-step behind the right. He stopped at the edge of the pit and everyone nearest him went still. She had not breathed since he came into the open.

"That's him," Devan said.

"Yes."

"He's lame."

"You did that."

"I struck his shoulder."

"He took it in the thigh coming down." She counted his steps. Eleven to the well, a pause, eleven back. The pause was longer than the leg needed. "Or the ground did. I was a cat, and then a woman, and then I was running."

Devan's fingers had gone white on the fern. She felt it as a tightness in her own hand. He was watching Vellan with eyes that were not afraid and that she could not read.

"He carved Ammu a bird once," she said. "He leaves them on the mats of the little ones. I don't know what that makes him."

"A man who kills with his hands full of birds," Devan said. "I won't answer that."

"I didn't ask you anything."

"You were going to. I'd have said it badly."

She turned her face back to the compound. Below the cook-house, behind a shuttered well, stood the oldest roof in the Resin House, long and low and tiled in black. She knew what it covered. Forty-one cages and one empty. A fox. A hare. A civet with a silver anklet.

She did not draw it in her head for the plan. She looked just long enough to see that no one went in or out, and looked away.

Devan's left hand moved six inches through the fern and lay beside hers, not touching.

* * *

Dusk came early, under cloud. The yards emptied in a line towards the long table. Smoke rose from the cook-house.

The door on the far side of the main court opened and Chathu came out.

She had not seen him in the daylight in three weeks, and her stomach turned over before her mind caught up. He was smaller than she remembered. Soft grey hair, soft grey coat. He walked with the uneven step of the old break that had never knit. Under his right arm, held close as a man carries a sleeping child, was a book bound in black.

"That's it," Devan said, very low.

"Yes."

"It's smaller than I thought."

"It doesn't have to be." Her voice sounded strange to her. "He doesn't shift it. Forty lives, and he carries it like an accounts ledger he's late with."

Chathu crossed the court. A hunter nodded; he nodded back. He went round the corner, behind the kennel roof, and out of sight.

Neela counted two hundred. At a hundred and eighty he reappeared, unhurried, and both his hands were free.

"The well," she said. "There's a house over it, behind the kennel roof, with shutters. I've never been inside. Nobody goes in but him."

"You're sure."

"I'm sure he came out without it. I'm sure it isn't in his room, because I was in his room at eleven with a grievance, and I looked." She wet her lips. "Hunters at the kennels guard the cages. The well house has no one."

"Because it needs none."

"Because he's the lock." She heard how that sounded. "Or something is."

Her hand rose to the ridged scars under her collar. The faint burn had begun there on its own, as it did at every sundown now.

"I can see it better from the wall," she said.

"You mean from four feet."

"I mean from the ground."

She had said all this to herself on the ride up. She still had to say it to him. She took Ammu's pouch from her waist and held out the last thumb of resin, the one smear she had kept for inside the compound. It was no larger than a cherry stone, and it shone black in the grey.

"The cache is empty, the horses are half a day down. I'm leaving you a cold camp and no fire." She breathed. "I'll keep to the dark."

"I'll take the pebble," said Devan.

He had already put one in his cheek. She had not seen him do it. When he spoke round it the words came soft. "I know the rule. I'll hold it until you're back."

"You don't have to."

"I know." He turned his face to her, in what was left of the light. "Go on."

She smeared the resin. It burned the throat first, then the wrists, the old fierce bite of scorched tamarind. Within a minute the world went wide and soft and full of smells. The last thing she knew as a woman was his hand, steady, not on her, just near, and his face turned away toward the dark, as she had asked.

* * *

The night was cold and wet and perfect.

The other one went down the slope without a sound, along the drain, flat and fast under the bitter fern. The wall had a gap at its foot where the water ran. Hunters passed above, hooked poles ticking on the stone, and she lay in the runoff and let them go by.

She could smell everything. Forty bodies at the long table. Sesame oil. Smoke. Under the kennel roof, a bitter, sweet, familiar reek that tightened her flanks: deer and hare and mongoose and a thin musky animal she did not let herself name. Hounds stirred there, close to the cages, and one lifted its head toward the drain. She froze until it lay down again.

The well house stood at the end of a flagged path. Four posts, a roof of old tile, shuttered windows. She put her nose to the sill and it was there, paper and resin-ink, dry and sour, as if the whole building were a single page.

The door was a plank door with an iron hasp, a farm lock as big as her skull, and a bar of teak that dropped into two brackets. A lock needs fingers. A bar needs hands that lift.

She pushed her shoulder against it, once. It shifted a hair and settled with a small knock.

On the far side of the kennel roof, a hunter's head turned.

The other one flattened against the stone and did not breathe. The man stood listening, one hand on his pole. Then he scratched his neck and walked on.

She knew then what she had come to learn. The book was in a house she could reach on four paws and not open. By day, in her own shape, she would be one woman in a yard of forty.

She went back up the slope to the man who waited in the fern with a pebble in his mouth.

* * *

Light came grey and without sun. She lay on the ground with the burn of the turn still in her bones and the shaking hunger tearing at her stomach. Her cloth lay folded at her hand. Devan had put it there and turned his back before she woke. He sat with his shoulders to her, spitting the pebble into his palm.

Beside the cloth was flat bread, dried plantain and a gourd of water. She ate it all. She dressed with her back to him.

They went down by the old tappers' path. Rain came and went in the trees. The horses were where they had left them.

The man stepped out from behind a trunk thirty paces from the ravine.

He was tall and stooped, in a grey wrap gone to mud at the hem, with a hooked pole in his right hand. His face was long and creased, and kind in a way that had not been used much. He was looking straight at her.

Her body knew him before her mind did. She stopped.

Pachan.

The hook came down level with her collarbone. The iron point stopped a thumb's width from her throat. It was daylight and she was a woman, and there was no resin anywhere to help her.

Devan was in front of her before she had finished the thought. He did not draw. His hands were open and a little away from his sides, and he stood with his weight low and his eyes on the iron, not the man.

"Don't," Pachan said. "Please. I'm not going to shout." He said it to Devan, but his eyes had not left her face. "I only want to say it."

"Say what?" Neela said.

"You were ten, and Chathu had you at the long table with your feet not touching the floor. You wouldn't eat. I gave you a sweet out of my sleeve." His throat moved. "That's the only kind thing I ever did in that house. And three weeks ago I held a net in that yard the day he bound you. I held it, and I looked at my boots."

"I saw," she said. "Everyone looked at their boots."

"Yes." The hook trembled. He looked at the man in front of her, at his open hands, at the wet black seam of the ridge behind them. "I should shout."

"You should," she said.

"They'd pay a year of resin."

"They would."

Rain began again, soft, on the leaves. She held very still. The part of her that always counted exits found that every one of them was a door in her own head, and behind each stood a man who could have shut it.

The hook lifted past her face, past her shoulder, until the iron pointed at the sky.

"I gave you a sweet," said Pachan, "and then I held the net. I owe you more than a sweet. I can't pay it. This is what I have."

He stepped off the path into the fern and turned his face away, like a man who has seen nothing and means to say so.

She went past him without looking back. She mounted without feeling her legs, and Devan's hand was on her stirrup a moment, as if to steady a horse that was already still.

* * *

They rode all day between downpours. She kept the flask inside her shirt against her ribs and felt it there like a second heart.

Near dark she took out Ammu's pouch to tie it to the saddle and opened it out of habit, the way she had every night for weeks. It was light, and loose in her fingers.

She had known it was empty. She had emptied it herself on the ridge. But she had not yet held it empty, in daylight, with nothing left to spend. She turned it inside out and smelled it. Scorched tamarind, faint, and under it the soap-and-sweat smell of a girl who was twenty-five.

She folded it flat, as if it might hold something, and put it back in her cloth.

"You'll want to throw that away," Devan said, without turning.

"I'll keep it."

"It's empty."

"I know what it is." She heard the roughness in her own voice. "I'm keeping it anyway."

He nodded once, as a man does when something has been said that does not need his help. The road went red under the horses' feet.

She rode with her mind on a flagged path and a plank door, and a teak bar that wanted hands.

"Say it," she said at last.

"I could go in," Devan said. He had been working it through as long as she had. "Hands at night. I have those."

"You'd have eight hunters under and on the wall, and four of them are where the wall says they're not. You'd have hounds at the kennels that know every boot in that yard, and yours isn't one. Your scent on the flagged path, your boots on the stone." She shook her head. "The other one can reach the door and not open it. You can open it and never reach it."

"Then someone opens it from inside."

"Or we make a way for the hunters to look elsewhere. And someone pays for that." She looked at the road. "Chathu put it where hands and dark can't be in the same body. He didn't need a lock. He needed us to be two people."

Devan was quiet a long time.

"We are two people," he said.

The rain began again. She did not answer. At dusk she would be a woman or an animal, and the book was behind a door that wanted both.
