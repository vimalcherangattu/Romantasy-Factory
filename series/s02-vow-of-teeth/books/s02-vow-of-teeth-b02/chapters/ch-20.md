# Chapter 20: Nellimala

The arrow came out of the grey with no sound before it, and it opened her across the ribs.

Neela went down between two nightbark trunks and counted. One, for the burn along her left side. Two, for the thorn scrub on the wood side, where a bowstring had spoken once and then kept quiet. Three, for the pair at her back, Vasu and Kuttan, who were already shouting and had their spears up at nothing.

"Stay on the trees," she said. "He's gone."

He was. It was a hunter's way: one shot, then away through the scrub before the light was good enough to give him a face. She pressed a hand to her side and it came back red to the second knuckle.

It was not deep. It was the second arrow of the night. The first had come at lamp-lighting from the same wood and only scored her, a line she had wiped and forgotten. She had read it as a test of the pairs, and she had been right. Then she had left the east pair standing through the dawn change, when men have stood longest and see worst. She had been awake for a day and a night, and she had counted wrong.

"Hold this." Vasu thrust a strip of his own sleeve at her. She folded it and pushed it under her wrap.

Hooves came on the wood road below, slow and uneven. Kuttan swung his spear toward the sound.

"Down," Neela said. "That's the grey mare. She favours the off hind."

Devan came up the grove path on foot, leading her. He was mud to the thighs. The road had been running water for a day and he had ridden all night through it, and his face was the colour of the clay. He stopped ten paces off. His left hand was flat against his own ribs, in exactly the place hers was.

For a moment neither of them spoke. The grove smelled of wet bark and bitter sap.

"Show me," he said.

"It's a scrape."

"Then it's a short look."

* * *

She took him to the tapping shed, because it had a door she could see from any corner. Pavi brought warm water without being asked, and the green salve in its clay pot, and went away again with her curled hand held close to her chest.

Devan stopped at the threshold. "Which side do you want me?"

It was a strange thing to be asked. She looked at the room. The door, the shuttered window, the drying racks. "Right. So I can see the door."

He took her right. He did not ask her to strip. He lifted the hem of her wrap the width of the wound and no more, and held it there with two fingers while he worked. The water was warm because he had had it warmed. She had once told him cold water on open skin made her teeth hurt. She had said it in the dark, in the middle of something else.

"Breathe out," he said, and pressed the cloth.

It stung. Under her ribs the claw lines from the bear pulled, and her breath went thin. His hand paused.

"Go on," she said.

He went on. His shirt had ridden up where he leaned. Across his left ribs, over the old white seam from the ford, lay a raw red line that matched hers to the finger's width, a half-breath behind. His mark was cleaner than hers and did not bleed. He cleaned hers.

"That's mine," she said.

"It's ours." He turned the cloth. "I felt the first one at lamp-lighting, in front of a crown officer. This one I felt coming up the hill."

"Rema?"

"Is counting." He said nothing more about it.

She watched his hands. There were seven pale scars across the right knuckles and a thin line at the thumb. They moved with the same care he gave to hurt horses. Eleven leaves lay between them, all of them read aloud and all of them still on the desk across the yard. She had not spoken of the stair. He had not spoken of his sister. The silence sat in the shed with them like a third person who had been asked to wait outside and had not gone.

He pressed the salve on with his thumb, light as breath, and laid a clean pad over it. "Hold," he said. She held. He wound the long cloth round, twice, snug and not tight, and tied it on the side where it would not rub her arm.

"You rode all night for a scratch," she said.

"I rode all night for a woman who counts the doors in a room." He rinsed the cloth. "I wasn't asking what it was worth."

She had nothing dry to answer with. She said, "Thank you," and it came out plain.

* * *

Pavi came back to the shed door in the sun, not quite in it.

"Master. There's a thing on the gatepost."

"Neela," she said, from habit.

"Neela." Pavi tried it as if it might not hold her weight. "The hunters put it there in the night. It was still warm when I found it. Nobody saw who."

It was a flat tin, round, no longer than her hand, wrapped in a cloth against the damp. She knew it before the cloth was off. She knew the dent in the lid. Eleven times before eleven contracts it had come to her mat, and each time she had learned a little more of what it was for. A twelfth had been held out to her in the kennels, and she had not touched it.

She lifted the lid. Sweets lay in rows, dark jaggery gone glossy, sesame seeds pale on top. Whoever had shot at her in the dark had stood at that gate and set this down while her hands were busy with a wound. And the gatepost had stood unwatched while she counted wrong.

A gift is the start of a bill. She had understood that by the fifth tin. She had not guessed the bill might be tucked under the sweets.

A folded leaf of thick paper lay beneath them, and a note beside it in Chathu's fine hand.

*Child. The book is not yours to carry. Bring it home, and the account closes. Under the sweets is the first of it. The rest I keep for your coming: who sold you, and every roll I did not burn. Refuse, and Thilak walks the north tapping path at dusk, as he has for twenty-two years. The hunters know the hour. Come home. C.*

Devan had come to the door behind her. He did not read over her shoulder. He stood where she could feel him, a pace off and to her right.

She turned to Pavi first. "Kuttan goes to the north path now, and he doesn't come back without Thilak. Tell Thilak I want him under this roof before the light goes. Tell him it's about tea."

Pavi ran. Then she unfolded the leaf.

It was her slip, the intake leaf Chathu had covered with his thumb in the mud and carried off. Along one edge ran the fastening-holes where it had been stitched to a page now ash, and along the other ran a torn edge, ragged and brown. Four lines stood on it. Two were in the members' cipher, which she had read aloud for days. Two were in the masters' hand, which she could not.

She read the first two with her finger beneath them.

*Intake.* And below it, *Sold at six years.*

Her mouth went dry. She knew those words. She had known them since the mud of the Resin House yard, and had told Devan in his chamber what the slip behind her page said, without being able to say the rest. The rest was in the masters' lines. Two lines of strange letters stood under the words she knew, small and tidy, and she could not make them say anything.

* * *

They went to the master's room because it had a table.

The black book lay where she had left it. She laid the leaf beside it and smoothed it flat with her palm. Neither of them spoke.

"The masters' cipher is on a separate leaf," she said. "At the back of the contract leaves. I found it two nights ago. A grid of cipher letters set against plain ones, and the masters' marks below. I have not had an hour to learn it."

"Then learn it now."

She found the grid. She laid the key leaf beside Chathu's and put her finger on the first strange letter of the first line. Down the left of the grid ran the cipher letters, across the top the plain ones. She found the cipher letter, followed the row until it met the plain one, and wrote it on the back of her hand in lamp-black. One. Then the next. Her lips moved. It was slow, and it was dull, and her finger shook only once.

The first word came out as four letters. She checked it against the grid a second time, because she did not believe it.

I. N. D. U.

"Indu," she said.

Then she went on, because she had to, and the rest came out of the grid a letter at a time. *Of Nellimala. Coast side of the Monkey Pass.* And on the second line, *Village burned the second harvest.*

* * *

It did not feel like anything. That was the first surprise. She had expected a door to open, and it was a word. It sat in her mouth like a pebble from somewhere she had never walked.

The second surprise was that the rest came after it, quietly, and fell into place.

"The goat path," she said. "Left of the split boulder. I walked it without a map." She was speaking to Devan and to the table. "I told you I couldn't say how. My feet knew it. They'd known it since before I had a number."

"Below Kettamala," he said.

"A day below, on the coast side. Nellimala." She turned the leaf over and the back was empty. "And lantana. All my life I have hated the smell of lantana. I used to cross a yard to be upwind of a hedge. The village is under it. It must be." She laughed once, short. "A hedge. It was a hedge."

"Neela."

"Indu." She said it to see if it would stay said. "Chathu gives it me now. After twenty-one years in his drawer."

Devan's face had gone still, the way it did when he was working out a cost. "He kept it until he could spend it."

"He kept it for the twelfth contract. Release meant the page burned and the birth name returned. That was the promise." She set the note on the leaf. "It was never going to come. Now he hands me the first of it. He's put my name in one pan of the scale, and the rest in the other, and Thilak on the beam." She could hear how level her voice was. "He knows what each of them weighs."

"Yes."

"Thilak brings me tea. He calls me Master and means it."

Devan did not say *I'm sorry*. He said, "What do you want to do with it?"

It was a question, and he waited for the answer as if there might be one. She looked at the black book, and at the tally book she drew from her wrap in its oilcloth. It was the size of her hand, forty leaves, and it had been in her wrap since the night they took the ledger.

"Read," she said. "First I want to read something."

She opened the key leaf's last page and laid the tally book beside it. "I've read this twice. I read the place names, and I read the sums. The marks beside them were only ink to me. They're the masters' marks, and they don't live in the receipts."

"They live in the key."

"They live in the key's last leaf." She laid her finger beneath a small inked bird with folded wings. She had passed it three times in the margin of Ittan's book and taken it for a clerk's habit. The mark leaf had a line for it. She read it slowly. "A sunbird alone. By leave."

"By whose leave?"

"The masters' receipts use it for the party that grants the passage." Her finger moved down the page. "A hook for a thing delivered. A slash for coin paid. And the bird, for permission given. Ittan copied his shorthand from ours, so his columns are ours." She opened the tally book flat. "He keeps two columns. On the left, his own passes, the extras, with the hook. Those I've read. On the right, a second column. Not every line in it carries the bird. Some do, tiny, folded, and with the key I can see where I'd missed them."

Devan sat down beside her. She did not notice at first that he was not across the table. He had pulled the stool to her left and put the book between them, and he looked at it as if it might bite.

She read down the right column. Dates, boats, a count of men, a place. *Three boats of Thurai men, the long coast road, by leave.* *Eleven boats, the lower ford, by leave.* The sums beside them were small and tidy. The oldest leaves were written in an ink gone brown, copied from some earlier book in a younger hand. She turned back toward the front and the years dropped away.

"Twenty-one years," she said. "Count with me. The roll says sold at six. I was twenty-seven this spring."

Devan counted on his fingers. "I was eleven," he said, softly, to himself.

Her finger stopped.

*Nellimala. Second harvest. Two boats of Thurai men, the coast road to the foot of the pass. By leave.*

It stood in the right column, in the brown ink, under its bird. It had been there when she read it the first time. She had read the name and gone on to the sums, because it was a name like any other, and nothing in her had so much as flinched.

She said it aloud. Devan said nothing. She read the line again, and then the roll leaf, and put them side by side so the dates touched.

"The village was burned by raiders," she said. "That's what I thought. That's what I told you."

"They did."

"By leave." Her throat was dry. "Someone gave a bird to two boats and let them up the coast road to the foot of the pass. And a little girl in Nellimala was six."

The fire spat in the hearth. Outside, someone dragged a bucket across the yard. Devan sat with his eyes fixed on the small ink bird, and she saw him go the way she had gone, one step at a time, down a road with no turning.

"The crown permits raids," he said at last. His voice was even. It was what he did with facts that cut. "Not Ittan's extras. These are the other thing. The ones he didn't sell."

"My village was in the other thing."

"Yes."

"It wasn't a failure of the border. It wasn't the cost of a bad year." She turned her hand over on the table, palm up, and looked at it. "It was agreed."

He did not touch her. He moved his own hand to the table's edge, near hers, with the palm open as it had been in the yard. She looked at it. After a moment she laid two fingers across the old scars on his knuckles, no more than that, and felt them under her skin, seven small ridges and a thin line.

He turned his hand a little, so her fingers lay in the hollow of it.

"It's the same bird," Devan said. "It's on my own death. It's on your village."

She did not answer. She left her fingers where they were.

* * *

She rose and crossed to the window. The shutter was stuck with damp. She put her shoulder to it and shoved it wide, and the sun came through in a flat white slab and lay across the ledger, the roll leaf and the open tin.

"Say it," she said.

"What?"

"The name. In daylight."

He lifted his eyes from the page.

"Indu," Devan said.

He said it carefully, as if he was afraid of putting weight on a thing so new. Her shoulders came down from where they had been since the arrow, and the sun warmed the backs of her hands. It was a name, said in the clean light with the shutter open, and the sound of it went through her and left nothing behind but the sound.

"Indu, of Nellimala," he said, and then waited.

"Again."

"Indu of Nellimala."

She closed her eyes and let it sit. When she opened them, he was still looking at her, steady, and had not moved his hand.

"Both," she said.

"Both?"

"I'm going to keep both. Indu was what the village called a girl who is gone. Neela is what I did with the years after." She breathed. "I'll answer to either. I'll not answer to Seventeen."

"Neela," Devan said. A pause, quite small. "Indu."

"Yes."

Kuttan's voice came across the yard, hoarse with running. She went to the door. Thilak stood behind him, tapper's knife still at his belt, breathing hard, bewildered, alive.

"Under this roof till I say," she told him. "Pavi will find you tea."

Then she picked up the tin. It was no longer warm. She held it a moment, the dented lid, the dark sweets in their rows, and thought of eleven tins eaten and a twelfth refused, and a man who had wept at her mat and kept her name in a drawer. *Who sold you.* *Every roll I did not burn.* There were forty women and men in this wood who did not know where they were born. Chathu had just offered her the key to forty drawers, and she was about to put it in the fire.

She crossed to the hearth and tipped the tin.

The sweets slid out in a sticky dark rush. The fire took them with a spit and a hiss. Jaggery bubbled, the sesame seeds jumped, and a thick sweet smoke rose into the flue, so rich it caught in her throat. It smelled of every night of her childhood. She stood in it and did not step back.

When the smoke thinned, she dropped the tin in after them, and then Chathu's note on top.

Behind her, Devan had not spoken. She turned. He was looking past her, at the tally book, at the small ink bird folded in the margin of a line she could not stop seeing.

"Everything I meant to carry to the high court," he said, very quietly, "has the queen's bird on it."
