# Chapter 21: The Sunbird Column

Devan laid the three things in a row on the master's table, and they would not make one line.

The tally book lay open on the left. In the middle was the roll leaf with Neela's village on it, and on the right the Keeper's quire, unsewn from the ledger, with Asha's leaf on top. The shed fire had burned down to a red seam. Across the yard a bucket clanked, and Pavi laughed at something, and the sound was so ordinary it hurt.

He had been staring at the same ink bird for an hour. Neela had gone to settle Thilak under a roof with a door she could count. He had told her he would sit with the book, and she had looked at his face and left him to it.

There were two columns in Ittan's hand. He had read the left one already, the hooks. *Delivered.* Extra tokens, sold on top, to Thurai men who came up the coast road with coin that was new and silver and never handled. The right column was the bird. *By leave.* A boat, a ford, a count of men.

He put one finger on each column and made himself look at what lay between them.

The hooks were thin in the oldest leaves, a few to a page. Further on they thickened, like weeds in a field somebody had cleared and left. Ittan had not built the raids. They had been there before him, agreed, with a bird beside each. He had stood in the cleared field and planted his own.

So there were two kinds of crossing. The queen's: limited, counted, through the passes she chose, in exchange for no fleet landing. He had not known the shape of it until Nellimala. Now he could see the whole bargain, and it made him feel the way a man feels who has walked a long time along a wall and finds it has no door. And Ittan's: extras, uncounted, sold on top of hers and paid into his own purse for a wife's physicians.

Asha had found an extra.

He made himself say it in order, in his head, as he would have said it to a patrol. Six years ago his sister found one token whose wax was too good. She wrote *Need a second to be sure* and tied it in red thread. She was right, and it was ugly, and she meant to say so. Ittan heard. A man who sells what the crown has not sold cannot afford a warden's daughter with a slate full of names. He sent a letter to a kind old man in the Blackbark Wood, and a bear went up the west stair.

Then, eight weeks before his own wedding, Devan had written to the council that the raiders' tallies carried true wax. He had asked to see every seal register. The registers led to Ittan's box. Within days the queen's green wax had come down on a payment writ, and a contract had been opened on him.

Ittan bought Asha's death. The queen bought his.

They were two crimes, and a single hand could not have written both. It was the plainest thing he had learned in six years, and it had come out of a margin.

He sat back. His ribs ached where hers did. The dressing under his shirt was clean, and the line had faded to a pink thread, but he still felt the wire of it.

What could he prove?

He made himself list it, the way Asha used to, with a stub of chalk on the back of a tile. Ittan's tokens: yes. Thirty-six blanks, the wax matched to Asha's token and the scouts' tally, the tally book in the Keeper's own hand. Asha's murder: the leaf in the Keeper's quire, *Buyer, the Keeper*, hand and lookout named, in the guild's own record. Rema's court could hold that. It was a conspiracy, and it had a body and a chain.

The treaty was another matter. So was the writ on his own life. Those had the sunbird on them. A warden's word would not carry them, nor a ledger from a house of killers, brought in by the man whose wife was named on a leaf. They needed the high court in full session, with the green-stitched quire opened before every bench. Rema could not do that. Rema could not even say it aloud and keep her post.

That was the case. Not the whole of it, but a case he could stand behind. Yesterday he had gone to the Resin House with his sister's name in his chest like a fist. He would go home with a list.

It did not feel like less. It felt like walking on a floor.

* * *

Neela came in with the light behind her and a clay cup in each hand.

"Thilak's tea," she said. "He made me drink some first. He says no master has ever refused his, and he's not starting with a new one." She set a cup at his elbow. It was ginger and something bitter, and he drank a mouthful out of manners. "He's afraid. He won't say. He asked if I would burn the black book now."

"What did you say?"

"That I read it first." She looked at the three things on the table and then at him. "You've been busy."

"I've worked out what I can prove."

He told her. He kept it short, the way he would have briefed Muthu: the two columns, the cleared field, the thirty-six blanks. He saw her follow the line from hooks to Asha without stopping, and he saw where it hurt her, which was the same place it hurt him. When he reached the end she put down her cup.

"And the other half?"

"Needs the high court sitting whole. The bench, the senior clerks, the other wardens. Not Rema alone." He turned the roll leaf a little, so the tear caught the light. "I want to write it down. All of it, in plain words, so a clerk can read it aloud without a key. What each leaf says, who wrote it, where it was found. And I cannot do it. I can't read the cipher, and half the ledger is in it."

She did not smile, but something eased. "You can't read it."

"No."

"You're asking me to read it to you while you write."

"I'm asking for help." The words came more easily than he had feared. "I'm no scribe either. Asha wrote my letters. I'd write *the thing* where I mean the name of it. I need someone to say it's wrong."

"I'll say it's wrong." She took the stool across from him and pulled the quire toward her. "Say it plain. 'Lookout, Seventeen' is plainer than anything you could do to it."

"I know."

"And I need something from you." She said it just as straight, and he saw what it cost. She kept her eyes on the leaf. "The groves. Hunters shot at me twice. Eight men stand on the wall of this place and I don't know yet whose they are. Bhaskaran's six can hold the pairs, but they don't know the trees, or which of the members I'd let carry a spear. I need you to stand a night with them before we leave. Tell them where the sun comes into the wood. You're the only one they'll take it from without a fight."

He looked at her. He had the fort, the court, a roll in Rema's hand with three of his refusals on it. She had forty people and a ledger and no wall of her own.

"Tonight," he said.

"You'll lose a night."

"I've lost worse." He thought about it. "I'll show Bhaskaran the east pair's blind side. You'll tell me the rest."

"Done." She drew a breath. "That's what we do, isn't it. You can't read. I can't hold a line. I ask you for a wall and you ask me for a cipher."

"It seems a fair trade."

"It's a good one." She looked, briefly, almost shy about it. Then she tapped Asha's leaf with one finger. "Now. Plain words."

* * *

They worked through the lamps. She read, and he wrote, and every few lines she stopped him. *Say who* and not *a hand*. *The Keeper, not the one who bought*. His letters stood in rows, large and square, a soldier's hand, and the pages grew in the lamplight. By midnight he had the first quire in order, and Ittan's tokens fell into place like a ledger of his own.

He came to the last leaf and put the pen down.

"This one," he said.

She did not look up. She knew which one.

*The cold month, six years gone. Kettamala, the warden's daughter, the west stair. Buyer, the Keeper. Hand, Thirty. Lookout, Seventeen. Closed. Paid in full.*

"I have to tell you what I'm going to do with it," Devan said. "Before I do it. I won't have you hear it in a hall."

Now she looked up. The lamp threw a gold line down one side of her face.

"The contract goes to the court whole. The lookout line goes with it. Every word. I'll not cut it, or blot it, or leave it in the quire and say nothing, because a case with a hole in it is the kind a queen's clerk drives a cart through." He kept his hand flat on the table so it would not move. "I will not strike out your number."

She was quiet for a moment. He could hear the cistern chain, faintly, in the wood's silence, and then remembered it was a sound from home and was hearing it only in his head.

"You'll name me," she said.

"The leaf names you. It says Seventeen. Rema already holds your name from the closing writ. A clerk who can count will put the two together before the sitting is over." He did not soften it. "I could have kept it from them for a while. I'd have won you a month. And I would have built the thing I mean to bring against the queen on a lie, and she would find it."

"A month isn't nothing."

"No. It's a month of you waiting for it to break."

She turned her cup between her palms. A tendon moved in her wrist, over the pale scars.

"I'm not asking you to," he said. "I want you to know that. If you ask, I'll hear it. I won't pretend I haven't thought of it. But I've thought of it for three days, and I can't find the place where I'd strike it out and still be the man who wrote *Buyer, the Keeper* underneath."

"The crown hunts a rogue it has a name for."

"Yes."

"The queen could send her sashes for me by the next moon."

"Yes."

"You'd have that on your hands."

"I'd have it on mine," Devan said. "It will be on yours too. That's the cost. I'm not hiding it in the margin."

For a long breath she said nothing. Then she gave a short, dry, unhappy laugh.

"I spent twenty-one years being a number nobody read aloud," she said. "Now you're going to read it in a hall."

"I'm going to read it with you in the room, if you'll be there."

"I know what you'll say. It's *true*, and we need the truth whole." She put the cup down hard enough to slop it. "You're right. That's what's hateful. If it were only cruel I could argue it. Mine is a true leaf, and I held that lamp. Whoever sits on that bench ought to hear that Seventeen was there." She rubbed her eyes with the heel of her hand. "And I don't want to be read. I want to be Indu, in a village, with a hedge. I want that more than any court."

"Both of those are real."

"Both are real," she said. "I won't ask you to strike it. I'll not do that to Asha."

It was a gift, and he knew it. He felt it settle somewhere under his ribs, next to the ache that was hers.

"It isn't the same as standing beside it," he said. He could not stop himself. "I'm not asking that, either."

"No." She met his eyes. "It isn't the same. I haven't got that far yet."

"You don't have to."

"I know. I'm telling you where I am." She said it without heat, as she'd have given the count of a wall. "I won't bargain you out of it. I won't promise I'll walk into the hall with my head up when they read it. Ask me that in a day, or when it's in the lamplight."

He nodded. He wanted to say something that would make it easier, and had enough sense to know that every such thing was a small lie.

"Thank you for telling me which," he said.

"Thank you for telling me first." She gave a crooked half-smile. "You could have just done it, and let me hear it from a clerk. I'd have hated you properly."

"I'd rather you hated me properly in advance."

That got a real laugh out of her, brief, startled. It broke the edge off the room.

* * *

She walked out with him to the grove before the wall changed. The east pair stood where they had stood the night before, and he showed Bhaskaran's men the dead ground under the tallest trunks, where a bowman could kneel and not be seen from either post. Bhaskaran grunted, and moved his pair, and said nothing, which from Bhaskaran was a speech. Neela named the trees. She named three members who could be trusted with a spear and two who could not yet. Vasu counted her hands with his eyes and then looked away.

When the sky greyed, they stood together at the gate. The dressing under his shirt had gone warm, and so, he thought, had hers. The wall behind them was only a wall.

"I'll leave the six, with Vasu over them," she said. "Pavi will hold the shed. Thilak stays under a roof." She counted them off in the air, with her thumb. "And I'm riding back with you."

He had been about to ask. He closed his mouth.

"The roll leaves are packed. The ledger rides in my saddlebag, so no one else carries it." She paused. "And I'm coming to Kettamala because there's someone there I owe. Before the court, and before anyone reads a word."

He looked at her. She was watching the road, her chin level, her mouth set the way it was when she had put a number on something and would not say it twice.

He thought of a dozen names. He did not say any. There was one he did not let himself think, because if he thought it he would also have to think what he'd say to it, and she had not asked him.

"I'll saddle the mare," he said.

"The mare favours her off hind."

"I know," Devan said. "I'll walk her up the hills."

She almost smiled. Then she turned and went to fetch her cloth, and he stood at the gate in the cold with the case he had made folded in his coat. It was larger than he had expected and lighter than it should have been, because someone had read it with him.
