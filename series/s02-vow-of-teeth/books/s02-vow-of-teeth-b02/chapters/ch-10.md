# Chapter 10: Every Name in the Barracks

Corporal Kandan said it twice, loud enough for the far table. "He bowed."

Neela stood on the wrong side of the barracks door with a basket of turmeric paste on her hip and her bandaged hand tucked against it. She did not go in.

"To a woman with a paper," Kandan said. "In his own court. Ittan sat in the guardroom, a man the warden held himself, and the warden stood by while she had the stump scrubbed. Raiu not a month in the ground, and our lord bows."

"She carried the queen's writ," said a younger voice.

"So did the last one who came for a head. Since when does he have to?" A spoon rang on a bowl. "Since her. Look at him. Eight years he won't lift a fist, and the week she's walking the walls he's in the pit with the captain. She's put something in his cup. Bewitched. There. I've said it, and not one of you will."

Nobody answered. Neela counted the silence the way she counted anything. Fourteen men, by the breathing. One of them chewed very slowly, so he'd have a reason not to look up.

Bewitched. It was nearer the truth than the corporal knew, though no cup had done it. What bound her to the warden was a rope with two ends, and a charm would have been easier to explain. She could not answer that from behind a door. But she could do the thing she did in every house.

She pushed the door open with her hip and went in.

The barracks smelled of wet wool and lamp smoke. Two rows of pallets, a table down the middle, fourteen faces coming round. She set the basket between the bowls.

"Eravi says heels first," she said. "Who's hiding a blister?"

No hand moved. Then Unni's did, because Unni was twenty and could not lie. He had stood the gate since dawn in wet boots. She sat on the bench, took his foot onto her knee, and worked paste into the raw place with her left thumb.

"Your mother still sells salt fish by the lower well?"

He went red to the ears. "Yes, lady."

"Tell her the pepper sacks are dear this year. She should hold her price."

By the time she had finished his heel she had two more. By dusk she had the rest of what she'd come for.

She wrote as she went and didn't hide it. A broken roof tile in her sleeve, a stub of chalk. A name, then the village where his mother lived. Vasu, whose mother kept ducks at Kalladi. Sankaran, whose mother had gone back to Neerattu when his father died. Kuttan, who had his mother's mouth and said so, and asked whether Neela would write her a line, since he had no letters.

She wrote it. She wrote the mother's name at the top, twice, so Kuttan would see it spelled.

She had seen the method once, in a shut room, on a slate gone grey with six years of dust. Asha, nineteen and new, had written every recruit's name and his mother's village. Standing there with the dead girl's notes in the lamplight, Neela had thought: she was afraid of them too. Afraid of a crowd. You stopped a crowd being a crowd by knowing the one thing about each man that was not his rank.

The guild had given Neela a number. She had never used these tricks to be clever. She used them because she could not stand a person going without a name. That they also worked on soldiers was only a use she'd found for the habit.

Bhaskaran's lads came first. On the first day they were wary, and by the third they were arguing over which of them she had learned earliest. Vasu showed her the duck-call his mother used at dusk, and half the room tried it, and Unni stood up when she came in and then looked as if he wished he hadn't.

Kandan she did not win.

She tried once, plainly, on the second evening. He was cleaning a spear at the end of the table, alone. "Kandan of Mathoor. Your mother is Kalyani, and she's been lame in the left leg since spring."

The cloth stopped. "Who gave you that?"

"Unni. And the well women."

"Unni gave you my mother." He stood. He was thick through the shoulders and past thirty, with a scar cutting one brow, and he never raised his voice. "You said Raiu's name in the yard at your vow. The whole house heard you say it like it was yours to say."

"I was on the stair when he died."

"I was on the wall." He tucked the spear under his arm. "A man's mother is his own, lady. You don't pick her up off the road and keep her in your sleeve."

He went out. She stood with the tile in her good hand and his name on it, and after a while she did not rub it out. A man could refuse to be known and still be named.

At dusk on the third day she was chalking on the barracks step when she felt someone watching from the yard arch. Devan stood there with Bhaskaran's report folded in his fist. He looked at the tile for as long as it takes to read forty names. His face did not change. Then he nodded once and went in.

She could read him better than most people. She could not read that.

* * *

The kitchen was the warmest room in Kettamala, and after supper it belonged to the children and the lame.

Chinna sat on her stool by the door with her bad hip propped on a sack. Kannan lay on his stomach on the floor with the plank between them, the seven hollows worn smooth as spoons. A white goat stood in the doorway, glaring at Neela. Pepper did not approve of her. Pepper did not approve of anyone.

"Wall first," Kannan said, for the third time. "The stone has to hit the wall of the hollow, and then it drops. If it only drops, it doesn't count."

"I heard you."

"You throw like you're stabbing it."

"Left-handed," said Neela. "Be kind."

He wasn't. At the fourth well she was behind by two, and Chinna's wrinkled hand had put a stone in the fifth that sat there like it had been born in it. Chinna ran the plank by an old rule. Rivals' stones could be flicked out. Cheats were rapped across the knuckles with the ladle that lay by her knee, and she had rapped every one of them, including a visiting priest, she said, though no one could say which priest.

Neela leaned over the plank. She set her knee against its edge. The plank jumped a finger's width on the third well, and Kannan said "Hey," and while his eyes went to the plank she touched Chinna's stone with the nail of her small finger and it left the fifth hollow as if it had thought better of it.

She sat back. Her own stone was already in the sixth.

"Beautiful," Kannan said, with deep injustice. "That one was beautiful."

"Beginner's luck."

"The plank jumped on the third well," said a dry voice from the door, "and Chinna's stone left the fifth by itself."

Everyone turned. Pepper bleated once.

Devan stood in the doorway with rain on his shoulders. He had been to the south range, she thought, and the gate, and heaven knew where else. He had the look of a man who had lifted too much of the day and put it down badly.

"Stones do that," said Neela.

"Mine don't," said Chinna, with the ladle already in her fist.

"Left knee on the plank. Small finger at the stone." He crossed his arms. "I played this at eight, you know. My sister beat me. I swore she cheated, and she swore she didn't, and she did it every time I played her until I was nine. I never found how." He looked at her hand, resting on her knee, the nails ragged at the quick. "I found how."

"Perhaps she was good."

"She was very good."

Chinna's ladle came down across Neela's left knuckles, not hard, and a clean sting ran up to the wrist. "Rule's the rule," Chinna said. "Even for the lady."

And Devan laughed.

It came out of him as if he had forgotten it was in the house, short and startled at first, and then it kept going. He put the heel of his hand against the doorframe. Kannan stared at him with his mouth open. In the doorway Pepper stopped glaring. For nineteen days the warden had gone about the fort with a face like a shut gate, and the sound went through the kitchen like the first sun after the rains.

Neela looked at her stinging knuckles and found she was smiling at them, stupidly, like a girl.

He sat down on the sack by Chinna's stool, still shaking a little. "Go on," he said to Kannan. "Deal her out again."

"She cheats."

"She does. Somebody should keep score."

Kannan gave Neela a pebble and a narrow look. Chinna put the ladle across her own knee where everyone could see it. Neela threw her stone, struck the wall of the second hollow, and watched it drop. She felt him watching her hand, not the stone, and did not look up.

"Unni's on both," he said, low, while Kannan was busy with the plank.

She understood him a breath later. Asha's slate and her tile. "I put him first."

"I know," said Devan, and gave her nothing else, which was more than most men could have given.

* * *

She went up alone. The ladle's sting had turned to a heat across the back of her hand, and the day had a long tail to it. Devan stayed to be beaten by a ten-year-old.

The lower court was dark and wet, with a torch hissing at the arch. The cistern chain clanked the hour. She crossed toward the west stair with her bandage held against her chest, counting the flags, and had reached sixty-one when a voice stopped her.

"You walk round it like I do."

Janu stood ten paces off with a lamp in her hand and a covered dish under her arm. She wasn't looking at Neela. She was looking at the foot of the stair.

"Round what?" said Neela.

Janu didn't answer at first. The lamp shook very slightly, and the flame made a long shadow of the parrot post at the foot of the rail, a forearm tall, the beak worn smooth as a river stone. "The flags are uneven there," Janu said at last, in the voice of a woman who had given the same excuse for six years. "Everyone says so." She shifted the dish. "Mind the wet. You've a hand to think of."

She went away toward the kitchen, her lamp swinging. Neela looked down at her own feet.

She had come across the court in a curve. She could see it now, laid on the wet stone like a rope someone had dropped: a long bend out from the straight line, a wide arc around the newel, wider than the stair needed. She hadn't chosen it. She had been counting flags.

It was Raiu, she told herself. A boy had died on that stair with four furrows across his chest and she had been on it, shouting. Anyone would walk round.

But she had walked round it on the day she came to Kettamala, before he had died. She had walked round it the first day, and the day after. She had only started thinking of Raiu when she needed a reason.

Her thumbnail began to drag along her forefinger. She made it stop, and she crossed to the stair.

The newel stood at the foot with its shut black eye. She stopped beside it. Up close she smelled wet laterite and old wax, and the pala rail was cold under her palm. She put her foot on the first step as a person puts a foot on thin ice. *One. Two.* The steps were worn into dips by a century of boots. At nine her breath began to go short, and she let it, and counted. *Ten.*

Her heel came down on the eleventh and it rang.

It was a hollow, dull note, like a struck cup with water in it. Nothing else on the stair gave it back. The other steps took her weight and said nothing, but the eleventh had a space under it.

She went down on one knee, and then flat on her good palm. The stair met the wall there, and a slot ran along the riser, black and narrow, and beyond it, low in the stone, was an iron grate.

It was new. Not new that spring, but newer than the stair. The wall around it was black with age and the laterite had gone soft at the edges, but the bars were clean and sharp, with bolt heads that still showed the file. There was no bloom of rust on them, only a skin of damp. Someone had pulled out whatever stood here before and put this in, years ago but not centuries.

For what? To clear a drain that had blocked. Any fort had a hundred drains. She looked at the bolts and told herself so again.

She stood, because her knee had begun to shake, and looked up the stair. The steps rose into the dark above the torch, and the rail went with them, and she felt her legs refuse. She had never refused a stair in her life. She'd gone up a well shaft with a knife in her teeth.

She went down backward, one step and then another, with her hand on the rail. At the bottom she turned to cross the court.

Her feet swung wide. She watched them do it, an arm and a half out from the newel, the same long curve Janu made. She hadn't asked them to.

"It's a stair," she said aloud.

The torch hissed. The chain clanked.

Neela turned her back on it and walked round to the east side of the court, to the narrow stair cut into the wall, and took the long way up. The wall-walk, the cold turn under the lamp niche, the long passage round the upper court. She counted it all. Eighty-one steps, against the forty-four she would have taken, and she did not slow on any of them.

At the chamber door she stood with her hand on the latch. The grate had not touched her. The stair had not touched her. She was certain of it.

So why did her knees remember it?
