# Chapter 10: Every Name in the Barracks

Corporal Kandan said it twice, loud enough for the far table. "He bowed."

Neela stood on the wrong side of the barracks door with a basket of turmeric paste on her hip and her bandaged left hand tucked against it. She did not go in.

"To a woman with a paper," Kandan said. "In his own court. Ittan sat in the guardroom, a man the warden held himself, and the warden stood by while she had the stump scrubbed. Raiu not a month burned, and our lord bows."

"She carried the queen's writ," said a younger voice.

"So did the last one who came for a head. Since when does he have to?" A spoon rang on a bowl. "Since her. Look at him. Eight years he won't lift a fist, and the week she's walking the walls he's in the pit with the captain. She's put something in his cup. Bewitched. There. I've said it, and not one of you will."

Nobody answered. Neela counted the silence the way she counted anything. Fourteen men, by the breathing. One of them chewed very slowly, so he'd have a reason not to look up.

Bewitched. It was nearer the truth than the corporal knew, though no cup had done it. What bound her to the warden was a rope with two ends, and a charm would have been easier to explain. She could not answer that from behind a door. But she could do what she did in every house.

She pushed the door open with her hip and went in.

The barracks smelled of wet wool and lamp smoke. Two rows of pallets, a table down the middle, fourteen faces coming round. She set the basket between the bowls.

"Eravi says heels first," she said. "Who's hiding a blister?"

No hand moved. Then Unni's did, because Unni was twenty and could not lie. He had stood the gate since dawn in wet boots. She sat on the bench, took his foot onto her knee, and worked paste into the raw place with her right thumb. The scab across her palm, three days old from the nightbark tree, pulled at every stroke.

"Your mother still sells salt fish by the lower well?"

He went red to the ears. "Yes, lady."

"Tell her the pepper sacks are dear this year. She should hold her price."

By the time she finished his heel she had two more. By dusk she had the rest of what she'd come for.

She wrote as she went and didn't hide it. A broken roof tile in her sleeve, a stub of chalk. A name, then the village where his mother lived. Vasu, whose mother kept ducks at Kalladi. Sankaran, whose mother had gone back to Neerattu when his father died. Kuttan, who had his mother's mouth and said so, and asked whether Neela would write her a line, since he had no letters.

She wrote it. She put the mother's name at the top, twice, so Kuttan would see it spelled.

She had seen the method once, in a shut room, on a slate gone grey with six years of dust. Asha, nineteen and new, had written every recruit's name and his mother's village. Standing there in the lamplight, Neela had understood it. You stopped a crowd being a crowd by knowing the one thing about each man that was not his rank.

Bhaskaran's lads came first. On the first day they were wary, and by the third they were arguing over which of them she had learned earliest. Vasu showed her the duck-call his mother used at dusk, and half the room tried it. Unni stood up when she came in and then looked as if he wished he hadn't.

Kandan she did not win.

She tried once, plainly, on the second evening. He was cleaning a spear at the end of the table, alone. "Kandan of Mathoor. Your mother is Kalyani, and she's been lame in the left leg since spring."

The cloth stopped. "Who gave you that?"

"Unni. And the well women."

"Unni gave you my mother." He stood. He was thick through the shoulders and past thirty, with a scar cutting one brow, and he never raised his voice. "You said Raiu's name in the yard at your vow. The whole house heard you say it like it was yours to say."

"I was on the stair when he died."

"I was on the wall." He tucked the spear under his arm. "A man's mother is his own, lady. You don't pick her up off the road and keep her in your sleeve."

"Then what do you want me to call her?"

"Nothing. Justice Rema still sits in the south range taking statements for her roll." He said it mildly, the way he said everything. "I've one to give her. A bride who walks a barracks learning every man's house, and a warden who bows the week she does it. The queen's justice can make of that what she likes. Your name's on a crown writ already, I hear."

He went out. Neela stood with the tile in her good hand and his name on it. She knew what a roll was. A roll was how a crown kept a record of who might be hunted.

After a while she did not rub the name out. A man could refuse to be known and still be named. But she did not take it to Devan either. He would move Kandan to the far wall and make him a martyr, and the men who were laughing at the duck-call would stop.

At dusk on the third day she was chalking on the barracks step when she saw someone watching from the yard arch. Devan stood there with Bhaskaran's report folded in his fist. He looked at the tile for as long as it takes to read forty names. His face did not change. Then he nodded once and went in.

She could read him better than most people. She could not read that.

* * *

The kitchen was the warmest room in Kettamala, and after supper it belonged to the children and the lame.

Chinna sat on her stool by the door with her bad hip propped on a sack. Kannan lay on his stomach on the floor with the plank between them, the seven hollows worn smooth as spoons. A white goat stood in the doorway, glaring at Neela. Pepper did not approve of her. Pepper did not approve of anyone.

"Wall first," Kannan said, for the third time. "The stone has to hit the wall of the hollow, and then it drops. If it only drops, it doesn't count."

"I heard you."

"You throw like you're stabbing it."

"One good hand, and it's the scabbed one," said Neela. "Be kind."

He wasn't. At the fourth well she was behind by two, and Chinna's wrinkled hand had put a stone in the fifth that sat there like it had been born in it. Chinna ran the plank by an old rule. Rivals' stones could be flicked out. Cheats were rapped across the knuckles with the ladle that lay by her knee, and she had rapped every one of them, including a visiting priest, she said, though no one could say which priest.

Neela leaned over the plank and set her knee against its edge. The plank jumped a finger's width on the third well, and Kannan said "Hey." While his eyes went to the plank she touched Chinna's stone with the nail of her small finger. It left the fifth hollow as if it had thought better of it.

She sat back. Her own stone was already in the sixth.

"Beautiful," Kannan said, with deep injustice. "That one was beautiful."

"Beginner's luck."

"The plank jumped on the third well," said a dry voice from the door, "and Chinna's stone left the fifth by itself."

Everyone turned. Pepper bleated once.

Devan stood in the doorway with rain on his shoulders. He had been to the south range, she thought, and the gate, and a dozen other places that wanted him. He looked like a man who had lifted too much of the day and put it down badly.

"Stones do that," said Neela.

"Mine don't," said Chinna, with the ladle already in her fist.

"Left knee on the plank. Small finger at the stone." He crossed his arms. "My sister's way. I told you she cheated and I never found how." He looked at her hand, resting on her knee, the nails ragged at the quick. "I found how."

"Perhaps she was good."

"She was very good."

Chinna's ladle came down across the knuckles of Neela's right hand, and its iron rim nicked the skin over one knuckle, and a clean sting ran up to the wrist. "Rule's the rule," Chinna said. "Even for the lady."

Across the room Devan's own right hand twitched against his sleeve. A red welt rose over the old scars on his knuckles, as if the ladle had found him too. He looked down at it. Then he looked at her.

And Devan laughed.

It came out of him as if he had forgotten it was in the house, short and startled at first, and then it kept going. He put the heel of his hand against the doorframe, welt and all. Kannan stared at him with his mouth open. In the doorway Pepper stopped glaring. Since the block, the warden had gone about the fort with a face like a shut gate, and the sound went through the kitchen like the first sun after the rains.

Neela looked at her stinging knuckles and found she was smiling at them, helplessly.

He sat down on the sack by Chinna's stool, still shaking a little, rubbing the welt with his thumb. "Go on," he said to Kannan. "Deal her out again."

"She cheats."

"She does. Somebody should keep score."

Kannan gave Neela a pebble and a narrow look. Chinna put the ladle across her own knee where everyone could see it. Neela threw her stone, struck the wall of the second hollow, and watched it drop. Devan was watching her hand, not the stone. She did not look up.

"Unni's on both," he said, low, while Kannan was busy with the plank.

She understood him a breath later. Asha's slate and her tile. "I put him first."

"I know," said Devan, and gave her nothing else, which was more than most men could have given.

* * *

She went up alone. The ladle's sting had turned to a heat across the back of her hand, and the day had a long tail to it. Devan stayed to be beaten by a nine-year-old.

The lower court was dark and wet, with a torch hissing at the arch. The cistern chain clanked the hour. She crossed toward the west stair with her bandage held against her chest, counting the flags, and had reached sixty-one when a voice stopped her.

"You walk round it like I do."

Janu stood ten paces off with a lamp in her hand and a covered dish under her arm. She wasn't looking at Neela. She was looking at the foot of the stair.

"Round what?" said Neela.

Janu didn't answer at first. The lamp shook very slightly, and the flame threw a long shadow of the parrot post across the rail, a forearm tall, the beak worn smooth as a river stone. "The flags are uneven there," Janu said at last, in the voice of a woman who had given the same excuse for six years. "Everyone says so." She shifted the dish. "Mind the wet. You've a hand to think of."

She went away toward the kitchen, her lamp swinging. Neela looked down at her own feet.

She had come across the court in a curve. It lay on the wet stone like a rope someone had dropped: a long bend out from the straight line, a wide arc around the newel.

She had known. That was the trouble. She had known since her first week, and when Devan asked why she took the turn on the eleventh step of a stair she had said *I don't know*, and her thumbnail had dragged along her finger while she said it. It had been a small thing then, a quirk, the sort a person keeps like a pebble in a pocket. She had blamed Raiu afterward, because a dead boy was a reason. But she had walked wide before he died.

Janu did it too. Janu, who had found Asha at the foot of that stair. It was not a quirk if two women walked the same curve.

Her thumbnail began to drag. She made it stop, and she crossed to the stair.

The newel stood at the foot with its shut black eye. She stopped beside it. Up close she smelled wet laterite and old wax, and the pala rail was cold under her palm. She did not mean to learn anything. She meant to put her foot on each step in turn and prove it was only a stair. *One. Two.* The steps were worn into dips by a century of boots. At nine her breath began to go short, and she let it, and counted. *Ten.*

She knew the eleventh. She had known it for weeks: a hollow, dull note, like a struck cup with water in it. She put her heel down on it anyway, on purpose, to hear it. It rang.

Her stomach turned over. That was new, too. A sound she had filed away as nothing had no business doing that.

She went down on one knee, and then on her elbow, to keep the scab off the stone. The stair met the wall there, and a slot ran along the riser, black and narrow. Beyond it, low in the stone, was an iron grate. She had seen it before and taken it for a drain.

She looked at the bolts.

The wall around them was black with age and the laterite had gone soft at the edges, but the bolt heads were clean and sharp, and they still showed the file. No bloom of rust. Only a skin of damp. Not new that spring, but newer than the stair. Someone had pulled out whatever stood here before and put this in, and not long ago.

For what? A drain that had blocked. Any fort had a hundred drains. She looked at the bolts and told herself so again.

She stood, because her knee had begun to shake, and looked up the stair. The steps rose into the dark above the torch, and the rail went with them, and her legs refused. She had never refused a stair in her life. She'd gone up a well shaft with a knife in her teeth.

She went down backward, one step and then another, with her good hand on the rail. At the bottom she turned to cross the court.

Her feet swung wide. She watched them do it, an arm and a half out from the newel, the same long curve Janu made. She hadn't asked them to.

"It's a stair," she said aloud.

The torch hissed. The chain clanked.

Neela turned her back on it and walked round to the east side of the court, to the narrow stair cut into the wall, and took the long way up. The wall-walk, the cold turn under the lamp niche, the long passage round the upper court. She counted it all. Eighty-one steps, against the forty-four she would have taken, and she did not slow on any of them.

At the chamber door she stood with her hand on the latch. The grate had not touched her. The stair had not touched her. She was certain of it.

Yet the last time, it had only been a habit she couldn't explain. Tonight it had a file mark on it.

So why did her knees remember?
