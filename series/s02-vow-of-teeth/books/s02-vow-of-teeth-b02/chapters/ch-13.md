# Chapter 13: The Wood Road

The rain broke at the lower ford, and Devan chose that moment to tell her the odds.

"Three in ten," he said, "if the hounds don't quiet."

Neela kept her eyes on the water. It ran brown and fast over the stones, and Bhaskaran's men were taking their horses across it one at a time, calling low to each other. Steam rose off the horses. Behind them the wood road climbed away into wet green dark. It was the first dry hour in three days, and she could not enjoy it.

"Three in ten that I come out," she said.

"That you come out unseen. Alive I put higher." He said it as flatly as a man reading a tally. "I don't like either number."

"I like them less than you do. I'm the one going in."

"Then I'll stop giving you them."

"No." She turned her mare's head. "Keep giving them. I'd rather hear the number than have you hold it back and be kind."

His mouth moved. He swung down and she did the same, and they led the horses to the bank where the men could not hear. Eight men, and she had their names by the first mile. Vasu, whose mother kept ducks at Kalladi. Sankaran. Kuttan, who could not write, and rode closest and grinned each time she looked back, because she had written his mother a line. Bhaskaran led them with his four daughters on his mind and a face like a shut gate.

At the padippura that morning Muthu had held the gate log against his chest and laughed too loudly at nothing. "Mind the wall, lady," he had said. Then he had gone quiet, the way he did, and said to Devan, "I'll keep the fort. And the justice in it." Devan had put a hand on his shoulder and said nothing. Neela had watched Muthu's hands. They had been steady, and she had not known what to make of that.

Devan took a stick and drew in the mud of the bank. A square for the compound. A line for the east wall. A dot for the well house.

"Say it as a battle," he said. "Not as a promise."

She crouched. "Eight hunters on the walls. Two at the drain, four under the wall, two at the gate. Hounds that know every boot in the yard. I go in as the other one. I need the hounds quiet, the drain open and the bar lifted. Nothing else."

"And if one hunter looks down?"

"Then I'm a beast in the yard, and he has a pole and a net."

"And a spear." He tapped the dot. "Here is the part I don't like. Anything that opens on you opens on me. A spear in your flank is a spear in mine, a hand's breadth lower, wherever I'm standing. If I'm in that yard with a blade out, and you take a hit, I go down with you. Two bodies for one mistake."

"Then you don't go in the yard."

"No." He said it like a man setting down something heavy. "I stay on the ridge with the lamp men. Bhaskaran's shuttered lanterns, his bows. I hold the signal. I come over the wall for the shaft and nothing else, and when the shaft is done I go back out the way I came." He stopped. "I don't carry a sword into the one fight that matters."

"It's the only way it works."

"I know it is." He looked at the stick as if it had insulted him. "I've commanded men into walls for five years. I have never once sat above a wall while someone else went over it. I hate it, Neela."

"I know."

"I'll do it."

She did not say thank you. It would have been a small word for what it cost him, and he would have left the bank to avoid it. She said, "Then wrap your palms for the rope. Twice. A rope burn opens on me."

"I'd thought of that." He drew a short line beside the well. "And a cut on your pads opens on my soles. I'll walk like a man in new boots tomorrow."

"You'll limp. The men will think I beat you."

"They've thought it for weeks."

She laughed, and so did he, and Kuttan across the ford waved at them as if they had called him. Then the clouds came back over the trees, and the first fat drop struck the map and spoiled the compound.

* * *

By dusk they were on the ridge.

The wood stopped at a crest of black rock, and the Resin House lay in the valley below, a square of dark with eight small lights along its walls. She had grown up inside that square. From here it looked no bigger than a tray. The wind came up the slope, and in it she smelled scorched tamarind and pine sap, so strongly that her wrists began to ache before the resin touched them.

Devan had put Bhaskaran's men three hundred paces back, at a cairn on the road, with their shuttered lanterns and an order that nobody climbed above the stones. Bhaskaran had asked no questions. He had looked at the warden's wife, and then at his own boots, and gone to see to the horses. Neela had noted that he looked at his boots. She would remember it later, when she had time to be grateful.

The two of them took a hollow in the rock, where a pit of coals gave out a dull red and no light. Devan laid a flat river stone beside it. Next to the stone he put a folded cloth and a leaf packet that smelled of cold rice and curd.

She watched him do it. "I never asked for the stone."

"No. You came back cold at the pass, and you came back hungry in the yard." He did not look up. "A stone's a small thing."

The last of the light went off the rim of the valley. The burn woke in her throat, as it did whenever the sun was gone, and she took the flask from her pouch and held it a moment.

"Sit where you can see my face," she said.

He sat on the rock across the coals. He had watched her turn more than ten times, and she knew it, and he knew she knew. But he had always lain still with his breath too even and his eyes a crack open. She had been a shadow at a window.

"I'm not asking you to pretend," she said. "Look. All of it."

"I never looked away," Devan said. "I only pretended I was."

"That is the least comforting thing you have ever told me."

"It's true."

"I know. That's why." She pulled the cork. In the yard at the Resin House she had turned because she had no time left and a man's life was in the mud. That had been a leap. This was a door, and she was holding it open on purpose. She had never done that for anyone, and her hands were not entirely steady.

The resin was cold and black, and it smelled of every dusk of her life. She drew it across her throat first, and then each wrist, and it bit through the old scars like a hot wire.

"Neela," Devan said quietly.

"Don't help. Just stay."

The burn climbed into her jaw and down her spine, and the pull began, the sense of every bone being untied. She did not look away from him. His face stayed on hers. He did not glance down at what was happening to her body, and he did not look as if he were watching a marvel or a horror. He looked like a man receiving something that had been handed to him.

Her hands left her. The ground came up. The valley floor opened below her like a lit book, with every lamp-wick in the walls and every hound's breath. His smell struck her whole: sweat and wet wool, horse, iron, and underneath it the thing that was only him.

Through all of it his face held, turned toward hers.

"Evening," Devan said, to the other one.

She could not answer. She put her head down and pushed it, once, against his knee.

* * *

She kept the edges.

It was what the animal did with a strange place, and she let it. Round and round the hollow, soft-footed on the wet rock, past the horses' scent, which tasted of meat to her and which she took care to keep downwind of. She marked every cleft where a man could stand. She counted breaths as she went.

Devan had settled his back against the stone. He sat with his hands loose between his knees.

"I'll talk," he said. "If you like. You said once the worst of the shape is that nobody speaks to you."

She had said it on the second night, in the dark, and had not thought he was listening. She slowed and came round the coals on her next circuit, and he took that for yes.

"Pepper ate the last of the wedding marigolds," he said. "Off the garland, off Kannan's neck. Kannan argued before the whole kitchen that she had been invited."

The other one's ears went flat with something that in a woman would have been a laugh.

"Janu said the goat had better manners than the bridegroom. She was right." His voice stayed low, steady, and pitched under the wind. It held the camp like a rope held a cart. "Asha drew parrots on the cistern wall when she was seven. Janu scrubbed them off. She drew them back. It went on two years. Janu says now that she let the last one stay a month before she scrubbed it, but she's lying."

Round the coals, past the stone, back. Each word fell where she could find it.

"She drew the newel at the west stair with a whole beak. Mended. She said a broken beak insulted the bird." A short pause. "I don't know why I tell you that."

The other one's pelt crawled along her back, and she put it down to the cold and kept walking. It was a wet night. Her nails would have been aching if she had had nails.

He did not ask her anything, and he did not wait for her to answer. When she came round the fourth time his voice slowed. When she came round the fifth, it stopped, and she looked up.

He had put his hand flat against his side, low on the ribs. The old seam, the white one. He was breathing in short, careful counts.

She stopped. Thirty years of rain in the hills had put that damp into him, and a long ride had worked it. He did not say a word about it. He never did.

She crossed the hollow and lay down along his side, spine against his ribs, as heavily as she could. The heat of her went into him.

Devan went still. "May I?" he said, and when she did not move he laid his hand on her shoulder, and then buried his fingers in the pelt. His breath shook once and steadied.

They stayed like that. Nothing in the valley below changed. The coals ticked. Her side rose and fell against his, and after a time she felt his ribs ease under her, one hard breath and then a long one. He leaned his head back on the rock.

"It's gone quiet," he said.

She did not move. She had asked a man once to stay with her through dusk, all the way to the light, and he had. The night had its cost. Tomorrow's hunger would take her at dawn, and the ache was still waiting in her nails. She counted what she was spending, and she spent it.

Below them, a hound complained in its sleep. Far off on the wall, a lamp moved and was still.

She got up.

Devan did not try to stop her. He drew his hand out of her fur one finger at a time and set it on his knee. "Two sawing calls," he said. "A count apart, when the drain is open and the bar is up. I move on the second. Not before."

She turned her head and looked at him.

"I'll sit here," he said. "I'll count a thousand, then stand. I'll be at the rope when you call, and not one step ahead of you." His voice did not lift. "I'd follow you down. I want you to know I would."

She could not tell him that she knew. She went to the lip of the hollow instead, where the dark began, and looked back once. He was a shape against the red of the coals with his hands open on his knees. Behind him, three hundred paces off, Bhaskaran's lanterns stayed shuttered.

Then she went down the slope.

The rock gave way to wet bracken and then to thorn, and the wall rose out of the dark with its eight lamps. The wind brought her the compound whole: tamarind scorch, hound, lamp oil, old cooking fat. A trace of musk in it, two days old and heavy. The hounds were quiet. They were quieter than they should have been.

She went on, low and alone, toward the wall, and the lights did not move.
