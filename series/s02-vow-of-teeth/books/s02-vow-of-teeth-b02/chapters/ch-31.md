# Chapter 31: The Third Slat

The third slat creaked when he pressed it, as it always had.

Devan stood at the west shutters on the last night with the lamp turned low and his thumb against the teak. Nobody had asked him to fix it. He had never wanted to. His father had said a room should breathe, and Asha had said a room should talk. Then a woman had slipped the latch on her wedding night, pressed this slat to see whether the sound would wake him, and gone out over the sill. He had lain with his eyes shut and counted her feet.

He slid the housing aside. Behind it, in the hollow of the frame, lay an oilcloth bundle tied with red thread.

The bundle came out cold from the wall. Devan sat on the bed to untie it. The buyer's half of Chathu's chit was a hide strip, narrow as a finger, with the warden's chamber and *death by beast* cut into it. The payment writ lay under it, folded in thirds. The green wax had a chip at the left wing tip. He turned it to the lamp, and the chip made the sunbird look mid-wingbeat, or a little hurt. Last came the cut seal from the palace betel set, which he had carried in his sleeve for weeks and then stopped being able to look at.

Three things that said the same sentence. His queen had paid for his death, and she had been courteous about it.

"You could mend it," Neela said from the door.

She had come in without a sound, as she always did. She wore the day kasavu with the torn border, and her hair was wet from the court. One hand rested on the frame. She wasn't counting. He could tell by her lips.

"No."

"It gave me away every night."

"It gave *you* nothing," he said. "It told me where you were." He wrapped the bundle again and tied the thread in the knot he had used before. "I never fixed it. I'm glad."

She almost laughed. It showed in her shoulders first. He put the bundle in his pack, beside the quire stitched round in green thread and still sealed. Neela thought it held the queen's own contracts with the Resin House, and perhaps the answer to the small folded-wing birds inked beside the cut stubs in the back half. Whether it was ever opened was theirs to choose.

Neela crossed the floor and set her palm over the slat, pressing. It creaked. She lifted her hand and the room was quiet again.

"Janu wants us in the kitchen," she said. "She says she has a bag."

"She has several."

"She says a bag."

* * *

Janu had five.

They stood in a row on the kitchen table, tied at the neck with cord: rice cakes in banana leaf, jaggery, dried fish wrapped three times against the damp, a twist of salt, a smaller twist of pepper. Someone had stood a copper pot at the end of the row. Chinna leaned in the back doorway with her bad hip against the frame and her arms folded, running a wager with herself on how many bags Devan would refuse.

"We're riding," he said. "Not marching."

"Then you'll be riding fed." Janu did not look up from the cord she was knotting. "Sit."

"Janu."

"Sit, boy."

He sat. Neela, beside him on the bench, pressed her mouth very flat, which was how she hid a grin. The rain walked on the roof tiles. The cistern chain clanked once.

Janu finished the last knot and stayed standing with her hands flat on the table. Devan had seen her stand that way a thousand times, usually before the words *not in my kitchen*. Tonight she looked at Neela instead.

"I told leopard stories at this table for forty years," she said. "To a boy who liked them, and then to a girl who didn't."

"I liked them," Neela said.

"You went white." Janu's chin came up. "I'm old, not blind. I thought it was the dark. Then my boy here said he was tired of leopards, and I took offence, and I never told the end."

"That was my doing," Devan said.

Janu turned.

"I said it so the kitchen would stop looking at the window." He heard how it sounded in the room, a grown man owning to a story. "Every servant who heard that tale at supper was one step from wondering why the bride's shutters were barred. I wanted their eyes off her. I lied to you in your own kitchen. I'm sorry for it."

"You were never any good at it," Janu said. Her mouth worked. "You could have asked me to stop. I'd have stopped."

"You'd have asked why."

"I would." She sniffed. "I'd have been told, too, in the end. I always am."

Nobody spoke. Chinna had unfolded her arms.

"You always stopped in the same place," Devan said. "The leopard at the edge of the hills. Tell it now."

Janu looked at him a long moment. Then she drew a breath, and he saw her go back forty years.

"There was a leopard who came down off the hill in a dry year. The village hunted her out. She went far, into forests with no names, and she grew thin, and she ate what she could. Every night she came back to the edge of the hills and looked down." Her voice dropped. "They kept the door open, and a lamp lit. And one night she walked in. That's all. It's a child's end. I always thought it foolish."

Devan did not answer. He looked at the table. Beneath it, Neela's hand found his and held on.

Janu came round the table and took his face between her hands, which she had not done since he was twelve, and kissed his forehead hard enough to hurt. She did the same to Neela, who sat quite still and took it.

"Eat," Janu said. "Both of you. There's more than you can carry, and you'll carry it."

Chinna pushed off the door frame. "Nobody won the shutters wager," she said. "I'm keeping the stakes." Neela laughed out loud, and so, after a moment, did he.

* * *

At sunset he came up the stair alone and sat on the bed with his hands between his knees.

The packs stood by the door. The shutters were barred, as they had been every dusk for months, and his hand still knew the weight of that bar. He did not know what he was waiting for. Neela found him there.

"Are you going to sit in the dark?" She shut the door with her heel.

"I was thinking."

"Did it help?"

"No."

She crossed to the shutters. He thought she meant to check the bar out of habit, all those months of it, and so did she, for her hand went to it. Then she stopped. She took her hand away, lifted the bar, drew the latch and folded both leaves open, wide, onto the failing light.

Rain came in on the wind. It smelled of wet red laterite and, from below, of the lantana banks, which she hated and which tonight she breathed straight in. The hills lay copper under the cloud where the sun went down behind them.

"Rain," Devan said.

She turned. Her face did something he had no word for, a slow thing, a door going back on its hinge. "You said that under a clear sky."

"I did."

"It's actual rain this time."

"I thought I'd get it right for once."

Then the last of the light slid off the hills, and her hand went to her throat.

He was on his feet before he knew it. She held up the other hand, flat. *Wait.* Her scars stood out pale against her skin, and her jaw was set against a burn he could only guess at. She breathed in. Out. In again, long, the way one breathes through a cramp. It took ten counts, and he counted them with her and did nothing, because she had asked.

The pull let go. She dropped her hand and stood human in the open window.

"That's the dusk," she said. "It comes every night I leave the resin off, and it passes, and the ache counts every one. I've nothing to ration on the road. The forty have the season's flasks, mine with them, and I told them they were theirs. So tonight's paid for out of the next moon, and I'll feel the bill." Her chin lifted. "I chose it. Say you heard me."

"I heard you."

"Say yes if you want me. Not because I asked."

"Yes," he said. "Because I want you."

She came to him across the boards. She was twenty-seven, he thirty-two, and they were married by two vows and a writ, and neither had to say any of it. She took his face in both hands as Janu had and kissed him slowly, then not slowly at all.

They went to the bed without hurry. He undid the kasavu at her shoulder, the torn gold border she had mended herself with stiff, careful stitches. She found the buckle of his belt and fumbled it, and he laughed into her hair. She said, "Don't," and he said, "I'm not," and he was. Her scars lay pale and ridged along her wrists, and he turned her hands over and kissed each one, throat last. She shut her eyes. His mouth caught her pulse jumping.

When he lifted his head she had opened them, and she bit him on the shoulder, hard enough to leave a welt. He hissed. Her hand flew to her own shoulder, where the same red mark was rising.

"So that's the price of biting you," she said.

"You bit yourself through me," Devan said.

They lay a moment staring at each other in the lamplight and began, helplessly, to laugh again.

After that there wasn't much laughing. The rain came in over the sill, and neither of them reached to close anything. She took him in her hands, and then in her mouth, and for a while he forgot the room entirely. When she moved over him in the lamp's dim gold he held her hips and let her set the pace, as he had learned she wished, and watched her face. She kept her eyes open. That was the thing. In all those months, in all the rooms with their shut windows, she had never kept them open. She looked at him the whole way down, and he said her name, the one she had chosen, and then the older one she was still trying on. She shook when she heard it.

He turned them gently when she asked, and was careful of her calf and of his own. He touched her until she said *there*, and *there*, and then said nothing. Afterwards she lay over his chest with her breath running ragged and her hair across his mouth.

"Terms," she said into his collarbone, after a time.

"Terms?"

"I've had two sets. The guild's and yours." She lifted her head. Her eyes were very clear. "Neither fits any more. You're not the warden now, and I'm not a bride. I came to this bed sold. I'd like to leave it free."

He thought about it with her weight on him. He didn't hurry; she had never wanted him to.

"No one leads," he said. "Or both do. If you say stop, we stop. If I say it, we stop, and neither asks why until we've stopped." He found her hand. "I won't carry things for you alone. You won't carry things for me alone. We tell each other what we're doing before we do it."

"Even if it's stupid."

"Especially."

"And at the back." Her fingers tightened. "You've slept with a wall behind you since the stair. I want to be the one behind you. Not the wall. Someone who could strike and doesn't."

His throat closed. He had told her that, once, in the dark, in a bad hour. He had not known she was keeping it.

"And you," he said, when he could. "Whoever comes at you comes through me. I'll know it's yours to answer and I won't take it. But I'll be there."

"Partners," Neela said, testing the word.

"If it's a good one."

"It's a terrible word." Her mouth shook. "I'll keep it."

* * *

The rain slackened toward the middle watch. He lay on his back and she lay along his side, and neither slept.

"Count me," she said.

He turned his head.

"You've counted me in your head every night." Her voice was low. "I've heard you. Your breath goes slow and square. I always knew. Tonight I want you to. Out loud, if you like."

So he did. He put his hand on her ribs and counted her breaths under his palm, in the dark with the shutters open, softly, like a man laying stones across a stream. *One. Two. Three.* She breathed in and out, long and even.

At nine, the breath caught.

Small. A skipped step. He waited, and on the next long count it came again at the same place.

"Neela."

"What?"

"You have a hitch."

She was quiet. "I don't."

"Yours falls on the ninth count. Mine falls on the third part of mine. You catch it like a woman missing the eleventh step."

She turned her face into his shoulder, and he felt her smile there, wet. "I've been trying to count it out of mine for twenty years," she said. "Nobody told me."

"Nobody was listening."

"No." Her hand lay on his heart. "You were."

* * *

He woke to numbers.

Grey light came through the open shutters, thin as watered milk. The rain had dwindled to a drip from the eaves. Somewhere below, the cistern chain clanked once, and Gopan's rhythm held.

She wasn't asleep. She lay on her side facing him with her chin on her arm, and her lips moved. *Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen.* Her eyes were on his chest.

He did not move. He kept his breath where it was. Slow, square, and at the third part of the count, the small catch he had never been able to cure.

"There," Neela whispered. "There it is."

"Do you always do that?"

"Every morning. It means you're still here." She did not look away from his ribs. "I never told you why."

"You've told me now."

She nodded. Her lashes were wet. Then her eyes lifted, and the fear in them went out like a lamp.

Below the window, hooves sounded on the wet road, many of them, and a horse blew. Then Bhaskaran's voice came up from the padippura, pitched low, the way a man speaks when he doesn't want to wake a house. Devan sat up and put his feet to the cold floor.

The gate stood open on the rain. Beyond it in the red road sat forty riders on forty borrowed horses, with no lamps, in the grey. Not one looked up at the window. They had learned to wait, and they waited with their hoods down for the two people who were coming.

He reached for his pack. The green-stitched quire lay at the top, the wax still whole. In the grey light he saw what the lamp had hidden. Pressed faint into the thread where it crossed the wax, the way a thumb leaves a print, was a small bird with its wings folded. The queen's sunbird spread its wings. This one had been put there by someone else's hand, or the same hand on another day.

Breaking the queen's wax was treason in law. He turned the quire over and the print was there on the other edge too.

"Neela," he said.

She came and looked, and said nothing, and put her thumb beside the mark without touching it.

He shouldered the pack. Together they went down the stair toward the forty and the rain.
