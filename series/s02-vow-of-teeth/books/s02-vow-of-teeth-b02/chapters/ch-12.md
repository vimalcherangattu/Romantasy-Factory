# Chapter 12: The Bargain in the Dark

Devan dropped the bar across the shutters himself, and the third slat creaked under it, and Neela's nails itched all the way down to the quick.

It was the hour before the burn. Outside, the sky over the pass had gone the colour of old brass. She stood in the middle of the warden's chamber with her bandaged hand against her chest and counted what she could see. The shutters. The stair door, with Devan's back to it. The bed, the sword on the boards with its hilt toward her side, the kindi full, its spout toward the pillow.

On the sill lay two pebbles, the grey one and Kannan's white one. Neither would be used tonight.

"You said all the way to the light," Devan said.

"I did."

"You'll pay for it."

"I'll pay for it in the morning, and on the wall." She flexed her fingers. Two nails had split in the night. "I've looked at the bill. I want this more than I want to be comfortable."

He crossed the room slowly, as he always did when he thought a thing might spook. He stopped a hand's width from her. He was thirty-two and had not lain with anyone for eight years. She was twenty-seven and had been touched by many people and let herself be known by one. Neither of them pretended it was a small thing.

"Tell me what you want," he said.

"That's my line."

"Then say it."

"Sit," said Neela.

He sat on the edge of the bed, looking up at her, and his stillness had a different weight than the pit-stillness she knew. She unbound the bandage from her left hand because it was in the way. She laid it over the bedpost. Then she stood between his knees and took his face in her hands, scab and all, and kissed him.

It was nothing like the rampart. On the rampart she had been a clock running down, one eye on the light. Now there was no light to watch. His mouth was slow under hers, and when she opened it he made a sound low in his chest, and his hands came to her waist and stopped there, flat and waiting.

"Is this all right?" he said against her jaw.

"Yes. Is it for you?"

"Yes." His thumbs moved once over the cloth. "Tell me if it isn't."

"I will. You do the same."

"I always do."

That made her laugh into his neck, and he laughed too, and the last of her wariness went out through it.

She undid his shirt herself. She learned him the way she learned a house: the seam of white at his ribs, puckered and old, the brow scar she could feel with her lips, the long muscles of his back that flinched when she touched a place she had not known was tender. He let her find each of them. When she pushed him back on the quilt he went, and watched her over his shoulder with his eyes gone dark and very steady.

When she had nothing on but the bangle, she stood a moment longer than she needed. He looked at the pale ridged scars at her throat and the layered ones on her wrists. He didn't look away from them, and he didn't look at them as a thing to be pitied.

"May I?" He lifted one hand, palm up, and stopped before he touched.

"Yes."

His fingers rested on her throat, on the old burn, as lightly as a man testing whether a bowl is hot. Then his mouth. She felt the heat of it travel down through her, and she shut her eyes and let it.

She took her time. She straddled him and set the pace, and when it was too fast, he said her name, and she slowed. When it was not enough, she said his, and he moved his hands to her hips and followed where she led. Once she caught his wrist against the quilt, and he went still at once, and she said, "No, keep going, I only wanted to feel your pulse." He turned his hand over and let her.

She had been a woman with a number and a leopard's weight on her back for most of her life. In this room she was neither. She was a body that wanted, with a man who asked, and the asking made her bolder than any command had ever done. She felt it build in her, hot and slow. When it broke she heard her own voice go high and cracked, and under her Devan said her name like something he'd been carrying uphill, and held on.

Dusk came and went in the middle of it.

She felt it as a pull at the throat, a faint burn in the scars, as if a thread had been tugged. She stiffened on him. His hands tightened.

"Neela."

"It's the dusk." She breathed through it, the way she always did. "It's nothing. There's no resin on me, it will pass."

It passed. Her skin cooled, and her shoulders came down, and she was only a woman again, breathing hard into the crook of his neck. On the sill, the two pebbles lay where they were.

"Nothing to hold in my cheek," Devan said into her hair, wondering.

"Nothing to hold in your cheek."

* * *

Later the lamp had burned low, and the dark rule came back into the room, as it did every night. In the dark you could ask anything. He answered, or he said *I won't answer that*. She lay on her back with her head on his arm, her bandage still on the bedpost, a sheet across her hips. Her nails ached. She did not mind.

"I want to make a bargain," she said.

"I thought we had one."

"We have a marriage. This is a clause." She turned her face to him. "Tomorrow night you'll hold forty lives. You said it yourself. You're afraid of your own hands."

"I am."

"I'm afraid of mine." She said it plainly, as she'd learned to. "I'd read mine first, my birth name and that slip. I said so. If I hold that book with my name in it, I don't trust myself either."

"So."

"So whoever holds it, and begins to read a page at night, for any reason, the other takes it from their hand." She pushed up on her elbow. "No argument. No *wait, I've a good cause*. The reason's always good. The one holding it lets go."

He was quiet. She could see the line of his throat in the last of the lamp.

"I thought of that," he said. "When I said you'd stop me. I didn't think of the other way round."

"I did." She said it before she could soften it. "I'd rather be stopped by you than by anyone. And I'd rather you took it from me when I asked you to than let me be right."

"What if you're right?"

"Then I can be right in the morning." Her mouth twisted. "By daylight. In front of the one whose page it is. That's the rule for the loophole anyway."

"By daylight, aloud, in full." He nodded slowly against the pillow. "Night's for letting go, then."

"Night's for letting go."

"And if I won't?"

"Then it's not a bargain." She held his gaze. "It's a thing I said. I'd have to leave you the knife, Devan, and I gave you the knife already."

He turned his head and kissed the inside of her wrist, over the scars. "Then I'll let go," he said. "I give you my word, and I'll say it twice, so you can hold me to it. If I begin to read a page at night, you take it. If you begin, I take it. Neither of us says why."

"I give mine," said Neela. "Twice."

She had expected to feel the cost of it as a loss. Instead it felt like a joint, the place where two timbers meet and take a weight neither could hold alone. She settled her head back on his arm.

The wound-share ran between them like a wire in the dark. Each of them had made the other a witness and a wound. Now each had made the other a lock, too.

* * *

"Now my question," Devan said.

The lamp had guttered. Somewhere on the wall, the chain clanked once for the middle watch. She had known he would ask. She had told him at the foot of the sealing that there were eleven before the twelfth, and he might have them one at a time, if he asked.

"One of your eleven," he said. "I'll take them one at a time, as you said. Not the worst. I don't want the worst. The first."

She was silent so long he began to say, "You don't have to," and she cut across him.

"I do. It's the rule." She stared at the dark beam over the bed. "And it's mine to give."

She counted three breaths, as she did before a drop.

"I was nineteen. Chathu called it the first proper work. Before that there'd been errands, a lookout's turn, a carrying. Never the knife." Her voice had gone flat. She let it. "A man named Raghu. He collected for a port enforcer in Kodiyur, a man who ran the quays behind the pepper warehouses. Raghu went to the houses of the ones who couldn't pay. He had a stick."

Devan said nothing.

"A boatman's son had stood in his way. Eleven years old. He lost the use of his arm. The boatman wrote to someone, and the someone wrote to the Resin House, and Chathu read the leaf out to me across his table with a tin of sesame sweets between us. A small leaf, in cipher. A little hooked mark in the corner, which he said was for the accounts." She swallowed. "I was given a night, an alley, and a number of paces."

"And you did it."

"I did it. With my hands, at the end of a lane behind the fish market. A strike here." She touched the base of her own skull, a thumb's width below. "The old masters call it the door. He fell the way you fall when you're drunk, and I walked away, and nobody ran. Nobody saw. It was very clean. I was sick in a drain two streets away, and then I went back and ate."

"Ate."

"I was hungry. It was the first thing I learned about myself. I'd been told for years I couldn't kill, and then I could, and then I was hungry." She turned her head. "He had a stick, Devan, and a boy with a ruined arm. I've told myself that for eight years. It's true. It doesn't make my hand any lighter."

She heard him move. His arm came across her, warm and heavy, and stayed. He did not say it was all right. She had braced for that, and was so grateful it hadn't come that her eyes stung.

"A man with a stick, and a boy with his arm," Devan said at last. "I'd have given you that one."

"Would you?"

"I'd have wanted to." He said it carefully, the way he would lay a hand on a hurt horse. "I know I'd want to. That's not the same as having a right to." His thumb moved against her ribs. "Thank you for telling it plain."

"It's the only way I know."

"I know." A pause. "Is there a second?"

Her stomach turned over. There was. She felt it at once, a long way down, a door at the end of a passage: a hill fort, a blindfold, a stair she couldn't see. She had told him she had a lookout's turn and no more. She did not know the fort.

He had asked the question lightly. He had already moved on to the next thing as he spoke, she thought, the way a man steps back from a cliff edge he has only just noticed.

"There is," she said. "I can tell it. If you ask."

The dark held. She heard him breathe, in and out, and the small hitch a third of the way along the count.

"I won't ask tonight," Devan said. "After the book."

"Why?"

"Because tomorrow I'll hold a book beside you, and I want to hold it with both hands." He drew her closer. "After. Whatever it is. I'll hear it then."

"All right," said Neela, and the word was nearly steady.

He slept, in time, the way he slept now: one arm across her, the wall at his back, and his breath going long and even, with its hitch a third of the way along. The lamp went out. Not a sound came from the passage but the draught by the stair.

She lay awake.

She counted the doors, because it was what she did. The stair door, with the bar he had set himself. The shutters, barred. The little door in the wall beside the hearth, which had no use in this house that she had found. Then, because the habit would not let her stop, she counted a fourth, the one she had no business counting. Beside her, an arm's length off, a man asleep.

Ten more to tell. She turned them over like coins in her pocket, trying to remember the faces. A magistrate with a bad knee. Two sisters in a rich house on the coast. A boy on a river barge, and the man who had paid to have him gone. And a stair, somewhere. A stair with a blindfold on.

She did not know which one he would hate.

She did not know which one she would hate herself for, in his eyes. That was a different sum, and a worse one. The chain clanked the hour. Her nails ached in the dark. Beside her Devan breathed, and hitched, and breathed, and she lay with her eyes open and counted them until the shutters began to grey.
