# Chapter 14: The Shuttered Well

The drain path had been bigger when she was nine.

The other one did not mind. She flattened her ribs to the wet stone and slid, shoulders first, into the culvert behind the midden. The wall's footing had washed out there before she was born. Water ran through it, and so had two girls who were told every day they had no use for dogs. Ammu had gone first, always, humming the fishing song under her breath. Neela had gone second and counted.

She counted now. Eleven body-lengths of cold slime and root. Then the grate at the far end. It was shut with an iron pin on a short chain, and she took the pin in her teeth and drew it out as slowly as a splinter. The grate swung. She was in the yard of the Resin House, and the whole place smelled of her childhood.

Scorched tamarind. Pine sap. Cold cooking fat. Over all of it lay the bear.

The musk was two days old, thick and sour, and it lay on the yard like a hand. It explained the hounds. They were down in their straw by the kitchen wall with their chins flat and their eyes rolled white, and not one lifted its head. They knew exactly who had walked here.

She set the pin on the stone, where Devan would feel it with his boot. She left the grate ajar. The first thing was done.

Eight lamps burned on the wall. She counted them, and the men who walked beneath them. Two hunters at the gate, their backs to her. Two on the drain side, looking out. Four under the wall. None of them looked in. Nobody had looked in at this yard for twenty years, because everything inside it belonged to Chathu.

She took the flagged path along the kennel roof, hugging the shadow of the eaves.

The wind came through the slats, and she smelled the cages before she saw them. There were forty-two in a row, rank with the sour sweetness of animals that had stopped being afraid. A deer breathed in the first one. A hare. Something that had been a mongoose and now only paced. She did not turn her head. She had told herself on the ridge that she would not.

She turned it for the fourth from the end.

It was the only cage with no breath in it. The door stood shut and the straw lay pressed flat in one long hollow, the length of a woman curled on her side. Scratches ran down the iron at the height of her own shoulder. Forty-one of these cages held someone. This one held the shape of her, three weeks cold, kept for the day she was brought back.

*Nobody swept it,* she thought. *They were saving it.*

She went on, and her stride broke at the third cage from the end.

Sandal smoke, long gone, and under it fish oil, and a girl who had fallen asleep over a slate. A civet lay in the straw with its small masked face on its paws. It lifted its head.

The lamp on the wall caught something at its hind leg, a thin line of silver. An anklet. The bells had been cut off years ago.

The other one stopped. The woman under her fur did not.

*Kirri.* Kirri, who had been the big girl at the long table and taught the little ones their letters, and could not spell tide. Who had sworn to send that anklet home in a fish basket the day she had a boat.

The civet looked at her. It was a long, mild, unfocused look, the look of an animal at a smell it half-remembered. Then the head sank onto the paws again. There was no one in the eyes to sink.

Neela could not speak. She had no hands. She could not put a cloth over the bars or a name in the air. She stood there a count of three, which was all she could spare, and then she did the only thing the shape allowed. She went close and breathed out through the slats, once, hard, so that Kirri would have her scent on the wind and no stranger's.

*After,* she told her, with nothing at all. *After the book.*

She went on down the path with her ears flat. She did not look back. A hunter looks back.

* * *

The well house stood at the path's end, four posts and old tile, shuttered. The door was plank. The teak bar lay in its two brackets, and over it the hasp hung on its staple with a farm lock through it, shut.

She could not pick a lock. She had no fingers for it.

But the post that held the staple was older than she was. She had pressed her thumb into that wood at eleven, on a dare, and it had come away damp. She hooked one claw behind the staple and eased it. The wood gave with a small sound like a sigh. The staple, lock and all, came out of the post and hung from the hasp. She lowered it to the stone by its weight and did not let it clank. Then she took the bar's end in her teeth, rolled it from one bracket, and let it down onto her shoulders.

The door opened inward on a smell like a held breath.

Inside was a ring of cold stone. The shaft opened in the middle of it, a black mouth with a curb around it. Over it ran a beam with an iron pulley, and a chain ran over the pulley and down. She put her forepaws on the curb and looked.

Far below, a few man-lengths down, a ledge had been cut into the shaft wall, dry and square, well above the black shine of the water. The chain ran down to it. Someone had lowered a box onto the stone and left the links slack. It was sealed all round, with a padlock the size of a fist. From inside, thin and bitter, came the scent of resin-ink.

The black book.

She was so close that she could have wept, if the shape had allowed it. She could reach the chain with her teeth. She could not haul a box that weighed as much as a child up a wet shaft with only her jaws. And she could not open that padlock with a boulder.

*Paws can't open it.* She had known that on the ridge, and Devan had known it, and the plan had stood on it. She had a part and he had a part, and the box lay between them like a hinge.

She lifted her head over the curb and gave the call.

It came out of her throat like a saw drawn once through green wood, rasping and low. She counted to the length of a held breath. She gave it again.

Two. A count apart. The drain is open and the bar is up.

*Come.*

She went to the door to watch the yard, and light swung round the corner of the kennel roof.

A hunter, thin, with a lantern on a hooked pole. He walked the path the way a man walks a round he has walked a thousand times, looking at his feet. He was ten paces off. The light came across the flags toward her in a slow yellow arc.

"Hup," he said, to nobody, and then, "What's—"

She went at him low.

She had no time to be afraid for him. She put her shoulder into his knees and her weight behind it, and he went down backwards with a grunt, and the pole swung. The hook caught her along the ribs. It was a short, flat, burning blow, and it opened her skin in a hot wet line under the fur.

The lantern hit the flags and rolled. She had half a breath to know what that blow would mean. Somewhere over the wall a man in wrapped gloves had just felt the hook land on his own side, with no enemy in reach. She hoped he would not stop.

She drove a forepaw onto the hunter's chest. His head cracked on the stone. His mouth opened, and she took her paw off his throat at once, because she would not be the thing that killed him over a lamp.

He breathed. She smelled it: sour breath, a boy's sweat, no blood but a little. Out cold. She had not killed him, and she was grateful, in the sharp quick way she was grateful for any door that did not need to be shut behind her.

The lantern had rolled against the kennel wall. Its flame lay down along the wick and began to lick the stone, and a drift of dry straw beside it caught the edge of the light. She raked a pile of wet midden straw across it with her paw and pressed it flat. The flame hissed, smoked and went out.

Dark. She stood in it, shaking, with her ribs on fire.

Something moved on the low wall above her.

An otter sat there. Sleek, black-wet, whiskered, sitting up on her haunches the way they do, watching. It was Pavi. The white chin gave her away, and the way she held one forepaw curled, the old break that had never set. Pavi, who had come to the House at eight with a fever and a doll. Neela had braided her hair the night before her first turning, because Ammu was with the little ones that night and there was nobody else.

Pavi was twenty-four now, and on the night watch. In shape, she was the same as the dark rows along the wall that Neela had not understood until this instant. Neela had left a House where watch was kept by hunters. Someone had put the shapes on the walls.

*That is a thing to ask later.*

Pavi looked at the hunter on the stones. She looked at the dead lantern. She looked at the leopard.

Neela did not move. If Pavi gave the cry, the whole yard would wake. An otter has a thin high whistle that carries across a pond. She had heard it once, at nine, in the sheds.

Pavi turned her head away.

She turned it slowly, deliberately, toward the outer wall, and kept it there. Her small round back was a wall of its own. She did not look again.

*Thank you,* Neela told her back. It was a stupid, useless thing to say to someone who could not hear it. It was all she had.

She dragged the hunter by his collar into the shadow of the nearest post, where a passing lamp would see a heap of cloth and not a man. Her ribs sang with every pull. She propped the well-house door with her shoulder and stood in it.

The wall was dark above her.

Then, softly, she heard it. A scrape of cloth on laterite. A weight set down on stone, and a breath held and let go. Boots on the flags, going quickly, going quietly, a man who had wrapped his soles in sacking so that the stones would not ring.

Devan came round the kennel roof with a coil on his shoulder and a pry bar under his arm. His palms were wrapped to the wrist. His other hand was pressed hard against his side, and his shirt was dark and wet under his fingers, right over the old white seam.

He did not speak. A voice carries across a yard at night, and the nearest lamp was thirty paces off. His mouth was shut hard around it.

He saw the dark heap at the post. He saw her. His eyes went to her ribs. She drew a breath, and his face tightened with it, as if the breath were his. She let it out, and his shoulders dropped a half-inch with hers. His fingers shifted on the stain.

"Yours?" he said, almost without voice.

She dipped her head once.

"Then it's mine." He crouched, so that his face was level with hers, and put one wrapped palm briefly flat between her ears. It was warm, and he did not linger. "Good," Devan said. "Hold the door."

He went past her into the well house with the rope. She heard him loop it over the beam and test it, the small creak of the pulley and a breath. She heard him go over the curb. The chain clinked.

She stayed in the door and did what she was best at. She counted.

Four lamps she could see. The nearest hunter was thirty paces off, facing away. The hounds had not stirred. Pavi's round back still did not turn. Down in the shaft, the rope groaned, and then the groan changed as it took his full weight.

Seven breaths. The pry bar scraped on iron below her, and she heard him swear without sound.

Then the wind turned.

It came round the corner of the cook-house roof and across the kennels, and with it came the smell she had braced for since the ridge. It was not two days old now. It was fresh and rank and close, and it was walking.

A foot came down on the flags beyond the cook-house corner. Not the rhythm of a bear's stride. A drag, and a step. A drag, and a step, as one hind leg took the weight a half-beat late. New pain, a month old. But the weight behind it she had heard on the long-ago nights when she was nine and had pretended to be asleep.

The bear rounded the corner with its nose high.

He was bigger than the wall's shadow had a right to be. The shaggy black bulk of him blotted the lamp. The pale Y on his chest hung like a scar, and his small eyes were wet and sharp. He stopped. He swung his long muzzle from side to side and drew the wind in, in great wet breaths, and found the lantern smoke, and the hunter, and the otter, and her.

He knew her. She watched him know her. The head came round and settled, and the hind leg shifted, and the breath that came out of him was not a snarl. It was something close to satisfaction.

Behind her, in the shaft, the pry bar rang on iron. The rope creaked and swung. A man at the end of it, with his back to the sky, was working at a box on a ledge, and every cut she took tonight he would feel.

*Hold the door.*

The other one lowered her head and stepped out of the doorway onto the flags. She put her whole body between the bear and the well.
