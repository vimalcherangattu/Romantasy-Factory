# Chapter 11: Sharp-Edged Silver

Parukutty bled on the queen's silver before she had finished counting it.

She sucked her thumb and laughed, embarrassed, and set the coin back in its row on the long table. Neela, standing at the back of the hall with her bandaged hand against her hip, stopped breathing for the length of a count.

Rema had paid the death-gift early. She had said so in the same level voice she used for everything. "A widow's gift is owed from the day of the death, Parukutty of the lower village. The harvest is only when the crown remembers to pay it." She wrote the receipt herself, since Ittan's clerk sat in the guardroom, and blotted it with the side of her hand. Twelve coins for Raman the spearman, who had died in single file under the shelf. It was more than the salt factor's six and a half measures and his tally fee together.

The coins were new. They lay in the light from the high window as bright as cut fruit. Sprig on one face. A rim milled so keenly that it had opened a woman's thumb. Not a dent among them, no clipped edge, no sweat-dark from a hundred palms.

Neela had seen that edge before. She had crouched above a cold camp in the lower narrows and watched it wink in a scout's satchel.

Devan sat on the bench by the window with his forearms on his knees. He had said nothing since Rema opened the purse. His face gave nothing away. But his eyes came up once and found Neela's across the hall, and went down again.

"Take it," Rema said to Parukutty, not unkindly. "Pay what you owe before the factor hears you have it."

Parukutty gathered the coins into her mundu with the care of a woman holding eggs. She bowed to the justice, to the warden, and to the room, and went out into the court with her head high.

Neela caught her at the padippura, under the roof, where the rain came off the tiles in a bead curtain.

"Parukutty. How is Minnu's cough?"

"Better, lady. The bark you sent." She tilted her head. "Is something wrong with the silver? The justice said it was good."

"It's good." Neela took a worn panam from her sleeve, dark with years of hands, and held it out. "I've a fancy. Minnu is six, and a child under a bright coin sleeps better, my mother used to say. Let me have one for her pillow and keep this old one instead. You'd get the same weight at the factor's."

It was a poor lie. Parukutty looked at the old coin, then at her, and her mouth twitched.

"You have a funny way of asking for things, lady."

"So I'm told."

The widow picked one bright coin out of her mundu, wiped the blood off it on her wrist, and traded. "For Minnu," she said. "Chandu will say I cheated him. He wanted the best one for his father's shelf."

"Tell him the warden's wife is sorry."

Neela closed her good hand on the sharp rim. It bit her palm, in the same place as the scab, and the scab tore open.

* * *

The tack room smelled of wet thatch and bowstring wax. Two buckets caught the roof leaks, and the tick of rain on tin kept time with her pulse.

Devan had borrowed the pepper-weigher's brass scale from the granary. He set it on the trestle and laid the scouts' satchel beside it. It had sat in the strongbox since the narrows. Three bright coins came out of it, as fresh as the day they were struck.

Neela put hers on the table. Four coins now, in a row.

Neither of them spoke. He put one of the satchel coins in the left pan and the widow's coin in the right. The beam shivered and settled. He added a grain of rice to the left. The beam shivered again, and held level.

"Same weight," Neela said.

"To a grain." He lifted both and turned them against the lamp. "Look at the sprig."

She looked. It was a pepper sprig like any other, five leaves on a stem. But the lowest leaf was doubled, a faint second edge ghosting beside it, as if the die had slipped in the striking. She turned the widow's coin over. The same leaf. The same ghost.

"One die," she said.

"One die, and one run. Struck together, I'd say, and not long before the wedding." He put the coins side by side and laid his thumb across them. "The crown paid Raman's widow and the men who killed Raman out of the same box."

"A mint sells its silver to a lot of hands."

"It does."

"A lot of hands could hand it to raiders."

"They could." He did not look up. "Say it. I know you want to."

"I want you to say it." She heard how it sounded. "The thing you're thinking. Who has a mint run that no one counts, and a reason to have two kinds of coin go out?"

Devan swept the four coins into his palm and closed his fist. Neela saw the palm as it went: a thin red welt across the right one, in the exact place her own had opened. It was already beading at one end.

"Your hand," she said.

"I felt it at the gate." He did not open the fist again. "It's where the coin bit you."

"Where it bit you, then."

"Same thing, now." The corner of his mouth moved. Outside, the buckets rang in the dark. "I won't guess it aloud. A guess said in this room is a thing I have to act on. I can't act on it yet. I've a book to fetch first." He put the coins in the satchel one by one, hers last. "Remember the leaf."

"I'll remember the leaf."

She would not forget it if she tried. But he had already pulled the charcoal toward him, and the tile map of the Resin House lay waiting between them, a long square with a well drawn small behind a roof.

"The compound," he said. "You and I, going in."

"You and I are going in two ways." She drew the wall with the side of her thumb. "I go as the other one. Eight hunters on the walls, two at the drain, hounds that know every boot in the yard. They don't know the other one. They never smell her at the long table. I'll be over the east wall with the dark of the moon on my back, and the hounds will go quiet before I've reached the flagstones."

"And the well house."

"Teak bar and a farm lock. A leopard can't lift a bar." She tapped the charcoal square. "And the book isn't in the house. It's down the shaft."

He stopped. "You know that."

"I know a thing Ammu and I did at eleven." It was easier to say flat. "We dropped a pebble down that well, through a gap in the shutter. I counted. The sound was wrong. Too soon, and too dry. A ledge, a few man-lengths down, cut into the stone, above the water." She breathed. "Chathu wouldn't keep the black book in a cellar a flood could reach. He'd keep it where fire can't and damp can't, and where it's one more place a girl can't climb with a knife."

"Then I climb it."

"You climb it. On a rope, with your hands, while I stand over you and keep eight men from looking down." She moved the charcoal. "I'll open the drain from inside. You come over the wall at the ridge party's signal, and I'll lift the bar with my teeth if I have to. After that you go down, and I stay up."

"And at dawn?"

"At dawn I turn back, and I'm human with no cloth and the hunger on me, and you're sitting on the well curb with the book."

She had said it. It settled on the table like a fifth coin.

"All night," Devan said slowly. "From the ledge to first light. Every page in my hands."

"You and an animal who can't speak or read or carry." She tried a smile. "It's not the plan I'd choose."

"No," he said. "It's the only one that works."

The rain slowed. A bucket overflowed and she rose to turn it.

When she sat again he was not looking at the map. He had the scale's brass chain wound twice round his fingers, and he held it as if he'd forgotten there was anything on the other end.

"I'll ask you something," he said. "If you'll pay a question back."

"A question for a question. It's the dark rule, near enough." She made her hands lie quiet. "You first."

"What would you do, holding it, if you were me?"

"Burn it." It came too fast. "No. Burn the wrong pages and I'd have forty rogues in a night. You'd have to read them aloud by daylight, one at a time, and every one of them would hear their own shape in front of a stranger." She frowned at the chain on his fingers. "That's my answer, and you're asking something else."

"I am." He unwound the chain. "I'm afraid of myself, with that book."

She waited. She had learned that when he was close to a thing, the best course was to be a wall for it to lean on.

"I've kept my hands clean for eight years by never using them," he said. "I told myself that was virtue. It's only what I do instead of choosing. And tomorrow night I'll hold forty lives in my lap, asleep in a yard, and nothing in this world will stop me from reading any page I like but me." His voice did not rise. "There's a name in that book I want badly enough that I don't trust my hands."

She knew whose. She also knew she could not say it, and the knowledge sat in her throat, dry and hard to swallow.

"You'd read a page and cage a man," she said. "Is that it?"

"I'd like to say no." He met her eyes. "I won't say no. I've never lied to you, and I'd rather not start by being brave."

It hurt, how clean it was. She turned her bandaged hand over on her knee and looked at the dirty gauze.

"Then it's my turn," she said.

"You don't have to."

"I know I don't." The rain had almost stopped. "It's what I owe you for the last one. Listen, because I'll only say it once." She counted to three, as she did before a drop. "If I held the book, I wouldn't free anyone first. I'd turn to my own leaf, and I'd take the intake slip Chathu stuffed in his pocket, and I'd read my birth name before I opened a single cage. I want my own name more than I want to be good." She made herself keep going. "I'd like to say I'd free forty before I looked. I've killed for eleven contracts on a promise that I'd get it back. I don't think I'm that woman. I think I'm a woman who'd read hers first."

The tack room had gone very quiet. Somewhere on the wall a sentry stamped and was still.

Devan did not look away, and he did not flinch. He took the answer the way he took a blow in the pit: seeing it, standing, and keeping his feet.

"Then that's what you are," he said. "It isn't wicked to want your name."

"It isn't good."

"No. It isn't *clean*." He almost smiled. "I'd sooner hold a book beside a woman who says that than one who says she's good. The good ones are the ones I don't trust."

"That's an odd thing to tell your wife."

"I'm an odd husband."

She laughed, once, the way she laughed when he surprised her, and was ashamed of how it went through her.

"Then let's bargain like odd people," she said. "Twenty-one of my twenty-seven years I answered to a number. You've had thirty-two years and chosen almost nothing. You've known what I was since our wedding night, and you could have buried me at the pass."

"I meant to," Devan said. "I took a bride the council gave me. I meant to hold her at arm's length for a season."

"And then?"

"And then you counted my stairs." The corner of his mouth moved. "We married twice, Neela. Once for the pepper house's price, and once because I stood in my own court at noon and said it with Janu crying into a ladle. Nobody gave you to me the second time."

"Nobody gave you to me either." She lifted her chin. "So here's my price. You choose it a third time, with a book in your hands and your own name for a thing you want. And I choose it back, with mine."

"Then it's chosen."

He did not touch her. He pushed the scale a finger's width to one side and set the charcoal on the map, and she took it to mean *later*. The roof dripped. The lamp ate the oil it had.

"The rest of the plan," she said at last. "Who's on the ridge, who's at the drain, who's got the rope."

"Bhaskaran's best. I'll write it out." He drew the well house. "And the first rule of a night I'll spend holding the book."

"What's that?"

"That if I start reading a page, for any reason, you stop me."

"With what? I'll be an animal."

"Then you'll have to think of something," Devan said, "that works on a leopard."

* * *

On the last evening, the sky over Kettamala went the colour of old brass. Rema's tally chest stood shut in the south range. Muthu was at the sixth clank of the chain, with the gate log under his arm and the bearing of a man watched by a hawk. They would ride at first light, and by dusk they would be on the ridge above the Resin House.

Neela sat on the warden's window ledge, where she had crouched a hundred times, and held the flask in her good hand.

It was full. Black and cold and very small, with a cork she had cut herself. It smelled of scorched tamarind and pine sap, and the bitterness found the back of her tongue before she had even lifted it. Tomorrow night she would need every drop, for the ridge, the wall and the hounds.

Not tonight.

Her nails had been warning her for days. Three split to the quick, two more going. A night sweat two nights back that she had hidden by throwing the quilt off her side. The ache was a bill, and it never forgot the date. Each night she went without added a line to the account. Those other nights she had had a quiet week ahead to pay for it. This time she had a wall.

Tomorrow she had to crawl over it with eight men on top, on hands already stiff. If she went to bed human tonight, she'd climb with a fever coming up and fingers that did not close all the way. Wanting him now would be paid for in her body, on the raid.

She turned the flask in her fingers. Then she thought of the sword on the floor with its hilt toward her. Of the dish of rice and curd she had found at dawn, on mornings she'd never told him she would need one. Of his hand on the brass chain. *I'd rather not start by being brave.*

She wanted to.

Below, a spoon rang in the kitchen. Kannan was shouting at the goat. The wall behind her was warm from the afternoon, and the last of the light lay along the sill in a red bar.

She pushed the cork home and set the flask in the pouch, in the fold of her cloth, where she could not reach it without thinking. The sun still stood above the ridge. The burn at dusk was an hour off, and it would find her human.

She would have nothing to hide behind in the morning, and the ache would count it. Fine. Let it count.

She stood. The ache in her nails told her the price exactly, down to the quick. She put her bandaged hand against the shutter, felt the third slat give a little under her palm, and went to find her husband.

He was in the kalari pit, alone, with the rope for the well coiled over one shoulder and a lamp in each niche round the rim. He looked up at her step on the stair. The red line still showed across his right palm.

"Stay with me tonight," she said. "Through dusk, with no pebble in your cheek and no resin on me. All the way to the light." She came down one more step. "And then tell me what you'll let me take out of your hand."

He set the rope down on the earth.
