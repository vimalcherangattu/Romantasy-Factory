# Chapter 28: Daylight Reading

Thirty riders sat their horses on the apron outside the Resin House gate, and every one of them had his back to the wall.

Neela stopped at the edge of the trees. Her calf had stiffened on the walk down into a hot line of pain, and Devan's weight was on her shoulder as hers was on his, two limps making one bad stride. Vellan came last, bare to the waist, with nobody holding his arm.

"Garrison grey," Devan said. "That's Muthu."

The captain swung down and came across the trampled ground. He didn't laugh and he didn't greet them. He looked at the two welts, the blood dried black on Devan's leg, the bear-sized man behind them, and he took it in the way he took in a bad ford.

"Rema posted her order on the hall door the morning you rode," he said. "Plain, so no man could say he misheard. No soldier of Kettamala to give aid to any rogue odiyan, on pain of the queen's displeasure." He rubbed his jaw. "Bhaskaran's holding the ridge road behind us. I brought thirty. Told them to face the road and not turn round, whatever they heard."

"They'll lose their stripes for this," Devan said.

"Some of them. I asked for volunteers and got more than I could mount." He pulled a folded square from his sleeve. Grey wax, unbroken. "My month's letter. Wrote it at the second watch, for the first runner on the goat path. Unni watched me do it and said nothing, which was kind of him."

An iron basket stood by the gate where the tappers burned their rag ends. The hunters had kept it lit all night. Muthu held the letter over it a moment and looked at Neela.

"I spelled your name right," he said.

"Thank you."

"Burned it anyway." He dropped it in. The wax ran grey over the coals and the paper browned and curled. "I'm talking, Neela. Have you noticed? Loud as a drum. I want it noted."

It was almost a joke. She nearly laughed, and the sound cracked in her throat instead. For six years he had carried two loyalties in one pocket. She watched the smoke lift off the last of the second one.

Above them the wall had filled with hunters. Hooked poles, nets, iron rings on chains. Pachan stood below the gate arch, tall and stooped in his grey wrap, with his eyes on his boots.

"Pachan," she said.

He looked up.

"I'm not asking you for anything. The book is down here in a pack, and nobody owns anyone in it by dawn. You can take that to the wall and hold it for him, or you can put down the pole."

A pole came off the wall and rang on the stone. Then another. Then all of them, clattering down like dropped spits. Pachan lifted the bar himself, and the gate dragged open.

"Face the road," Muthu told his men, very quietly. Thirty heads stayed forward.

Neela went in alone first. She would not cross that sill with a soldier at her back.

* * *

The forty stood in the yard at noon, and the sun stood straight over them.

They wore blankets and sacking and one another's shirts. The torn resin scars at their throats and wrists had been bound with strips of tapping cloth, and the cloth had bled through. Some looked at her. Most looked at the ground. Pavi had ridden back from the cairn camp at first light, and she stood at the edge of the crowd with her curled forepaw hand pressed to her chest.

Chathu sat on the cook-house step, quite upright, with his hands in his lap. Two of Muthu's men had walked him there. He had taken in the empty hunters' wall and said nothing.

"You two," Neela said to the soldiers. "Out to the apron. Face the road with the rest."

They looked at Devan. He nodded. They went, and the gate shut behind them. Thilak and Kuttu came and stood either side of the step, and Chathu did not turn his head to either.

Devan stood at the gate with the pack at his feet. Vellan sat against the well wall under the eye of nobody. Thilak had put a cup of ginger tea beside her boot without a word.

"I'm going to read your pages aloud," Neela said.

The yard hushed.

"All of them. Aloud, in full, in daylight, in front of whoever you choose. Once I've read yours, you take it, and from then on nobody can read it against you. Not me. Not him." She didn't point at the step. "That's the one way out of the leash that Chathu never told you about."

"What's the price?" That was Kuttu, thin and angry.

"Whoever hears your page hears your shape. The reading makes them your witness. If I read it here, the whole yard has it." She made herself say the rest. "That's thirty-nine new people with a word in their mouths, and I don't get to pick who they are. The soldiers are outside, and they stay outside. But everyone in this yard is a witness by the end. You can ask me to read yours alone, in the shed, to one person you name. You can ask me to wait. If you ask me to wait, I hold it, and a holder is a master, and I don't want to be that to anyone. But it's yours to say."

"And if we say nothing?" Kuttu asked.

"Then I stand here until you do."

Nobody moved for a long moment. A hunter had left a tapping bell on the ground, and it rocked in the dust and went still.

Twenty-two stepped out first, narrow and pale in a man's shirt. "Shed," she said. "Her." She pointed to Pavi. "Nobody else."

"Noon sun, I said."

"The shed has a skylight."

They went in together. When they came out, a quarter hour later, Twenty-two held a strip of tacky paper in both hands and kept saying a word under her breath, four syllables. *Kamalakshi.* She said it like someone trying a stair in the dark.

After that they came. Six asked for the shed. The rest stayed in the sun and heard each other's animals read out in Neela's flat, careful voice. *Thirteen. Boar. Witnessed at first turning, and sworn in resin-ink by Chathu of the Resin House.* A boy flinched at *wolf.* Nobody laughed at *hare.* She read the intake rolls fastened behind the pages, and she watched what they did to faces.

"Thulasi," she read. "A salt-boiler's daughter, of Pazhayangadi."

"My mother boils salt," Thulasi said. Then she sat down in the dust.

"Kumaran, called Kuttu. A ferryman's son."

Kuttu said nothing. He took the page. Twice more as the afternoon went on, she heard him say it to himself, flat and wondering: *Kumaran.*

Her voice went twice. Thilak brought more tea, and she drank it and went on.

When she reached him, he stood with his big hands hanging. A tapper before he was ever a beast, twenty-two years in the House. *Four. Boar.* She finished the roll, and his village, and the name of the man who had signed him over for a debt.

"Burn it," Thilak said.

"The roll burns with the page. You won't get it back."

"I know where I'm from. I know who sold me." He held out his palm. "I don't want it written down anywhere for someone to carry."

She gave it to him. He took it to the gate basket and fed it in, page and roll, his village and the debt together. The ink curled green and black. He stood watching the smoke with his shoulders loose.

She had not known a man could look lighter for losing something.

Madhavi came last of the forty, brindled shape and bitten tongue. She looked at Devan's thigh, then at the ground. "I'm sorry," she said, to the dirt between them.

"It's closed," Devan said. "Take your page."

She took it, and she did not look up.

* * *

Vellan's she read standing, because she did not trust her knees.

She had torn it from the ledger herself, on a rock, and it had lain against Devan's ribs most of a night. She unfolded it in the sun with the whole yard watching.

"Thirty. Sloth bear. Witnessed at his first turning, and sworn in resin-ink by Chathu of the Resin House."

Vellan sat very still against the well. When she reached the roll she read it plainly. "Vellan. A joiner's son, of Venmanal. Sold at nine."

"I knew that," Vellan said.

"I know." She walked across the packed earth and held it out. "It's yours. Read against you by no one. Burn it or keep it, but you decide."

His big hand closed on it and stopped. He looked up past her, to the gate. "Not me you should be giving it to."

"You were holding it all night," she said, over her shoulder, to the man at the gate. "I'm done holding it."

Devan came forward. His limp showed with every step. Some of the forty drew back, and Thilak's weight shifted. Pavi caught his arm without turning her head.

"Nobody touches him," Neela said. She said it as a request, and she heard how it sounded. It was her voice, not a master's. "He walks out of here. He owes every one of you, and he knows it, and you can tell him so when you find him. Today he walks."

Devan stopped an arm's length from the man who had killed his sister. She could not read his face. She had thought she could read it by now.

"You saw me," Vellan said. "Dawn. You've got it."

"I have."

"Say it, then. It's all I'd have done." Vellan's mouth did something that was nearly a smile. "Not a bad swap, for the stair."

Devan said nothing for the length of a long breath. Then he turned his back.

He did it slowly. He put himself between the bear and the gate, and he put his bare shoulders and his bitten leg in reach of a man with a joiner's hands. The yard saw it. She saw it. Vellan's eyes widened, and he had no mouth for a joke now.

"Go," Devan said, to the gate.

Vellan got up, which took him two tries. He folded the page small and put it in his waistband. He stood a moment by the well wall as though he had left something there. Then his hitching stride crossed the yard behind Devan's back, and out through Pachan's gate, and across the apron past thirty soldiers staring at the road, and into the wood.

Neela counted his steps. At forty-one she stopped.

"Forty-one," Devan said, still not turning.

"What?"

"The steps. After I said go." He turned at last. His face stood open as a shutter, as it had been under the lamp. "I learned it from someone."

She tried to answer, and it didn't come. She stood and looked at the man who had held the one sentence he had wanted for six years, and had put it in her hand, and had just let it walk. He looked at her in turn, at the stack of empty cloth on the ground where forty pages had been, and the ink on her fingers.

He didn't say it. Neither did she. Her eyes burned, and he nodded once, as if she had.

* * *

Kennel nine had straw changed that week, and a clean cloth in one corner.

The aisle was cool and dim, forty-two cages in a row under the oldest roof, forty-one of them filled, and Muthu's water-carriers had been through. Pavi walked behind her with the ledger's back half. Devan stayed at the door. Neela went down on her knees at the iron bars.

The civet lay on the cloth with its chin on its paws. A silver anklet circled one thin hind leg above the foot, its bells cut off long ago.

"Kirri," Neela said. "It's Neela. Ammu's chechi, I think she'd say. I've come to read your page."

The small eyes moved, slowly. They found her face and went away again.

She read it through the bars. *Number Twelve. Civet. Witnessed at first turning, and sworn in resin-ink.* Then the roll, in the plain tongue, at the speed of walking. "Kirthika. A boatwright's daughter, of the coast. Sold at five."

A boatwright's daughter. She read it again, in case it might matter.

Ammu had written four times. Neela told her that, in a low voice, because someone ought to. Ammu had sat in her inn at Kettamala and hummed a fishing song and written a fifth letter that she'd never had time to send.

Kirri's whiskers trembled. The long grey body stayed where it lay.

There was no use waiting for more. She had known it, the way she'd known the hour at which her own second wrist would burn. A caging could not be undone, and a page in the straw could not undo it. She folded the sheet once and slid it through the bars. It lay on the cloth by the silver anklet, in reach of a paw that would never close on it.

"It's yours," she said. "Nobody reads it but you."

The civet blinked once. Then it curled round and slept.

Pavi put a hand on her shoulder. Neela stayed on her knees until she could trust her face, and then she got up and went to ten. There were forty more pages in the book, and she read them one after another through the afternoon, cage by cage, until the light went from the slats. Each time she laid a page down in the straw.

* * *

At dusk, Muthu's men took Chathu out through the gate.

He did not struggle. His hands were bound before him, and a strip of clean cloth hung at his neck for after dark. Devan had been very exact about that: gag him at sunset until the sun comes up again, and nobody speaks to him but by daylight. He walked with his uneven left foot on the packed earth and stopped by Neela.

"Seventeen," he said. He said it kindly. "There's a balance, child. Twenty-one years of feeding and keeping and—"

"My name's Neela." Her voice was steady. "And Indu. I keep both. The account's closed."

He looked at the back half of the ledger on Pavi's arm, empty of pages, stitched thread and stubs. "A book is a thing that holds," he said, almost to himself. "Take it away and see what's left."

"We'll see," she said.

They put him on a horse for the Justice at Kettamala. Pachan stood in the gate and watched him go, his long face working. When it was done he turned to Neela with his hands open.

"Someone should keep the kennels," he said. "And the groves. If anyone wants them kept."

"Keep them. Water them every day. Nobody rations resin. No one is the master of the groves. You tap, and each member takes a season's flask from the store tonight, and next season you ask them what they need."

He nodded slowly. "And who's it for, if it's nobody's?"

"Everyone," she said. "That's the whole trouble with it."

They left in the dusk, the forty, in twos and threes, with their bound wrists and a flask apiece. They rode east along the ridge to the old cairn camp, where Bhaskaran's men would hold the stones and look the other way until the court had spoken. Some of them spoke to her as they passed, and some did not. Thulasi put her page to her mouth. Kuttu said the word *Kumaran* once more and grinned like a boy.

Neela cut the back half of the ledger from its cover with a tapping knife. It came away from its stitching with a soft sigh. Before she wrapped it she turned it over once more, and her thumb found the stubs near the end.

Leaves had been cut from the binding with a clean blade. There were a great many. By each stub, in the margin, someone had inked a sunbird no bigger than a lentil, with its wings folded.

She counted them twice. Then she closed the book on them and said nothing. Devan was at the gate with the pack, and she could not make the number mean anything yet.

The emptied back half she kept, wrapped, and what she had meant to keep: the contract books, loose-stitched, and the last quire in its green thread under the wax of a spread-winged bird. Devan took them from her and buckled them under his flap. He did not ask what else she had seen.

"A day's hard ride to Kettamala," he said. "The assize is still sitting."

"I know."

She knew what the court would want, too. A leaf in the black book, in Chathu's hand, with a number at its foot. *Lookout, Seventeen.* She had read eighty-two pages today and given every one of them away. She could not hand that one on to anyone.

Devan did not look at her. He did not ask. She stood with her ruined hands in the dusk, the ledger gone from her, and understood that she would have to decide it on her own.
