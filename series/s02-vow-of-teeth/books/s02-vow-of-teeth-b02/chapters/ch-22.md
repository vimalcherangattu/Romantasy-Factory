# Chapter 22: The Hollow Step

Neela did not put the ledger down.

She had carried it up from the Blackbark Wood in her saddlebag, thumped against her hip for a day and a half of wet road. When the grey mare stopped under the padippura, she slid off with the bag still on her shoulder. Unni took the reins. She gave him her thanks and his mother's name, the salt-fish seller, and he went red to the ears.

"Rooms are aired," Unni said. "Janu says you're to eat first."

"Janu says a lot of things."

"Yes, wife of the warden." He ducked his head. "She says them louder when you're thin."

Devan had already gone to find Rema and the pit and, she suspected, a wall to put his hand against. He had looked at her once at the gate. It was the look of a man leaving a lamp lit in a window and walking away from it.

The kitchen was warm, and it smelled of coconut oil and curry leaf. Chinna sat on her stool by the hearth with her bad hip stuck out. Janu stood at the long table with her sleeves rolled, banging a lid onto a pot.

"Sit," Janu said, without turning. "Look at you. You've gone down to a lamp-wick. Two nights in a wood with men, and nobody thought to hand you a plate."

"We had rice."

"Cold rice is not rice." Janu turned, wiped her hands, and looked her over. She was sixty-one, small and square, with a brass key on a cord at her neck, and her eyes missed nothing. "Sit."

Neela sat. The saddlebag stayed on her knee, both arms over it. Janu's eyes went to the bag and away again, which was her way of asking nothing.

The plates came. Rice, ghee, fish curry, a dish of plantain, buttermilk with curry leaf floating on it. Janu scolded as she set each one down. The guests ate too little, the warden ate nothing, and the stew had been made for forty and would feed six. Then she set down another plantain.

Neela picked up a ball of rice. It was warm. She held it, and the kitchen noise went on around her: Chinna's grumble, a girl scraping a pan, the iron cistern chain clanking its hour out in the yard. She looked at the key on Janu's cord.

*She holds it six years and cannot go in.* Devan had told her that, and Neela had seen the shut door at the end of the south passage.

She put the rice back on the plate.

"Not hungry," she said.

Janu stood very still. For a moment Neela thought she would be scolded properly, and wanted it. Instead Janu said, "Whatever it is will be a worse thing on an empty stomach," and put the plate where Neela could reach it. She did not push it. She stood there with her arms folded, and then she went to see about the fire.

Neela sat with the book on her knee and a full plate going cold. In the end she ate three mouthfuls. They tasted like ash.

* * *

The dispensary was a low room off the north court, hung with bundles of bark and leaf. It smelled of camphor and boiled cloth, and under that of something else.

Eravi was stooped and slow. He unwound Devan's dressing from her ribs with fingers that shook a little and never slipped. He looked at the graze for a long breath.

"Clean," he said. "Cleaner than I'd have done. Your husband has a gentle hand for a man who breaks people."

"He's had practice with horses."

"So I hear." He smeared salve from a clay pot. Cool, then a sting. "You'll keep this. The skin's closing well."

She watched his hands, because she watched everyone's. They were spotted, and the cuffs were stained green. His satchel sat on the stool beside her, flap open, and the smell came off it like heat off a stone. Scorched tamarind. Pine sap. Nightbark.

She kept her face as it was. There was a tapper on the wood road, he had told Devan. A man for willow bark and poppy. A man for a man's bad hip. There were tappers everywhere in the hills, and resin got into everything they touched, and an old physician might carry its smell home on his coat the way a cook carries onions.

She counted the bundles on the beam. Eleven. Nine of them were willow.

"How many does the House hold now?" Eravi asked, winding the cloth.

"The House?"

"Janu likes to know how many mouths." His eyes stayed on his work. "Forty, I heard. I only ask for the beds and the pots."

"Who's asking, you or Janu?"

"I ask for everyone. It's the trouble with the work." He tied the knot, and patted it, kindly, as he might have patted a child's hand. "Oh, and forgive an old man, I nearly forgot. The day you read the pages back. If I knew which, I'd keep the dispensary open. When it falls, I mean. A day of that sort, there will be tears and fainting, and nobody sends for the physician until the floor is wet."

She had said that sentence to four people. Devan. Pavi. Bhaskaran in the grove, and Thilak over tea. None of them would tell a fort physician, and Devan had been at her side every hour since.

"Thank you for the dressing," Neela said. It came out level. She stood and eased the wrap down over the cloth. "It's very neat."

"It's my work."

"It is." She lifted the saddlebag onto her shoulder. At the door she looked back, once, at the old man scrubbing his hands in a basin, humming something that was not a tune. His satchel sat on the stool. The flap hung open on the dark inside.

She walked out into the court and filed it. *How many. Which day.* Two questions, side by side, with nothing under them. She would not answer them yet, and she would not forget them.

She counted nineteen steps to the arch without meaning to.

* * *

The sun was going down behind the west wall when she came to the stair.

She avoided it. Since her first week at Kettamala she had taken the east stair, eighty-one steps by the wall-walk where the west way took forty-four, and she had climbed the west only when forced. She had told herself it was the view. She had counted the west stair once, in the dark of her own head. She had shouted from the head of it on the worst night of her life. Once she had gone up it on purpose, under Rema's eyes, and her knees had known the difference.

She had never touched the newel.

The lower court was nearly empty. A boy was carrying water, one slow bucket at a time. At the foot of the stair stood the newel, a carved parrot as long as her forearm, its beak broken short and worn smooth as a river stone.

She put her bare hand on it before she could decide not to.

The stone was cold. At her throat, where the resin scars lay in their pale ridges, there was a faint burn, the first warning of dusk. She had smeared nothing. She breathed through it, in and out, and it passed, as it did on the nights she kept her own shape. She stayed a woman. She stood a woman at the foot of the stair, with her palm on the bird.

She climbed.

One, two, three. The treads were worn in the middle by feet. Pala wood in the rail, smooth under her fingers. Four, five, six. Nothing in her stirred but the count. Seven, eight, nine. The light went amber on the laterite. Ten.

Eleven rang.

It was not loud. A hollow note under her heel, like a knock on a shut door in an empty house. The drain slot ran beneath it, and the tread over it gave back the sound of the space below. She stood on it and did not move.

She had stepped on that note before. She had known it at once on the first day, and told Devan *I don't know* with her thumbnail dragging along her finger. She had said it for two months.

Eleven. She went on to forty-four and stood on the landing. The passage ran off toward the south range, dim, with a window at its end. She looked at it and did not walk it. Then she turned and went down again, counting aloud under her breath, and stopped at the foot with her hand on the newel.

The memory came the way water comes up in a well, from under.

A hand on her shoulder in the dark. The cloth over her eyes, with its smell of scorched tamarind. A walk she did not remember, some of it uphill, some of it stone. Then the cloth coming off, and the world very sharp and very black.

She had stood where she stood now. A stair rose in front of her. A brass lamp was in her hand, a shuttered one, small and warm as a held bird.

*Hold it. If anyone comes into the court, open it twice. Do not speak.*

She had held it. The thin bar of light had leaned across the bottom step and the carved bird, a parrot, with its beak broken short. She remembered thinking it looked insulted. She was twenty-one and she had been given a job, and she was good at her jobs.

Then a big man's weight went past her on the stair. Soft, for a thing that size. Up and up, a step at a time, into the dark. She had heard the eleventh tread ring under him, very faintly. She had held the lamp.

And a voice, above her. A woman's, young, quick, not frightened yet. It came down the stairwell with a laugh just fading out of it.

*Who's there?*

Neela stood at the foot of the stair with the light in her hand.

*Who is that?*

The lamp did not shake. She had made sure of that. She had been told to hold it, and she held it, and the bar of light lay across the bird, and she said nothing.

There had been a sound, after. She had not needed to hear it twice, and she did not call it up now. Afterward she had counted. Three hundred and ten. Then the hand on her shoulder, and the cloth.

That was all of it. It was the same night she had seen in the book's lamplight, in the master's room in the wood. She had told Devan about the blindfold and the lamp and the count. She had left out the voice. She had not decided to. It had simply never come up out of the well until her hand was on the bird.

*Who's there?*

She had known the answer. Someone had stood at the bottom with a light, and the light was hers.

Neela took her hand off the newel. It left a damp print on the stone. She looked at it until it dried.

"Ask me now," she said to the bird, to the stair, to a girl of twenty-two with a laugh in her voice. Her own voice came out flat and small. "Ask me who it is."

Nobody asked.

* * *

Boots crossed the court behind her.

She knew the tread. It stopped a long way off, and she was grateful for that, and angry at being grateful. When she turned, Devan stood by the well post at the edge of the lower court, twenty paces away, with a lantern unlit in his hand.

He did not come nearer. His shirt was damp from the pit, and there was a smear of red earth up one forearm. He looked at her face, then at the newel, then at her face again. She saw him understand it, all of it, without a word said. His mouth flattened. He set the lantern on the well curb.

"You're going to tell Janu," he said.

"Tonight." Her mouth was dry. "I thought I'd have a day. But she'll be along with the lamps."

"At the hour." He nodded toward the cistern. "She comes round the court at dusk. She always has."

"She stops short of the stair."

"Ten paces." His eyes didn't leave her. "Six years."

Neela folded her arms and held her own elbows. A boy crossed the court with a bucket and saw them, and went wide.

"I won't ask you to help me," she said. "I'd like that understood. I'm going to say it, and it'll be the wrong words. If you step in and say them better, I'll never know if I could."

"I know."

"If you say a single thing for me, I'll hate it."

"Then I won't." He said it slowly, as if setting down something heavy. "I'll stand where you can see me. That's all. Here, by the post, or closer if you tell me. I won't speak for you. If you stop, I won't finish it. If you want me gone, say *go* and I'll go, and I won't ask why."

She looked at him. He looked back, with that dry tired steadiness, a man who could have crossed the court in five strides and was keeping his boots where they were.

"And if I want to hit something?" she said.

His mouth moved, a very little. "The newel's yours."

It was a small joke, and it cost him. She heard that. Something in her chest that had been clenched since the kitchen let go a notch.

"It's insulted enough already," she said.

"Asha thought so too."

Neela breathed in. She breathed out. "Stay there."

"I'm staying."

"Don't come nearer."

"I won't."

She turned back to the stair. Her whole body wanted to run. She stood at the foot of it with her hands open, and above her the light went from amber to a thin rose, and the first lamp showed in the south range.

The cistern chain clanked its hour.

Across the court a door opened. Janu came out of the kitchen passage with a lamp in each hand, brass, lit, swinging a little as she walked. She was already scolding someone behind her about the oil. She came down the flagged way along the north side of the court with her small quick step, and her eyes went up the stair, and she stopped.

She stopped ten paces from the foot, where she always stopped. She did not seem to know she did it.

Neela's hands were cold. She had held a lamp steady for a long time. She had learned how.

"Janu," she said.

The lamps stopped swinging.
