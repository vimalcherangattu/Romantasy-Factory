# Chapter 7: Blackbark in the Rain

The knife slipped on the third cut and went into her palm instead of the bark.

Neela did not swear. She counted. One for the sting, two for the heat, three for the bright line that welled across the heel of her right hand and ran down to the wrist. The tree went on dripping black into the flask below it, unbothered.

Thirty paces back, behind the dead stump where she had told him to wait, someone sat down hard in the wet fern.

They had ridden since full dark. The wood road ran under blackbark and wild jackfruit, red mud to the horses' fetlocks, and the rain came in sheets and stopped as if someone had shut a door on it. Between downpours the wood steamed. By the time grey light showed through the canopy she had stopped feeling her feet. By noon they had left the horses tied in a ravine and climbed the ridge above the Resin House groves.

This tree was a stray. The wind had seeded it on the high ground, outside Chathu's fenced rows and his count. Its trunk was black and plated like dried mud. Nightbark can be tapped at noon, and she had known that since she was nine. She had been taught on this kind of slope by a tapper's wife who closed her hands over Neela's and cut for her, and who sang while she did it.

She was humming it now without noticing. The tune only. The words had never come, not past the first verses, and the last verse she had never heard at all.

The knife had gone because of the tune. She had been listening for the next note.

A shadow fell across the fern. Devan had crossed the thirty paces without a sound. He did not say anything. He took a square of linen from inside his shirt, already folded, and held it out.

She stared at the cloth. Then at his right hand, which was held a little out from his side, palm up. Across the heel of it ran a thin red line the length of hers. The welt stood up as she watched, bright and small, and a bead of blood came to its edge.

"I said behind a tree," she said.

"It was a tree. It was four paces further than the one you meant." He still did not look at his hand. "Take the cloth."

She took it. She pressed it into her palm and he pressed his own palm shut around nothing, and for a moment they stood there, two people holding the same wound. She had cut herself a hundred times. She had never had a hundred witnesses to it.

"Don't wipe the flask rim with that," he said. "Tamarind and pine. It will burn the cut."

"I know." She did not know. The black drop that had landed on the edge of her palm was already burning like a spark, and she shook it off into the fern with a hiss. "Now I know."

"Does it hurt on you?"

"It hurts on both of us." She nearly smiled. "That's the arrangement."

He looked at his palm at last. "Then it's a good thing I don't need it for anything."

He went back to his stump. She wrapped the linen under her bangle and tied it with her teeth, and finished the cut one-handed, slow and careful. When the flask was full to the neck, the weight of it in her left hand was so strange that she held it a moment before corking it. Twenty-seven years old, and she had never once held a full flask that was hers.

* * *

The ridge looked down on the Resin House like a hand on a map.

They lay flat on wet leaf-mould under a bank of fern, with the horses a long way below. Devan had said little all morning and now said nothing. He watched with his chin on his folded arms, the way he watched the pass from the wall.

She gave him the counts without being asked, low, as she would have to Ammu.

"Forty in the yards. Mostly women, a few boys under twenty. Those in the dust-coloured wraps are first-year and the ones in grey have a mark, a shape, and a number." The yards were the same red earth, the same long pits, the same tapping sheds with their sloped roofs. She knew where each door opened. "Eight hunters on the walls. Hooked poles, nets, the iron rings on chains. Two at the main gate, two at the drain, and I'd bet the other four are not where the walls tell you."

"Where?"

"Under the wall. The wall tells you where a man is not."

He gave a small grunt. It was the nearest he came to praise. "And them?"

On the practice ground, a girl of perhaps fourteen was being taught to fall. Her teacher knelt and folded her wrist into her chest and made her do it again. A boy was cleaning a net. Two women ran the length of the east wall and back, again and again, with stones in their cloths. They were quick and silent, and they all wore the same look.

"Twenty years ago that was me," she said. "I thought it was a school."

Devan did not answer. Below them a man limped across the yard, big-shouldered, bear-slow, dragging his left leg a half-step behind the right. He stopped at the edge of the pit and everyone nearest him went still. She had not breathed since he came into the open.

"That's him," Devan said.

"Yes."

"He's lame."

"You did that."

"I struck his shoulder."

"He took it in the thigh coming down." She counted his steps. Eleven to the well, a pause, eleven back. The pause was longer than the leg needed. "Or the ground did. I never saw. I was a cat, and then I was a woman, and then I was running."

His fingers had gone white on the fern. She felt it as a tightness in her own hand and turned to look at him. He was watching Vellan the way the bear had watched him on the stair, with eyes that were not afraid and that she could not read.

"He carved Ammu a bird once," she said. "He leaves them on the mats of the little ones." She did not know why she said it. "I don't know what that makes him."

"A man who kills with his hands full of birds," Devan said.

"Yes."

"I won't answer that."

"I didn't ask you anything."

"You were going to." He rested his cheek on his arm and looked at her. "I'd have said it badly."

She turned her face back to the compound. Below the cook-house, behind a shuttered well, was the oldest roof in the Resin House, long and low and tiled in black. She knew what it covered. She had known since she was a child, and had learned it again in the mud of the last yard she crossed, on four paws and then on two.

Forty-one cages and one empty. A fox. A hare. A civet with a silver anklet.

She did not count it. She did not draw it in her head for the plan. She looked at the roof just long enough to see that no one went in or out, and she looked away.

Devan said nothing. His left hand moved six inches through the fern and lay beside hers, not touching.

* * *

Dusk came early, under cloud. The yards emptied in a line towards the long table. Smoke rose from the cook-house.

The door on the far side of the main court opened and Chathu came out.

She had not seen him in the daylight in three weeks, and her stomach turned over before her mind caught up. He was smaller than she remembered. The soft grey hair, the soft grey coat. He walked with the same uneven step, the left foot swung out a little on the break that had never knit. Under his right arm, held close as a man carries a sleeping child, was a book bound in black.

"That's it," Devan said, very low.

"Yes."

"It's no larger than a hymnal."

"It doesn't have to be." Her voice sounded strange to her. "Look at his arm. He doesn't shift it. Forty lives, and he carries it like a prayer-book he's late with."

Chathu crossed the court. A hunter nodded; he nodded back. He went past the cook-house, round the corner, and out of sight, behind the kennel roof.

They waited. Neela counted two hundred. At a hundred and eighty he reappeared, unhurried, and both his hands were free.

His arm swung loose at his side. He was no longer carrying anything.

"The well," she said. "Behind the kennel roof. There's a house over it, with shutters. I've never been inside. Nobody goes in but him."

"You're sure."

"I'm sure he came out without it. I'm sure the book isn't in his room, because I was in his room at eleven with a grievance, and I looked." She wet her lips. "Hunters at the kennels are for the cages. They guard the cages and the cook-house. The well house has no one."

"Because it needs none."

"Because he's the lock." She heard how that sounded and set her teeth. "Or someone is."

It was almost dark. Her hand rose to her throat, to the ridged scars under her collar. The faint burn had begun there on its own, as it did at every sundown now, as if the body remembered.

"I can see it better from the wall," she said.

"You mean from four feet."

"I mean from the ground."

She had said all this to herself on the ride up. She still had to say it to him. She took Ammu's pouch from her waist and opened it, and held out the last thumb of resin, the one smear she had kept for inside the compound. It was no larger than a cherry stone. It shone black in the grey.

"The cache is empty, the cloth is spent, the horses are half a day down. I'm leaving you with a cold camp and no fire." She breathed. "There will be hunters on the wall at the drain tonight, and I'll keep to the dark."

"Take the pebble," said Devan.

He had already put one in his cheek. She had not seen him do it. His grey one, from his palm, sat in the hollow of his jaw, and when he spoke round it the words came soft.

"I know the rule. I'll hold it until you're back."

"You don't have to."

"I know." He turned his face to her, in what was left of the light. "Go on."

She smeared the resin. It burned on the throat first, then the wrists, the old fierce bite of it, scorched tamarind in her mouth. Within a minute the world went wide and soft and full of smells. The last thing she was aware of as a woman was his hand, steady, not on her, just near, and the knowledge of being watched by the one pair of eyes she would allow.

* * *

The night was cold and wet and perfect.

The other one went down the slope without a sound, along the drain she had known since she was nine, flat and fast under the bitter fern. The wall had a gap at its foot where the water ran. Hunters passed above, their hooked poles ticking on the stone, and she lay in the runoff and let them go by.

She could smell everything. Forty bodies at the long table, unwashed. Sesame oil. The smoke from the cook-house. Under the kennel roof, a bitter, sweet, familiar reek that tightened her flanks, deer and hare and mongoose and a thin musky animal she did not let herself name.

The well house stood at the end of a flagged path. Four posts, a roof of old tile, shuttered windows. Paper and resin-ink, dry and sour. She put her nose to the sill and it was there, the black-book smell, stronger than in Chathu's room, as if the whole building was a single page.

The door was a plank door. She sniffed its edge. An iron hasp, a farm lock as big as her skull, and over both a bar of teak that dropped into two brackets. A lock needs fingers. A bar needs hands that lift.

She pushed her shoulder against it, once. It shifted a hair in its brackets and settled, with a small knock.

On the far side of the kennel roof, a hunter's head turned.

The other one flattened against the stone and did not breathe. The man stood, listening, one hand on his pole. Then he scratched his neck and walked on, and the dark closed behind him.

She knew then what she had come to learn. The book was in a house she could reach on four paws and not open. At night, in her shape, she could stand at the door and smell it all the way to dawn. By day, in her own, she would be one woman in a yard of forty.

She went back up the slope, through the drain, over the stones, to the man who waited in the fern with a pebble in his mouth.

* * *

Light came grey and without sun. She lay on the ground with the burn of the turn still in her bones and the shaking hunger tearing at her stomach. Her cloth lay folded at her hand. Devan had put it there and had turned his back before she woke, and now sat with his shoulders to her, spitting the pebble into his palm.

Beside the cloth was a flat bread, a handful of dried plantain, and a gourd of water. She ate it all and did not say anything, and he did not say anything. She dressed with her back to him, to the wood, to the ridge.

They went down by the old tappers' path, which she had known since she was nine. Rain came and went in the trees. The horses were where they had left them.

The man stepped out from behind a trunk thirty paces from the ravine.

He was tall and stooped, in a grey wrap gone to mud at the hem, with a hooked pole in his right hand. His face was long, creased, and kind in a way that had not been used much. He was looking straight at her. There was nothing else on the path.

Her body knew him before her mind did. She stopped.

Pachan.

The hook came down a hand's width from her collarbone. The point of the iron was a thumb from her throat, and it was daylight and she was a woman, and there was no resin anywhere to help her.

Devan was in front of her before she had finished the thought. He did not draw. His hands were open and a little away from his sides, and he stood the way he stood before the block, with his weight low and his eyes on the iron, not the man.

"Don't," Pachan said. "Please. I'm not going to shout." He said it to Devan, but his eyes had not left her face. "Seventeen."

"Pachan," she said.

"You grew." He sounded surprised. The hook trembled. "The hair's wrong. The face is the same. You were ten, and Chathu had you sitting with your feet not touching the floor, and you wouldn't eat, and I had a sweet in my sleeve. That's all. A sweet." He swallowed. "I never thought I'd see you outside."

"I'm not outside," she said. "I'm running."

"Yes." He looked at the man in front of her, at his hands, at the wet black seam on the ridge behind them. His long face worked. "I should shout."

"You should," she said.

"They'd pay a year of resin."

"They would."

Nobody moved. Rain began again, soft, on the leaves. She held very still, and the part of her that always counted exits found that every one of them was a door in her own head, and that behind each stood a man who could have shut it.

The hook lifted. It went up past her face, past her shoulder, until the iron pointed at the sky.

"I owe you a sweet," said Pachan. "I never paid it."

He stepped back off the path, into the fern, and turned his face away like a man who has seen nothing and means to say so.

She went past him. She did not look back. She mounted without feeling her legs, and Devan's hand was on her stirrup for a moment, as if to steady a horse that was already still.

* * *

They rode all day, between downpours, in a silence that was not empty. She kept the flask inside her shirt against her ribs and felt it there like a second heart.

Near dark she took out Ammu's pouch to tie it to the saddle and opened it by habit, to look, the way she had every night for weeks. It was light, and loose in her fingers.

Empty. For the first time since the grey morning she had taken it off the dead girl's belt. Not a thumb, not a smear, not a crumb. She turned it inside out and smelled it. Scorched tamarind, faint, and under it the soap-and-sweat smell of a girl who was twenty-five.

She stood a moment with it open in her hand. Then she folded it flat, as if it might hold something, and put it back in her cloth.

"You'll want to throw that away," Devan said, without turning.

"I'll keep it."

"It's empty."

"I know what it is," Neela said. She heard the roughness in her own voice. "I'm keeping it anyway."

He nodded once, as a man does when something has been said that does not need his help. The rain began again. The road went red under the horses' feet.

She rode with her mind on a flagged path and a plank door, an iron hasp, a teak bar that dropped into two brackets, and a book she could smell through the wood. Chathu had put it where only a person with hands could lift it, in a place where only a person who was not the other one could stand at night.

At dusk she would be a woman or an animal. The book was behind a door that wanted both.
