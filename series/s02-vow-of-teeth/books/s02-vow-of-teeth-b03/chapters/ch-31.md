# Chapter 31: The Charter

By noon the queen had not come, and Neela had counted the road four times.

The old watch-house stood on the shoulder above the narrows, a squat laterite room with a slate roof, a door that had lost its hinges and a gate fire-basket rusted to the colour of dried blood. It had one window facing the pass. Someone had swept it. Janu, probably, who had arrived at dawn with a broom and a list of complaints.

Rema's table stood in the middle, borrowed from the lower court. The Company stood along the walls, sixty names and more, with garrison men packed in at the back. Devan stood at Neela's shoulder, a step behind and a little to the side, as he had promised to stand. His dead arm lay bound in Thankam's green sash. His good hand was in her sleeve, and he did not seem to know it.

"She'll come," he said.

"I know."

"You've counted the road."

"Four times."

"It hasn't moved."

"Neither has she," said Neela, and Kandan's voice came from the door, loud with relief.

"Riders. The queen's own."

Nirmala came up the last of the slope on foot. She had left the litter at the foot of the shoulder. Usha walked at her side, and behind them came Captain Haran with a grim face and a flat green case under his arm. The queen's grey hair had been dressed fresh, and her face carried a night with no sleep in it. She stopped in the doorless frame and looked at the room, at the sweepings and the rust and the people.

"I have been in many halls," she said. "None of them had its owners in the doorway."

"It isn't finished," said Neela.

"No. It is better for that." She walked in.

She did not take a chair. She stood at Rema's table, and Rema, who had spent a night with her own pen, stood to one side with the roll open. A clerk's round hand had written the first copy of the charter. Neela saw as Nirmala unfolded it that the last third was in the slanting hand she had seen in the sea room, quick and uneven, with two lines struck through.

"I wrote this myself," said the queen. "I would like to read it myself. Justice, you will tell me if I leave a word out."

"I will."

She began.

"*We, Nirmala, queen of Varanad, in daylight and before witnesses, recognise the Night Company as a body of free persons, owning its name, its groves, its hall and its members' lives. No page, leaf or ledger of any member shall be held by the crown or by any hand but the member's own. No member shall be hunted, caged, sold or named rogue under this charter. The Company contracts by its own vote and refuses by its own vote.*"

She read without hurry. Neela had heard her read a dozen petitions in the sea room, each in the same warm even voice. It struck her that the voice had not changed, only what it was carrying.

"*The Company holds the Blackbark groves under the map of Pachan, hunter and tapper, and the resin is the Company's to ration and no one's to sell. The sums entered in the crown's book as 'Sold to the crown', for thirty pages over twenty years, were taken from the price of children. They are owed back to the members they name, with the years, from the crown's own treasury, in panam, on the first day of the dry season.*"

A sound went round the walls. It was not a cheer. Thulasi had shut her eyes.

"*And the crown sets down, so that none who comes after may say it did not know, what it permitted.*" The queen's voice did not drop. "*The crown permitted limited raids by Thurai through the controlled passes, in exchange for a fleet that did not land. The crown permitted the pass-tokens of its councillor. The crown permitted the taking of children from villages the raids had burned, and bought their pages, and told the children they were safe. The crown permitted the death of the warden's sister Asha and the sale of the warden's own death, and sealed the writ for the latter in its own wax.*"

She stopped, and breathed once.

"I have left no word out, Justice?"

"None," said Rema.

"Then I sign."

Rema had a pen ready. The queen's hand was steady. *Nirmala,* in full, quick and slanted, no stamp, no clerk's copy. She set the pen down as if it had weighed more than a pen should.

"Recorded," said Rema, writing. "In daylight, by the high court's leave, before the parties. Captain Muthu."

Muthu came forward with the iron warden's disc in his fist. He had worn it five days like a stone in a shoe, and he pressed it into the black bead of wax beside the queen's name with his whole weight, as if to be sure. "Witnessed," he said. His voice cracked. "For the border. And the villages."

"And not for the household," said Devan, quietly.

"Not for anyone's household," Muthu said, and stepped back without looking at anybody.

Neela found she was holding Devan's hand hard. She had not known it. A welt on his palm rose slowly on her own, from nothing but the pressure, and she let it.

* * *

The green case lay open on the table. Haran had unbuckled it without being told.

Inside lay the crown leaf, what was left: four stiff pale leaves, each with a green-threaded roll, scorched along one edge. Beside them lay the sheet headed *Sold to the crown*, with thirty ledger numbers and the folded-wing birds. And under that, tied with a plain tape, forty leaves in Chathu's tidy hand and in plain ink.

"The gate basket, I think," said the queen. "Is it lit?"

It was. Kuttu had lit it at dawn, with a face that said he had a view on the matter. Nirmala took up the leaves herself. Haran moved to help her, and she shook her head.

She carried them to the door and the rusted basket and the small hungry flame in it. The Company let her through. Usha came after her, and Neela went too, because she would not have missed it for a pardon.

The queen put the sheet in first. It caught at the edge, curled, and the thirty numbers blackened in order. She fed in the four guards' pages, one at a time. The first was Sajan's. He stood at the back of the room with his brother beside him and did not look away. The ash rose and was gone on the pass wind.

"There was never a reason for these," Nirmala said, to the fire or to no one. "Only that I could. That is no reason." She took Chathu's forty, loosened the tape, and let them fall in a heap. They smoked, brown, ordinary, a clerk's list of rooms nobody lived in. The queen watched them go.

Neela thought of a grey-fingered man in a rock cell, writing in the wrong ink.

"Done," said the queen. She looked at her hands. She wiped them on her skirt like any woman at a hearth. "I keep my throne, Neela Seventeen, I believe. I do not keep my knives."

"You keep your name," said Neela. "That's a good deal."

"Is it?" The queen smiled slightly. "It is the one thing I may no longer hide behind."

Neela could have left it there. She had braced all morning for the second thing, and it came up in her throat as if it had been waiting.

"I have a request."

"You have earned a dozen."

"It's one. Chathu."

The queen waited. Behind Neela, Devan did not move or speak.

"Don't kill him," said Neela. "Don't keep him in the rock either. The kennels at the groves are full. Forty-one cages, and nobody to carry water but Bhaskaran's men on the road. Kirri's in the third from the end. He kept them. He was the only one who knew which animal was whose." She drew a breath. "Send him there. Under Pachan. He feeds them and waters them and he cleans every cage with his own hands, and he does it till he dies. He doesn't leave, and he doesn't write, and he doesn't speak a shape aloud between dusk and dawn. Pachan can keep the gag cloth."

"For life."

"For life."

"You think that is mercy?"

"No," said Neela. "I think it's the only job he's never done."

Nirmala was silent. Then she said, "Captain Haran, you will write that."

"Majesty—"

"At once, Captain."

Haran wrote.

* * *

They had one more thing to do, and Neela had been afraid of it all morning.

It was small. The Company's roll lay on a board across two buckets, as it had since Kettamala's well, a crown leaf in clerk's ink with sixty-odd names. Neela's own was first, in her hand, written the morning after the battle. Beside it, a gap.

She looked at Devan. He looked at the gap.

"I can't hold the pen," he said. "I can hold it left-handed. It will look as if a hen wrote it."

"Then it'll look like yours."

He took the reed in his left hand and wrote his name, and it did look like a hen's. *Devan.* Under it, in her own steadier letters, she wrote the line they had worked out in the dark, in the tent the night before, in whispers over the spill of the lamp.

She read it aloud for the walls, because the Company had a right to hear.

"*Bound by choice.*"

"Bound by choice," Devan said after her, to the sixty, and he said it as if it were a clause he had read ten times and agreed with.

"That is all?" said Rema, peering.

"That is all," said Neela. "We'd say the rest to each other."

Kamalakshi, near the wall, said "Finally," and nobody told her to hush.

* * *

The tent was Muthu's, lent and not asked for. It stood on the shoulder behind the watch-house with its back to the wind. There was a lamp in it, a rush mat, two blankets and one pot of water. A leaf packet of cold rice and curd stood by the door, put there by a hand that had not come in.

Neela undid the flap, and Devan ducked in after her.

He stood with his bad arm against his chest. In the lamp his face looked both tired and very young, the grey in his hair standing out. He was thirty-two and she was twenty-seven, and he had said no to a throne's offer, and he looked at the floor of the tent as if it were a field he had been told to cross.

"I can't do some of it," he said.

"I know."

"I can't hold you."

"You can hold me with the other one. Or I can hold you." She sat down on the mat and looked up at him. "I've been thinking about it all day. In the middle of the queen's clauses, I confess. I'm a bad woman."

"You were thinking about it during the reading of the sums."

"Between sums."

The corner of his mouth gave. Then it went again, and he said, low, "I'm afraid I'll do it wrong. I've done it very little. Not for eight years. And not with a hand I can't use."

"Come here."

He knelt on the mat. She took the green sash in her fingers and found the knot. It was one Thankam had tied with a surgeon's firmness, and her nails caught on it, and she bent over it, the way she bent over a lock. When it fell open she eased the arm out and laid it across her knee, the dead weight of it, the hand curled and still.

She kissed the palm. Nothing moved, but he drew a sharp breath through his teeth.

"Can you feel that?"

"A little. Like a lamp across a room." His voice had roughened. "I feel it on yours."

"Mine?"

"The welt. You pressed my hand in the hall." He was looking at her wrist where the mark lay, on the soft inside. "The share runs back to me. I can feel something there."

So the hand was dead, and still it spoke through her. She took that and held it. She turned his fingers over and set his palm against her ribs, under her arm, where the hook had torn them in Kodiyur, and she held it there with her own.

"Then feel it there," she said. "Tell me if it's too much."

He told her it was not.

She undressed him slowly. She was the one with two hands, and she used them as she had used them on every hard lock of her life, carefully, with her eyes on the work. She eased the wool from the wounded shoulder and kissed round the stitched place under the collarbone, then the nine silk stitches on his ribs. Under her mouth his breath went to pieces. When she was done with him she let him do the same for her, with his good hand, one finger at a time, slowly, as if he were learning a word in a language he'd stopped speaking.

"Ask me," she said, when he stopped.

"What?"

"You always do it without asking. Every time. You work it out from my back, and my face. Do it aloud."

His breath stopped. "Is it—may I—" The sentence did not come, and she waited. He closed his eyes. "Neela. May I put my hand here."

"Yes."

"And here."

"Yes."

"Will you stay close. I can't—reach."

"I'm not going anywhere," she said, and she moved over him.

She guided his hand where she wanted it. He learned fast, as he had learned everything she had ever shown him. He asked, and she answered, and between the answers neither of them had much breath. Her fingers laced through the ones that would not close. She took his weight on her own thighs so his shoulder would not carry it. She moved, and he said her name into the hollow of her throat, over the pale ridged scars he'd kissed a dozen times without once mentioning them, and something that had been wound tight in her since she was six years old came loose.

The share carried the rest. He had told her once it ran both ways. She had not understood. It ran now as warmth, her heat in his skin and his in hers, a doubled pulse. She felt the answer of her own pleasure arrive in him before she had finished having it. He made a sound she'd never heard from him, low and astonished, and held on to her with his one good hand as if she might be taken.

When it was over, neither moved for a long time.

"I count the exits," she said into his collar. She hadn't meant to say it.

"I know."

"I counted them when I came in. Door, flap, the seam by the pole. I always do." She was quiet. "I stopped. About the time you said my name. I didn't count anything."

He didn't say *good*. He did not say *safe*. He turned his head and put his mouth against her hair.

"Then it's the one place," he said.

"What?"

"The place where you can stop." He said it slowly, picking it out from the rest. "I'd like to be that. If you let me."

She lay on his good shoulder and looked at the tent roof, at the lamp's moving ring. She had a leash round her neck once, a ledger, a number. She had spent six years learning that a hand on the back of the neck means *wait*. His hand lay on hers now, and it meant nothing at all but *here*.

"And I'll be your right hand," she said. "All of it. The pen, the sword, the knots. Whatever you can't do, I do. Ask, and I do it. I'll say when I can't."

"You'll say it before you do it."

"I'll say it before," said Neela. "That's the bargain."

"It's a better one than the first."

"The first was a bad one," she said, and he laughed, quietly, into her hair, the slow dry laugh she had waited eight chapters of a life for.

* * *

She woke at first grey with her cheek on his collarbone and the lamp long out.

The cold came in under the flap. Out on the shoulder a langur barked once and stopped. She lay still. She did not count the door. She counted what she had counted every morning for weeks, since the first dawn it had mattered, and it was waiting where it always was: a small catch in a long slow breath, a third of the way through the count.

*One, two, and—*

There. Alive.

"Seven," said a voice by her ear, rough with sleep.

She went still. "What?"

"Seven breaths from the first of the grey. Yours catches at the ninth count. You hitch like a cart on a stone." His chest lifted under her cheek. "I've been awake a while. I wanted to be sure."

"You count mine."

"Every morning. Since the wedding. I thought you knew."

She hadn't. She lay with that and found her eyes were wet, and thought, quite calmly, that she'd been counting him for a year and he'd been counting her for as long, and neither had said.

"Then we've kept each other alive for a year," she said, "on a bad habit."

"It's a good habit." He shifted his head. In the thin light his face looked slack and easy, a man who had slept. "I have one question."

"Ask."

"The Company will want something to do. A vote, a first one. They'll want it today. Kuttu has been looking at the horn since we came in." He sounded almost amused. "What do you think they'll vote on?"

She thought of Kamalakshi's raised hand, and the sixty names on the plank, and sixty ways of saying *no.*

"I don't know," she said. "That's the point of it."

Outside the tent the horn spoke, one long bellow, from the watch-house door. It was Kuttu's, and he was not a man who waited for the light.

Neela sat up. He reached for her with his good hand, and she took it, and for a breath neither of them moved.

Then she helped him find his sleeve.
