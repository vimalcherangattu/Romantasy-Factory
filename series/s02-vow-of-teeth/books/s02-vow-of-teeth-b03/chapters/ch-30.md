# Chapter 30: Say It Anyway

Rema did not sit.

She came into the hall with the road still on her, mud to the knee and her case tied in oilcloth against her chest. Four bailiffs in borrowed green sashes stood behind her. Her old escort had gone over to the Company's side of the hall. She looked at the warden's chair, and at who was in it, and bowed to the queen exactly as far as the law asked.

"Majesty," she said.

"Justice," said Nirmala. "You have come a long way for a hall with no cushions."

"I have come with the high court's roll." Rema's voice was dry and quite steady. "It sat nine days ago. It entered Asha, daughter of the late warden of Kettamala, officer of the border, aged twenty-two, killed under contract. It entered the pass sales and the killing as one conspiracy, bought by the Keeper." She did not glance at the dead man's empty place. "The court cannot try whoever sealed the writ that paid the guild, because no one has laid it before the court. So it did the one thing left to it. It authorised me to record any charter the parties make. A true one, in daylight, with the witnesses present."

Devan stood by the table with his dead arm in its sash. His good hand was already at his coat.

Janu waited in the doorway, Muthu behind her. The Company had come in at Neela's back, thirty-odd of them, and stood along the pillars in tapper's wool. Captain Haran had stopped leaning on his pillar.

*Say it anyway,* Devan thought.

Asha's scrap. Neela had heard him read it at dawn in the warden's chamber, and she had said *Thank you for telling me.* Asha had meant it for whoever found the ugly truth. He had spent six years being a man who found it and kept it in a box. Neela knew that. They both knew what it was asking of him now.

"Justice," he said. "I have a paper to read. Then I will give it to you."

He took the oilcloth bundle from inside his coat. He had lifted it out of the hollow behind the third slat at dawn, and it had ridden against his ribs since. The red thread came away without a knot. He laid the contents on the table: a strip of hide, cracked and dark, a folded sheet with a green seal, and a lump of cut green wax.

The queen did not move. Usha, at her shoulder, did.

"This is a contract chit," he said. "It is the buyer's half. It names the warden's chamber at Kettamala, death by beast, before the pass closes. It is in Master Chathu's hand." He turned the sheet and held it where the window light fell. "And this is a payment writ. It is dated seven weeks before my wedding. It is payable to the Resin House on the warden's account. It is sealed in green wax with a sunbird, and the sunbird has a chip at the left wing tip."

He set the cut wedding-gift seal beside the writ. The chip matched.

"I wrote to the council eight weeks before the wedding," Devan said. "I said the raiders' tallies carried true wax and asked to see the seal registers. Within days, this was sealed. The woman whose wax it is sits in the warden's chair."

The Company stood at his back like weather.

Three hundred spears, he had told Neela at dawn. The captains who had not yet decided to follow a man with one hand. The pass. She had asked for no softer word, and he had given none. Now he was spending all of it.

"I will not hold this over you, Majesty," he said, turning to the chair. "I have carried it on a hill for weeks and thought about how I might. I don't want a lever. I want it seen."

He walked the three paces to Rema and put the paper in her hand.

"For the court roll. In daylight. Enter it as read."

Rema took it as if it were hot. Her thumb found the chip in the wax. She looked at the queen, and for the first time her face did something other than record.

"Entered as read," she said.

* * *

Nirmala stood.

She did it without Usha's arm, which cost her something. The welt on her left forearm showed pale where she had pushed back her cuff. It was Usha's, shared, and she did not try to hide it.

"May I answer?"

"The hall is yours," Devan said. It was the first thing he had given her that was not evidence.

"Twenty-six of my own knives knelt in the dark and chose who they would bleed for," said the queen. Her voice was warm and unhurried. It did not rise. "I held that a realm is safe by what it has not seen. It is only blind."

She stopped. The hall let her.

"I have not finished being sorry," she said. "I only found out this week I could do it standing."

Devan looked at Neela. She stood at his shoulder, a step to the side, and she was not looking at the queen. She was looking at him, and her fingers had found his sleeve and closed on it hard.

* * *

"Before this goes to the crown," Devan said, "it goes to the Company."

The queen's eyebrows rose a little. He didn't blame her. A man with one hand and no office had just told a monarch that her charter would wait on a show of hands.

"The Company owns itself," he said, to the pillars. "That is the first term. Nobody holds a page, a leash or a lease on any of you. You take contracts by vote, and you refuse them by vote. Second, the Blackbark groves go to the Company, under Pachan's map. Resin is yours to ration and nobody's to sell."

Kuttu made a sound halfway to a laugh.

"Third. The crown never holds a page again. Not the four it keeps, not the eleven it claims. They come to the people they name, or they burn." He looked at Nirmala. "Fourth. The crown recognises the Company in law. Every named member is under the charter, so none is rogue, and none may be hunted. Fifth. The Company holds the Monkey Pass for Varanad by contract. For pay, for a term, by vote, and with the right to say no."

He lowered his hand. "That's all of it. These are not mine to sign. Hands, if you want them. Nay, if you don't. If you'd cut a clause, say it."

"The groves," said Pavi. "You'll not ask the Company to guard them for the crown?"

"No. They are yours. Guard them or don't."

Madhavi had not raised a hand. She stood with her arms folded, and her voice, when it came, was flat.

"The fifth. Contract means pay. Lekha wasn't paid. Orma wasn't, nor Kani, nor Swathi. They died on that shelf because we voted to stand on it." She looked at the queen's chair and back. "If the crown can hire us to hold a pass, it can hire us to die on one. I'll not raise a hand for that."

Nobody moved. It was a fair thing to say, and Devan did not pretend otherwise.

"Then the clause changes," he said. "The Company holds the pass by contract, and no contract binds a member to stand in any place she hasn't voted to stand. The vote comes first, every time, the dead or the living. It goes in as written."

Madhavi studied him a long moment. "And if the crown calls it a refusal?"

"Then it's a refusal. That's what the word means."

She unfolded her arms. Thulasi, beside her, lifted a hand, and then Madhavi did, last and slowest.

Kuttu counted. He counted twice, because he did not trust his own thumb. When he was done he looked at Neela.

"All five," he said. "None cut. Fifth carried as amended." He swallowed. "Kamalakshi's hand was the last one up, and the highest."

Devan found his own throat unreliable. He had asked, and they had answered. It was the whole of the thing.

"Then I've a last matter," he said. "It isn't a term. It's mine, and I'd like it said in front of you."

Neela went still beside him.

Once he had stood in a hall and had a clerk write that he harboured his wife. That was a shield, and it had cost him a seal. This was a debt, and he meant to sign it.

"I am bound to Neela," he said, loudly, in the middle of the hall, to sixty people and a queen. "Behind her when she wants a back, beside her when she doesn't, and never in front of her like a wall. Justice Rema, I ask you to enter it on the court roll, and to enter me as a member of the Company. Not as anyone's warden. Devan, of the Company, bound to Neela, odiyan, by his own word and by hers if she gives it. Set it beside Asha's name, in the same ink."

Rema's pen stopped over the page. "That roll goes past the assize note, Devan. It goes to the high court. A member of the Company is party to every act the Company takes under its vote. Every contract it accepts, every blow it strikes. Your name will stand under all of it, and no one will be able to say you only watched."

"I know."

"You would have no office to shelter behind. The crown could answer any one of them on you."

"Then it's spent." He kept his eyes on her. "I want the Company's life. I do not want Kettamala. I will not go home to it if home means standing apart from her. My name goes on the roll, and it stays there."

The silence went on a moment longer than he could bear.

Then Neela stepped out from his shoulder, so that everyone could see her do it.

"I chose him," she said. Her voice shook once, and she let it. "On a rampart, with resin on my wrists. In the Resin House yard with a bear coming. I chose him in a fever, and I chose him with a bar across a shutter. I'm saying it by daylight, so nobody tells it differently." She took his left hand, the good one, and held it where the hall could see. "Enter it, Justice. I chose him, and I choose him again."

Rema wrote. The scratch of the nib was the only sound in the room. Then she sanded the line and turned the roll so the whole hall could see it.

The Company did not cheer. They were not people who cheered. Kamalakshi said, loudly, "About time," and Madhavi hit her on the arm.

Janu, in the doorway, had both fists in her apron and her chin up. Muthu looked at the ceiling.

* * *

"Majesty," said Rema, when the hall had settled. "The Company has voted its terms. Will you answer them?"

Nirmala looked at the table. At the hide strip and the green wax and the roll with Asha's name.

"One night," she said.

"Majesty—" said Haran.

"One night, Captain. I have not asked for much in thirty years that I did not take." She looked at Devan. "I have something to do tonight. I would rather do it alone, and be seen to have done it. I will answer at noon, by daylight, wherever this Company chooses to hear me."

"The old watch-house," Neela said, "on the shoulder above the narrows." She spoke at once. "It's empty, and it's ours to ask for. We want it for a hall."

"Noon," said the queen, "at the watch-house." She sat down again, rather suddenly, and put her good hand over the green case. Usha's fingers went to her own forearm.

Devan didn't ask what she meant to do. He thought he knew where her eyes had gone. They had gone to the case.

* * *

At dusk the court was cleared.

It took some doing. The garrison faced the wall, every man, on Haran's grudging word and Kandan's loud one. Bhaskaran stood by the gate with his horn unlifted. Muthu held the stair. Devan stood at the padippura with his back to the court and his face to the road, and Neela stood next to him in a coat that wasn't hers, a smudge of Thilak's ink still on her wrist above the bangle.

She had turned her back too. He had not asked it.

"You can't hear faces," she said, very low. "I can hear feet. I'll tell you who's coming. Names only. You'll know it was done, and you'll have seen nothing."

He kept his eyes on the road. It was an odd thing to be given: a witness's knowledge with the witnessing taken out. "Names only."

"Names only."

Behind them, a flask clinked. Resin has a smell like scorched tamarind, and he had hated it for twelve years. It no longer made his skin crawl. It smelled like the hour she came back to him.

"Lata," Neela murmured. Hard heels on the stones, a guard's boots, then a stop. "She's stopped at Usha."

"Usha," said a voice behind them.

"I'm here."

"I said no in the palace. I said it so the queen would know I meant it. I rode north anyway." A long breath. "I don't want it to be her hand. If it's anyone's, it's yours."

"You know what it is," Usha said. "I already bleed for her. This makes two. I'll bleed for you both, and you'll bleed for me."

A pause. Devan could not see it, but he could hear the small scrape of cloth being pushed back. He guessed Usha was looking at the dressed cut on her forearm, the one that had raised its twin on the queen.

"I'd rather it cost somebody," Lata said. "That's what it's for."

"Then I won't tell you it's light."

"Look at me. Don't look away when it starts."

"I won't," said Usha.

Then came the small sound of a woman changing, wet and fast, a catch of breath and the shuffle of a body going down. Above it rose Lata's voice, low and flat and perfectly clear. One word.

Usha let out a long, shaking exhale. It was the sound of a person taking a weight she had been afraid to take.

"I have it," Usha said. "Lata. I have it."

"Done," Neela breathed. "Both of them standing." Her shoulder was warm against his arm. She went on counting for him, a name at a time, as the last of the unbound came. "Sajan. He's come to the edge, and he's stopped. Sanu's watching him." A long scuff, then a boot turning back. "He's not stepping out. Not tonight." Devan didn't turn. "Kandan, close behind Sanu. Fists under his arms by the sound of his coat." Voices, one by one. A word. A breath answering. Madhavi crying. Kuttu telling someone, in a cracked voice, that it didn't hurt, it didn't, he swore it.

"Kamalakshi," Neela said last, and he heard her smile. "Straight line. She's walking it like a rope."

He heard Kamalakshi say, "Steward."

"What?"

"I want to do it to you."

A silence. Then Janu, in the voice she used for burned rice, "Me? I'm sixty-one."

"You fed the lot of us and didn't ask for anything after."

Another pause, longer. Devan could picture the apron, the fists in it.

"I'll bleed when you bleed."

"Yes."

"I'll feel it if you fall off a wall."

"I don't intend to fall off a wall."

"You've a face like a wall already," said Janu. "Stand there. No, there, in the light. I'm not squinting at you in the dark." Her voice thickened and went on without a break. "Yes. Yes, child, all right. Do it. Do it before I think better of it."

The change came, and the word, and a long, wavering breath. After it Janu drew in her own, once.

"Well," she said, after a time, in quite another voice. "That's that. And I'll tell you now, since we're bound, I've eaten your cairn-fire rice. It's a crime. Grey as a drowned rat. If I'm to bleed for you I'll not do it on that."

Someone laughed, a high cracked sound that was nearly a sob. Kamalakshi, he thought, and then he was sure.

The court breathed out. In the west the light had gone from the stair, and the single small flame at the foot of it stood steady, as it had at noon.

Neela's hand found his, the good one, and stayed. "That's all of them," she said. "You can turn."

He turned. She was already looking at him, her eyes bright.

Across the court, the north range had one window lit. A thin thread of smoke rose from it, too pale for a cookfire and too steady for a lamp.

"That's the queen's," Neela said.

"Yes."

"She said she'd do it alone."

"She said she'd be seen," said Devan. "Come on. Janu will want to feed us."

"She's going to be unbearable."

"Yes," said Devan, and for the first time in a long time he did not mind at all.
