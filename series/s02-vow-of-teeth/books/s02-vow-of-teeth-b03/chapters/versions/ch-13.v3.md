# Chapter 13: A Gift Is the Start of a Bill

Usha went down the cell stair three steps ahead and did not look back.

Neela counted anyway. One hundred and four steps cut into the cliff, wet at every turn, and the sea working at the rock somewhere below like a slow tongue. The green cuffs rubbed her wrists. The lime paste on her throat had dried tight enough to pull when she swallowed. She wore a guard's boots from Usha's locker, a size large, and she put each foot down flat so they would not slap.

At the bottom a lantern hung from a hook. Under it sat a turnkey with a bowl of rice and a face the colour of tallow.

"Mani," Usha said. "The new girl. She carries his supper tonight."

Mani looked at the sash on Usha's hip, then at the paste on Neela's throat. He had seen both on a hundred palace guards. He went back to his rice.

"Eighth cell. The lamp's lit. He won't give you trouble." He wiped his fingers on his knee. "He gives nobody trouble. It's the worst of him."

"I'll stand the turn above," Usha said, low. "Two knocks if anyone comes down. Don't be long."

"How long is long?"

"Sixty breaths longer than you think." Usha's mouth twitched. Then she went back up into the dark.

Neela took the bowl and walked the passage. Eight doors, plank and iron, five of them dark and open on nothing. Seaweed smell. Lamp oil. Under it, faint, a thing she knew in her sleep: warm sesame and burnt sugar.

Her stomach turned over once, like a fish.

She stopped at the eighth door. It stood ajar. She counted her own breath to four, and went in.

* * *

The cell was a cut in the rock, three paces by four. A cot, a bucket, a slit window with the sea behind it. A lamp on a ledge. And on the cot, with a grey blanket drawn to his chest, sat a man who had lost the softness he'd had at the Resin House.

Chathu had been round in the shoulder, and his hands had always moved as if smoothing a cloth. They still did. But the cloth had gone slack on him. His wrist bones stood out. His hair had been shaved close by someone who did not care for it, and it showed the shape of his skull.

He looked up and smiled with his whole face.

"Seventeen," he said. "Come where I can see you. My eyes are not what they were."

She stayed by the door with the bowl in both hands. "Neela."

"Neela." He tried it as if tasting. "It suits you. I did not know you would keep it."

"You'd have known if you'd asked."

"Yes, child. That is the oldest of my faults." He nodded at the bowl. "Put it down. Mani will listen for the dish, not for us."

She set it on the floor. Her foot found the cell's one dry spot and stayed there.

"You asked for me," she said. "Three days."

"And you came." He looked pleased and a little ashamed of looking it. "I said you would. I said it to the steward, I said it to the guard. I began to wonder whether I was only talking to myself."

"I didn't come because you said."

"No. You came because you want something." He spread his hands, palm up, in the old gesture of a man laying out his accounts. "I would not insult you by pretending otherwise. Ask."

She had planned this on the road and in the shed and on one hundred and four steps. A bargain had two sides. She would not let him set the other.

"There's a leaf," she said. "The queen's. Thirty pages. I want to know where it's kept."

"Ah." Not surprise. Something nearer a sigh. "And how to reach it, I imagine."

"The way. Nothing else."

"Nothing else," he said gently. "Very well."

He told her, without hurry and without being asked twice, in the soft, patient voice she had heard all her childhood across a long table.

"This passage ends at the water gate. The sea stair starts there and climbs the cliff, a hundred and ten steps, roofed against the spray. Halfway up, a landing where the rock bends. Your friend's guards stand it."

*Usha's landing.* Usha had called it the way from the lower cells to the water gate. It was one stair, named from its other end. Neela filed it as true, and filed beside it that Chathu knew where Usha's pair stood.

"At the top, a door of black wood in a passage lined with screens. The queen's room is the third door beyond. I sat in it four nights. She had me write at her table, by the window. Beneath the window, a low chest. Red lacquer, a bird picked out in gold. A farm lock, of all things. She keeps the leaf in it. You opened worse than that at fourteen, I recall."

Her skin crawled. *Of course he recalls.* Then the rest of it caught.

"Four nights. You've been in the cells longer."

"These four nights past, she has had me brought up each evening, and sent me down again before the lamps burned low."

"By the stair you've just described."

"With a guard before me and a guard behind." He tilted his head. "You are thinking it is a strange road for a prisoner."

She was. A caged man did not walk to the queen's table by the queen's back stair unless the queen's own hand had opened every door on it. The way in was her gift too. Neela turned that over and did not like either side.

"And the queen?"

"Sits audience in the long hall at noon. From the gun till the water-clock turns." A thin smile. "The gun is a mercy. It shakes the whole rock, and for the length of it a footstep on that stair is the least sound in Kodiyur. A patient woman might walk the last flight inside one report."

Noon.

It landed in her like a stone in a well. Three small stings in her thumb, and a man four days away sitting down on a rock to count them. If she climbed under the gun, she would be on a roofed stair with her hands full of wall. There would be no thorns. Devan would sit on his rock and wait for a word that did not come.

He would not know if it was a day lost or a death.

"You've picked the one hour I can't spend," she said.

"Have I?" Chathu's eyes were mild. "I only said when the queen sits."

"You know what I do at noon. Usha's friends carried it to you, or you guessed it. You always guessed."

"I guess a great deal, child. I am not always right." He drew the blanket higher. "But you will want the gun. You will want it badly, when the time comes. I only mention it."

She said nothing. He did not need her to.

* * *

She looked at the tin.

She had known it was there since the door. It sat on the cot beside his thigh, a round flat thing of dull brass, its lid dented at one edge where it had once been dropped on a flagstone. She knew the dent. She had run her thumb along it eleven times, in eleven rooms, the night before eleven deaths.

He saw her looking. He did not hurry. He took the tin on his knee and thumbed the lid, and it opened with the small flat sound she remembered, and the smell came up. Warm. Still warm.

Somebody had warmed it. In a cell with no fire. She did not ask who.

"You always pay your debts, child," Chathu said. "It is the best thing about you. I wish I had taught you nothing else."

"What do you want for it?"

"Take a sweet."

That was all. He held out the tin in both hands, the way a man offers water. Nothing in his face asked for anything.

She had learned around the fifth tin what it was. A gift is the start of a bill. Not a trap, which has teeth you can see. A gift has a soft mouth. It is warm in your hand and sweet in your teeth, and a year later it is a ledger line in a good hand: *one kindness, given.* You never remember agreeing. At the Resin House they had never needed to say *you owe us.* They gave you a sweet, and you did the arithmetic yourself.

In the kennels she had refused to touch the tin. She had thought the refusing was a victory. It had been a way of saying, *I am still afraid of you.*

She could walk out now with the route in her head and her hands empty. Chathu would let her. He would smile and say, *Of course, child,* and the debt would sit on her anyway, because he had given first. A refused gift was only an unpaid one.

If she took it, she would at least know the size.

*Let it be a thing I picked up,* she thought. *Not a thing sewn on me.*

She crossed the dry spot and put two fingers into the tin and took one.

It was a small round sweet, the size of a thumbnail. Jaggery, sesame, a bit of grated coconut. Her fingers came away shining.

His hand was close to hers as she lifted it, and she looked at his fingertips because she looked at everyone's hands. They were stained. Grey-brown, dry as dust, flaking in the creases. Not black. Not tacky.

She knew resin-ink. It was black as a beetle's back and it clung to skin for days. She had never seen Chathu's hands without it. This was only ink. Common ink, the sort a clerk uses for a petition.

She put the sweet in her mouth.

It was perfect. That was the thing she could not forgive. Sugar breaking, sesame going to oil, the warmth of someone's hands still in it. For an instant she was ten years old on a bench with her feet not reaching the floor, and a big gentle man was telling her she had done well.

She swallowed. It went down like a coin.

"There," Chathu said softly. "That is all."

"It isn't."

"No." His eyes were wet. He did not hide it. "But it is all I will ask tonight. Paper is the least of it, child. You will see. But you have asked for the paper."

* * *

She put the tin back on the cot with the lid shut, so that her hands had something to do.

"Your fingers," she said. "No resin-ink."

"No." He turned his hands over, regarding them with mild interest. "The cells are poor in everything but quiet. They allow me a pot of common ink, and a reed, and a plank to lean on. The queen asked me for the forty again."

Neela went still.

"She sent the steward with a list on the second day, in a clerk's hand. The forty names, and beside each a blank." He said it in the tone he used for sums. "She would like them written fresh. A master who saw a girl turn can always write her again. I said that to you once, without thinking what it was."

*A master who saw you turn can always write you again.* She had stood at his door at twelve, and he had mended Kirri's rat-eaten page in a quarter hour.

"And you said?"

"I have not answered her."

"Not yet?"

"Not yet." He folded his stained hands in his lap. "I find that I am not in a hurry. A very odd thing, at my age."

She waited for the rest. A man who is angling for pity says more. Chathu said nothing. He sat in the lamplight with his shaved head and his uneven left foot drawn up on the cot, bare and grey against the blanket, and he was so small that she could have put her hand round his throat.

"You could write them," she said. "You've done it before."

"I could." He considered it. "The queen is not unkind. She asks and does not command. It lets one go on thinking oneself a free man." He lifted his eyes. "I held every key at the Resin House, Neela. I held every cage in this world that mattered to me. And now I sit here and understand that I am the last cage I hold."

It went into her like a splinter.

She had come down a hundred and four steps expecting hatred, and she had it, it was all there. She remembered the tin in the kennel. She remembered the paddle coming down, and the resin burning on her throat. That man was real.

So was this one. The old man with his knuckles gone to string, who still folded his sweets in a tin.

Her hand had moved before she knew it. It had gone to the edge of the grey blanket, to draw it down over that cold bare foot. She stopped with the wool between her fingers. Her teeth hurt. She opened her hand and let the blanket fall.

*No,* she thought. *You do not get that.*

His eyes had followed her hand. He said nothing. That was almost worse.

"I wept for Ammu," he said quietly. "You should know it. I wept in this cell, the first night. I do not say it to be forgiven."

"Good," Neela said. "Then you know what it's worth."

He nodded as if she had paid him a compliment.

She turned to the door. Behind her the thing she had been avoiding was waiting, and she faced it with her hand on the cold iron.

She had taken a debt. For the first time since a stone and a pebble and a vow in a warm room, she had taken one alone, and she had told no one. It was a small debt, a sweet and a stair. She knew exactly how it would grow.

"The gun," she said, without turning. "You set the stair at noon on purpose."

"I set nothing, child."

"Then hear this, because you'll try to collect on it." Her voice came out flat. "If I climb under that gun, a man sits on a rock four days from here and gets no word. That's his cost, and it's yours to carry too. I'll say it to his face before I pay you anything. Not you. Me."

A pause behind her. When he spoke it was almost tender.

"Then you will have to say it soon," he said. "The queen sits every day."

She had her hand on the latch when she heard the boots.

* * *

No knock. That was the first wrong thing.

Usha had said two knocks. Neela listened, and the stair above sent down nothing but the slow beat of the sea and, under it, one set of boots coming down. Unhurried. Measured. A good leather sole.

And a light. A lamp swinging on a hook; she saw its yellow tongue lick the turn of the passage.

She moved before she thought: back across the cell, behind the open door, into the angle between the plank and the wall. The lamp ledge threw its glow in a half circle, and the dark behind the door was thick and bare. She put her shoulders to the rock. She stopped breathing in the way the guild teaches, by counting.

One. Two.

Chathu did not look at her. His hands lay folded on the blanket, and he looked at the open door with the mild, attentive face of a man waiting for his supper.

The boots came to a stop outside.

The steward stood in the passage with a lamp held at the height of his shoulder. Through the crack at the door hinge Neela saw a slice of him: a grey cloak, a clerk's round shoulders, a hand that held the lamp so steadily it might have been nailed there. He did not come in. He stood where the light reached the cot and no farther.

*Grey.* A man in grey who counted children onto a cart. A man in grey who counted a boy like a coin.

"Master Chathu," he said. A dry, careful voice, round at the edges. "I did not wish to wake you."

"I am awake."

"So I see." The lamp shifted a finger's width. The glow slid across the floor and touched the edge of the bowl of rice. "Has she come?"

Neela did not move. Her pulse knocked in her ears.

Chathu looked at the steward for a long moment. His face was kind. It was the face of a man who had told her that the kennel was a mercy.

"Not yet," he said.

The lamp did not move. The sea breathed in the rock.

"The queen would wish to know when," said the steward.

"When she comes," Chathu said gently, "you will know it."

A pause. Neela counted it. Four. Five. In the dark behind the door she could see one hand of the steward's, and the lamp, and the stained fingers of an old man laid in his lap, quite still.

She did not know.

He might have looked past her, and kept her. A man who sells a debtor does it with exactly that face. Or he might have said *not yet* because he had taken a sweet's worth of her side, and meant to see the bargain through. Both were Chathu. Both always had been.

The steward's lamp stayed where it was.
