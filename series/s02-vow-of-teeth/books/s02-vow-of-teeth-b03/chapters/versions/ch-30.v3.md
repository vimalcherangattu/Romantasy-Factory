# Chapter 30: Say It Anyway

Rema did not sit.

She came into the hall with the road still on her, mud to the knee and her case tied in oilcloth against her chest. Four green sashes stood behind her. She looked at the warden's chair, and at who was in it, and bowed to the queen exactly as far as the law asked.

"Majesty," she said.

"Justice," said Nirmala. "You have come a long way for a hall with no cushions."

"I have come with the high court's roll." Rema's voice was dry and quite steady. She had been a clerk for thirty years and spoke as if she were reading from a ledger. "It sat nine days ago. It entered Asha, daughter of the late warden of Kettamala, officer of the border, aged twenty-two, killed under contract. It entered the pass sales and the killing as one conspiracy, bought by the Keeper." She did not glance at the dead man's empty place. "The court cannot try whoever sealed the writ that paid the guild, because no one has laid it before the court. So it did the one thing left to it. It authorised me to record any charter the parties make. A true one, in daylight, with the witnesses present."

Devan stood by the table with his dead arm in its sash. His good hand was already at his coat.

Janu was in the doorway, with Muthu behind her. The Company had come in at Neela's back, thirty-odd of them, and stood along the pillars in tapper's wool. Captain Haran had stopped leaning on his pillar.

Devan thought: *say it anyway.*

Asha had written it on a scrap he found under a camphor block. He had kept it from Neela for weeks, because it was the one thing of Asha's he could not share. She had meant it for whoever found the ugly truth. He had spent six years being a man who found it and kept it in a box.

"Justice," he said. "I have a paper to read. Then I will give it to you."

He took the oilcloth bundle from inside his coat. He had lifted it out of the hollow behind the third slat at dawn, and it had ridden against his ribs since. The red thread came away without a knot. He laid the contents on the table: a strip of hide, cracked and dark, a folded sheet with a green seal, and a lump of cut green wax.

The queen did not move. Usha, at her shoulder, did.

"This is a contract chit," he said. "It is the buyer's half. It names the warden's chamber at Kettamala, death by beast, before the pass closes. It is in Master Chathu's hand, and I know the hand from a letter shown at a border school." He turned the sheet and held it where the window light fell. "And this is a payment writ. It is dated seven weeks before my wedding. It is payable to the Resin House on the warden's account. It is sealed in green wax with a sunbird, and the sunbird has a chip at the left wing tip."

He set the cut wedding-gift seal beside the writ. The chip matched.

"I wrote to the council eight weeks before the wedding," Devan said. "I said the raiders' tallies carried true wax and asked to see the seal registers. Within days, this was sealed. The woman whose wax it is sits in my chair."

He could feel the Company at his back like weather.

He knew what he was spending. Three hundred spears. The captains who had not yet decided to follow a man with one hand. The pass. He had told Neela the cost at dawn, plainly, because she asked for no softer word.

"I will not hold this over you, Majesty," he said, turning to the chair. "I have carried it on a hill for weeks and thought about how I might. I don't want a lever. I want it seen."

He walked the three paces to Rema and put the paper in her hand.

"For the court roll. In daylight. Enter it as read."

Rema took it as if it were hot. Her thumb found the chip in the wax. She looked at the queen, and for the first time her face did something other than record.

"Entered as read," she said.

* * *

Nirmala stood.

She did it without Usha's arm, which cost her something. The welt on her left forearm showed pale under the bandage where she had pushed back her sleeve. It was Usha's, shared, and she did not try to hide it.

"May I answer?"

"The hall is yours," Devan said. It was the first thing he had given her that was not evidence.

"I permitted the raids," said the queen. Her voice was warm and unhurried. It did not rise. "Limited, through controlled passes, in exchange for no fleet on the coast. I made that treaty with sea lords I did not respect, and I called it keeping the peace. I bought thirty children's pages from a man I knew to be a seller of children, and I called it an economy. I sealed this writ." She did not look at it. "I paid for your death, Warden. You had asked a question that led to my councillor's door, and my councillor's door led to mine."

Devan had heard liars confess. He had heard frightened men do it. He had never heard it done like a ledger being read aloud, one line at a time, by the person who kept it.

"I believed a realm is kept safe by what it never has to see," she said. "The widows saw the sums paid. The villages saw the smoke. I saw only that the harbour was whole." She took a breath that Devan watched her pay for. "The night the Thurai came up that cut with bronze caps and ladders, twenty-six of my own knives knelt in the wet, bleeding for strangers. A realm is not safe by what it has not seen. I held that it was. I find it isn't so. It is only blind."

She stopped. The hall let her.

"I have not finished being sorry," she said. "I only found out this week I could do it standing."

Devan looked at Neela. She was at his shoulder, a step to the side, and she was not looking at the queen. She was looking at him, and her fingers had found his sleeve and closed on it hard.

* * *

"Before this goes to the crown," Devan said, "it goes to the Company."

The queen's eyebrows rose a little. He didn't blame her. A man with one hand and no office had just told a monarch that her charter would wait on a show of hands.

"The Company owns itself," he said, to the pillars. "That is the first term. Nobody holds a page, a leash or a lease on any of you. You take contracts by vote, and you refuse them by vote. Second, the Blackbark groves go to the Company, under Pachan's map. Resin is yours to ration and nobody's to sell."

Kuttu made a sound halfway to a laugh.

"Third. The crown never holds a page again. Not the four it keeps, not the eleven it claims. They come to the people they name, or they burn." He looked at Nirmala. "Fourth. The crown recognises the Company in law. Every named member is under the charter, so none is rogue, and none may be hunted. Fifth. The Company holds the Monkey Pass for Varanad by contract. For pay, for a term, by vote, and with the right to say no."

He lowered his hand. "That's all of it. These are not mine to sign. Hands, if you want them. Nay, if you don't. If you'd cut a clause, say it."

"The groves," said Pavi. "You'll not ask the Company to guard them for the crown?"

"No. They are yours. Guard them or don't."

Thulasi asked whether *the pass by contract* meant they could be ordered back to the cut against their wishes. Devan said, "No. The contract says what you agree to. Anything past it comes back to a vote." She nodded and said nothing more.

Kuttu counted. He counted twice, because he did not trust his own thumb. When he was done he looked at Neela.

"All of them," Kuttu said. "All five. No one wanted a clause cut." He swallowed. "Kamalakshi's hand was the last one up, and the highest."

Devan found his own throat unreliable. He had asked, and they had answered. It was the whole of the thing.

"Then I've a last matter," he said. "It isn't a term. It's mine, and I'd like it said in front of you."

He felt Neela go still beside him.

For eight years he had said what he meant only in the dark, to a handful of people, and never once where a clerk's pen could take it down.

"I am bound to Neela," he said, loudly, in the middle of the hall, to sixty people and a queen. "At her back, and not her wall. Justice Rema, I ask you to enter it on the court roll, and to enter me as a member of the Company. Not as warden. Devan, of the Company, bound to Neela, odiyan, by his own word and by hers if she gives it. Set it beside Asha's name, in the same ink."

Rema's pen stopped over the page. "That roll goes past the assize note, Devan. It goes to the high court. A member of the Company is party to every act the Company takes under its vote. Every contract it accepts, every blow it strikes. Your name will stand under all of it, and no one will be able to say you only watched."

"I know."

"You would have no office to shelter behind. The crown could answer any one of them on you."

"Then it's spent." He kept his eyes on her. "I want the Company's life. I do not want Kettamala. I will not go home to it if home means standing apart from her. If the vote sends her west, I go west."

The silence went on a moment longer than he could bear.

Then Neela stepped out from his shoulder, so that everyone could see her do it.

"I chose him," she said. Her voice shook once, and she let it. "On a rampart, with resin on my wrists. In the Resin House yard with a bear coming. I chose him in a fever, and I chose him with a bar across a shutter. I'm saying it by daylight, so nobody tells it differently." She took his left hand, the good one, and held it where the hall could see. "Enter it, Justice. I chose him, and I choose him again."

Rema wrote. The scratch of the nib was the only sound in the room. Then she sanded the line and turned the roll so the whole hall could see it.

The Company did not cheer. They were not people who cheered. Kamalakshi said, loudly, "About time," and Madhavi hit her on the arm.

Janu, in the doorway, had both fists in her apron and her chin up. Muthu looked at the ceiling.

* * *

"Majesty," said Rema, when the hall had settled. "The Company has voted its terms. Will you answer them?"

Nirmala looked at the table. At the hide strip and the green wax and the roll with Asha's name. She seemed to be reading the room, line by line, as she had read herself.

"One night," she said.

"Majesty—" said Haran.

"One night, Captain. I have not asked for much in thirty years that I did not take." She looked at Devan. "I have something to do tonight. I would rather do it alone, and be seen to have done it. I will answer at noon, by daylight, wherever this Company chooses to hear me."

"The old watch-house," Neela said, "on the shoulder above the narrows." She spoke at once. "It's empty, and it's ours to ask for. We want it for a hall."

"Noon," said the queen, "at the watch-house." She sat down again, rather suddenly, and put her good hand over the green case. Usha's fingers went to her own forearm.

Devan didn't ask what she meant to do. He thought he knew where her eyes had gone. They had gone to the case.

* * *

At dusk the court was cleared.

It took some doing. The garrison faced the wall, every man, on Haran's grudging word and Kandan's loud one. Bhaskaran stood by the gate with his horn unlifted. Muthu held the stair. Devan stood at the padippura with his back to the court and his face to the road, and Neela stood next to him in a coat that wasn't hers.

"You don't have to turn your back," she said. "You're no one's witness here."

"I know." He kept his eyes on the road. "They won't have to say it in front of anyone who isn't theirs. It's a small thing to give."

She was quiet. Then her fingers tucked into his sleeve.

Behind them, a flask clinked. Resin has a smell like scorched tamarind, and he had hated it for twelve years. It no longer made his skin crawl. It smelled like the start of her mornings.

Lata went first. Devan heard her cross the stones in her hard-heeled guard's boots. He heard her stop.

"Usha," she said.

"I'm here."

"I said no in the palace. I said it so the queen would know I meant it. I rode north anyway." A long breath. "I don't want it to be her hand. If it's anyone's, it's yours."

"You know what it is," Usha said. "I already bleed for her. This makes two. I'll bleed for you both, and you'll bleed for me."

A pause. Devan could not see it, but he could hear the small scrape of a sleeve being pushed back. Usha was looking at the dressed cut on her forearm, the one that had raised its twin on the queen.

"Not a thing the queen wrote down for me," Lata said. "I'd rather it cost somebody. That's what it's for."

"Then I won't tell you it's light."

"Look at me. Don't look away when it starts."

"I won't," said Usha.

Then there was the small sound of a woman changing, wet and fast, a catch of breath and the shuffle of a body going down. And above it Lata's voice, low and flat and perfectly clear. One word.

Usha let out a long, shaking exhale. It was the sound of a person taking a weight she had been afraid to take.

"I have it," Usha said. "Lata. I have it."

Neela's grip on his sleeve went tight. He turned his hand and covered hers. Two women stood in a court. The queen's old claim on Lata was gone, and so was every other witness's. After tonight no voice but Usha's, joined to Lata's own, could shut that cage.

The last of the unbound came after that. Sajan stood a long moment at the edge of the court before he went, and Sanu watched him, and Kandan stood very close behind Sanu with his fists tucked under his arms. Devan didn't turn. Voices, one by one. A word. A breath answering. Madhavi crying. Kuttu telling someone, in a cracked voice, that it didn't hurt, it didn't, he swore it.

Kamalakshi came last.

He heard her walk a very straight line over the court. He heard her say, "Steward."

"What?"

"I want to do it to you."

A silence. Then Janu, in the voice she used for burned rice, "Me? I'm sixty-one."

"You're the only one who lit a lamp for her and didn't ask for anything after."

Another pause, longer. Devan could picture the apron, the fists in it.

"I'll bleed when you bleed."

"Yes."

"I'll feel it if you fall off a wall."

"I don't intend to fall off a wall."

"You've a face like a wall already," said Janu. "Stand there. No, there, in the light. I'm not squinting at you in the dark." Her voice thickened and went on without a break. "Yes. Yes, child, all right. Do it. Do it before I think better of it."

There was the sound of the change, and the word, and a long, wavering breath. After it Janu drew in her own, once.

"Well," she said, after a time, in quite another voice. "That's that. And I'll tell you now, since we're bound, I've eaten your cairn-fire rice. It's a crime. Grey as a drowned rat. If I'm to bleed for you I'll not do it on that."

Someone laughed, a high cracked sound that was nearly a sob. It was Kamalakshi, he thought, and then he was sure.

The court breathed out. In the west the light had gone from the stair, and the single small flame at the foot of it stood steady, as it had at noon.

Devan turned. He found Neela already looking at him, her eyes bright, a smear of resin still on her wrist and the bangle sitting just above it.

Across the court, the south range had one window lit. A thin thread of smoke rose from it, too pale for a cookfire and too steady for a lamp.

"That's the queen's," Neela said.

"Yes."

"She said she'd do it alone."

"She said she'd be seen," said Devan. "Come on. Janu will want to feed us."

"She's going to be unbearable."

"Yes," said Devan, and for the first time in a long time he did not mind at all.
