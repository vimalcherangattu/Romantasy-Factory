# Chapter 21: The Third Slat

Muthu was waiting at the foot of the north stair with a lamp in one hand and his other arm full of bedding.

"I've been told I snore," he said. "So I'm sleeping in the gate chamber tonight. Which is a kindness to everybody."

Neela looked at the bedding, then at him. He went quiet when he lied and loud when he joked, and he was neither now. He was just a big man holding a pillow like a shield.

"The warden's chamber," she said.

"It was always his. I only kept the bed warm." He held out the lamp. Sesame oil, full to the lip. "One night. Janu's changed the sheets and sworn at me while she did it. There's rice and curd on the sill and water in the kindi, spout toward the pillow." He stopped. "I didn't do that last part. She says that's how it's done."

Devan had come up behind her. She felt him there, a step back, as she'd asked.

"Muthu," he said.

"Don't." Muthu raised the pillow an inch. "The slat still creaks. I didn't fix it either."

He went down the stair, whistling something that wandered off the tune, and did not look back.

* * *

The room had not changed. The teak shutters faced west, closed and latched. The long bar hung on its hooks. The bed stood where it had always stood, and on the floor beside it lay a sword in its scabbard, hilt toward her side, because Muthu had been told once and had never asked why.

She counted the doors. Two. The little one by the hearth made three.

The council had run until the lamps burned low. Nobody would answer the queen until the queen had spoken in the open, at the gate, with the Company standing where she could see them. No vote on her offer had been taken. It was a delay. It was also a door held ajar. Dusk had come with the old pull at her throat, ten counts of burn in the scars, and gone. She was human. The ache would count the night against her. She let it.

Devan hung his pack on the peg and set the lamp on the table. The stitches pulled at him when he reached. She saw him hide the wince.

"Sit," she said. "Please. I'd like to say a thing before I lose it."

He sat on the edge of the bed. She stayed standing, because the telling wanted her on her feet.

"You asked me at the fire what I thought of the queen's offer. I gave the Company the reasons. I didn't give them the rest." Her bandaged hands were clasped before her. "I wanted it."

He said nothing. He watched her and waited, and the waiting let her go on.

"In the fish shed. She sat on a crate among the racks and asked if she might come in, because there was nothing to knock on. She was kind about it. She offered me thirty knives with no pages on them and a roof with no number on it, and I stood there and wanted it the way I want water after a turning." She heard her voice go flat and let it. "Command, Devan. Nobody over me. And nobody looking. That's the part I'm ashamed of. I could have been unseen again. Twenty-one years I answered to a number. It was safe. I missed it for an hour, and I hated that I missed it, and I came home and put my hand to the thorns and I still missed it."

"Is it gone?"

"No." She made herself look at him. "It's smaller. It's not gone."

He did not tell her it was nothing. He reached out and turned one of her bound hands over in his, palm up, and set his thumb on the strip of hem where the blister was. Not pressing. Just there.

"Thank you for telling me," he said. "I won't make it smaller than it is."

Nobody had ever said that to her.

"Now you," she said. "I know the answer you gave the queen. I don't know the one you gave yourself."

His jaw worked. He looked at the lamp.

"Muthu asked me on the shelf if it hurt," he said. "To refuse her. I said no. That was a lie, and he knew, and he let me have it." He drew breath. "It hurt more than I let him see. Her letter said *Kettamala restored,* and I stood on the shelf and smelled the pepper coming down the cut and I wanted it back. All of it. My father's chair. Asha's door. The shutters open for the air, the way he kept them. I wrote my refusal on the lid of an arrow cart, and my hand didn't shake, and then I went and hit a post for an hour."

"You didn't tell me."

"I told you I wouldn't dress it up as sparing you. I left out that it cost me." His thumb moved on her palm. "I wanted the fort. I still do. I would trade it again for what I got. Both are true."

She sat down beside him, close enough that the bed gave under them. "That one's easier to carry than the other."

"Which other?"

"The one where you're only noble."

He laughed. It came out short and cracked, and it startled him. She leaned into his shoulder and felt it in her own ribs.

"I won't take the command," she said. "That's settled. But I won't refuse it in a corner either, the way I'd refuse a tin of sweets. She hears my reasons in the open, with the Company standing there. If she's wrong, she can be wrong where people hear."

He nodded slowly. It was a smaller thing than a vow, and it settled in her like one.

* * *

She did not remember who moved first. She remembered undoing the strips of hem from her own hands, one wrap at a time, and his hands taking over when her fingers fumbled. The raw skin of her palms shone in the lamp. His matched it. He turned them side by side and studied the pair for a long moment.

"I'm twenty-seven," she said, because she liked to say it plainly, and because it felt like part of the asking. "You're thirty-two. We're both grown, and nobody here is paying for either of us."

"No one." His voice had gone low. "Neela, if you want to stop, you say. Any time. No reason."

"I know." She laid her cheek against his scarred knuckles. "Leave the lamp."

He went still.

"Keep it lit," she said. "I don't want the dark tonight. I want you to look at me. All of it. The whole time." She swallowed. "I've been seen in a hundred rooms by people who owned the seeing. I want to choose this one."

He took a breath that shook. "I'll look."

"Then do."

His hands came to her face first, careful of the blisters, and he kissed her like a man learning a path. She pulled at his shirt. He lifted his arms so she could drag it over his head, and the lamp showed her what the shelf had cost him. The long ladder-hook cut across his back, dressed and still pulling. On his right ribs, nine stitches of waxed silk, black and fresh, the skin round them angry. Beside them ran the old white seam from the ford.

She put her mouth to the old seam first. Then, slowly, past the stitches, as lightly as she could manage. The ache came anyway, a dull hot line under her own right arm, where the same hook had torn her in a room in Kodiyur. He made a sound she felt in her chest.

"Does it hurt?" he said.

"Both of us. Don't stop."

"I won't." His voice was rough. "Tell me if it's too much."

When he bared her throat, he stopped. The scars lay there, pale ridges under the lamplight. He looked at them the way he looked at the pass from the wall, and then he put his lips to each in turn. She had once let a man see them as a lie. She let him see them now as a fact, and she did not look away.

"Look at me," she said again.

"I am."

He was. His eyes did not leave her face as he laid her back on the bed that had held the worst night of her life and the best. His hand went under her mundu, slow, a question, and she answered it with her whole body. She reached for him, blistered palms and all, and the burn of her own hands bloomed across his skin at the same moment, and he gasped. Then warmth came through it. The wound-share carried that too, she had learned: a heat in her that she felt twice, once in herself and once in his shoulders, bright and shared.

"You feel that," he said, roughly.

"Both ways."

"Both ways."

It slowed after that. He moved above her, and the lamp showed her his face, the grey in his hair, the line between his brows, which eased and came back and eased. She watched him watch her. She did not close her eyes. When the pleasure rose in her it rose in her face, open, helpless, undefended, and he saw it. She felt him see it.

She said his name. He said hers. Not the number. Not the cover. Her own, the one she had chosen, with the other one folded behind it and still unsaid.

The bed creaked under them. The slat did not.

* * *

Afterwards she lay with her head on his good side and the lamp burning down. His breathing slowed, and she counted it, as she had counted it since the first week of her marriage, and only lately told him why.

One, two. The hitch came a third of the way through the long count, soft and sure. She counted again to be sure, and it came again.

"You're counting," he said, into her hair.

"You're awake."

"I'm lying very still so you can." His arm tightened a little. "Is it right?"

"It's always right."

On the sill by the shutters sat a grey river pebble in the dust, exactly where he had left it. Muthu had not moved it. Janu had cleaned the sill around it. She looked at it a long time in the thin, steady light.

She slept.

* * *

She woke before the first clank of the chain. The lamp had burned out. Grey light lay under the shutters. Devan was asleep on his back, one hand open on the sheet between them, and she watched him for a while without counting anything.

Then she got up, wrapped a cloth around her, and went to the third shutter on bare feet.

The housing of the slat gave under her thumb with its dry creak. He did not stir. A piece of the frame swung in on its hidden pin, and the hollow behind the third slat opened, no wider than a hand.

The hide strip of the contract chit lay inside, with the warden's chamber and *death by beast* in a hand she knew. Beside it lay the gift seal cut from the sandalwood box, green wax, a sunbird with a small chip at the left wing. Raiu had died of the first. Ammu had died of what came after. In the grey they looked very small.

There was room for a third thing, and the third thing was not there.

"It's all there," Devan said behind her. "Except one."

She had not heard him wake. He was propped on one elbow, hair on end, his eyes already clear.

"The writ's in your pack."

"Since the hill." He swung his legs over the bed's edge, and she saw him catch his breath at the stitches. "I took it out of here the night we left for the shelf. The chit and the seal can sleep in a wall. That I wanted against my ribs."

He crossed to the peg and took the oilcloth from the pack, tied with its red thread. He knelt at the shutter beside her and unwrapped the writ. Folded small. Dated seven weeks before her wedding. The queen's green wax, the sunbird, the chipped wing.

"Where it lived," he said, and laid it in the hollow beside the chit and the seal while she watched.

The camphor block sat at the back, a grey cube that kept the insects off the papers. It stood crooked, and when he moved it to square the writ, something small showed underneath. A scrap of paper, folded twice, gone soft as cloth.

He went still. Then he unfolded it with the tips of his fingers, as though it might bleed.

"I know this hand," he said. His voice had gone careful. "She wrote my letters for me. Every one, for years. Her sevens had a bar through them."

"Asha."

"Asha." He held it to the grey light and read it aloud, plainly, because he had told her once that he would hide nothing. "*Say it anyway.*" He turned it over. Nothing on the back. "It was under the block, in the hollow. I've never found it. I didn't know she used this place."

She did not take it from him. She laid her bare hand over his, over the scrap, and they stayed that way until the chain clanked.

"She'd have said it to the queen's face," Neela said.

"She'd have said it to the queen's face in a market, and been right about the price of pepper." His mouth moved, not quite a smile. He folded the scrap and laid it back under the block, exactly as he had found it. "I'm telling you where it is, so it's not mine alone."

Then he lifted the writ again, and held it, and looked at the sunbird.

"I'll read this aloud," he said. "Before the queen. In daylight, at the gate, with the Company and the garrison and the village standing there. The whole of it, her wording, her wax."

"With three hundred spears at her back."

"With three hundred spears." He did not reach for comfort. He gave her the answer as he gave her everything, flat and whole. "Under captains of her household who have never met me. On our side, ninety-eight. Forty-two odiyans. Twenty-two of her own riders, who may not hold when she's named. Forty garrison volunteers. That's a hundred and four, less six in the ground: Biju, Sankaran, Chami, Rajan and two more. Fourteen hundred Thurai wait beyond the cut. If one captain lifts a spear when I finish, we fight at our own gate with the host at our backs, and we lose the pass. If none lifts, I've still named her in front of the only men who can hold the cut beside us. Some will not forgive me. Some will go home."

"So it might cost us the spears."

"It might cost us the spears and the pass with them. I think that's likely. I'm telling you so you don't find out at noon."

It was no comfort at all. She found, oddly, that she could stand on it.

"Then read it," she said. "I won't ask you which it is. Rope or light."

"I don't know which it is yet." He said it to the writ. "I'll know when I see her face."

She folded the oilcloth over it. Her hands knew the knot. Then she shut the hollow and the slat gave its small dry creak under her thumb.

"Mine is the other half," she said. "I'll refuse her in the open. That's settled. What isn't, is whether I say at the gate what I am."

He turned his head. She watched him decide not to speak.

"You told me last night you'd say nothing of it unless I did," she said. "I don't know yet if I will. The queen has the shape of me from Usha. She hasn't heard it from my mouth, before the Company, in daylight. It might cost me more than the fort cost you. It might be the only thing that makes the refusal mine."

"Do you want me to ask which?"

"No."

"Then I won't." His good hand came to rest against the shutter beside hers. "I'll stand there, not knowing, until you open your mouth or you don't. That's the cost, isn't it? Not knowing."

"That's the cost."

"I can pay it."

Down in the court the cistern chain clanked, and the first cocks answered. She crossed to the sill and picked up the grey pebble, cold and light in her scarred palm, and held it out to him.

"Keep it in your hand, not your cheek," she said. "I want to see you hold it. And not put it in your mouth."

He closed his fingers round it. He did not lift it.
