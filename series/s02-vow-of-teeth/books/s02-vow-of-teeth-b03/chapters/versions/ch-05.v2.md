# Chapter 5: The Lightning Boulder

Corporal Kandan came over the wall without leave, and he brought nine men to prove it.

Devan heard them before he saw them, ten sets of boots on the cart road above the boulder. They did not hide. They came down out of the dark with spears held crosswise and the beacon burning orange on their faces. Kandan walked in front, the scar through his brow bright as a split in wood.

Devan waited in the road with a borrowed spear and no seal. He felt every inch of that.

"Sir," Kandan said.

"Not tonight." Devan kept his voice level. "Muthu didn't send you."

"Muthu told us to sit on the wall. We sat on it till the beacon went. Then we got down off the other side." Kandan's jaw worked. "He'll be shouting by now. Then he'll be sorry he can't join us."

"I won't order you to stay."

"I know you won't. That's why we came."

Behind Devan, the scree shifted with a long, soft, shingling noise, like rain on a roof of loose slate. Thirty-six shapes lay along the slope above the lightning boulder, low and still, their eyes catching the fire. They had slipped behind the rocks while the nine were still on the stair. Devan had asked them first, in the lower court, and every hand had gone up before he finished the question. At his right shoulder stood a spotted shape with a long tail that moved once, slowly, against his knee.

He didn't look at it. He looked at the nine.

"I'll say what I mean to do," he said. "Then you'll tell me yes or no. I'll take no. Anyone who goes home tonight goes home with my thanks."

Kandan's eyes flicked past him to the scree and came back. He said nothing. He had called Neela a witch to her face once, at a barracks door, the week Raiu burned. Devan did not ask him to take it back.

He drew the gorge in the dust with the spear butt. "Twenty-odd men, an advance guard. They came faster than I thought. I was wrong about three days. There's a picket where the cart road narrows, here. And somewhere along the lip a man holds a bronze mirror. At first sun he answers the ships, and the ships learn the road is open."

"Somewhere?" Sankaran said.

"The lip is half a mile of scree. That's too long a front to search before dawn, even for them." He glanced up the slope. "If the mirror stays dark, someone out there has to decide whether to land the rest into a gorge that's gone quiet. It buys a day. I need a day."

"And if he flashes?" Vasu asked. He was the young one with ducks for a mother. Devan knew that because Neela had written it on a roof tile, and he had read the tile twice.

"Then we've lost the pass. So ten of you hold the road at the cut, with spears, and nobody chases past the boulder. I want one of them alive, to talk."

"You want us to hold a road while they go up a hill," said Sankaran. "Alone."

"I want you to hold it with me."

Nobody spoke. On the hill the beacon popped and threw a spark. Vasu's lips moved. He was looking south, down the coast, toward Kalladi and a woman with ducks.

"Behind me are things you were told not to look at," Devan said. "They're on your side. Face the road. If you turn round, you may carry what you see for the rest of your life, and so may they. I'd rather you never did."

Kandan stared at him. Then he grounded the butt of his spear in the road and said, quite slowly, "Raiu held the stair at your door."

"Yes."

"You sent him for oil."

"Yes."

"I'd have asked you why, if you'd come back to the wall to be asked." Kandan's mouth twisted. "I'll hold your road."

One at a time, the others grounded their spears. Sankaran. Kuttan, who could not write and had had a letter written for his mother. Vasu, last of the three he knew, and then six more. Devan said each name aloud as the butt struck stone. He had never learned the names of ten men in an hour before. He had a woman with a lamp-black plank to thank for it.

Kandan turned his back on the scree. The rest followed.

The bear had come down the slope to Devan's left side. It was breathing through its nose.

* * *

The picket stood where the road pinched between the rock and the drop. He was a short man in a dark coat with one hand on a fire-basket he had not lit, and he looked toward the beacon, not toward the boulder. They had not yet learned to look behind.

Devan walked out of the dark into the orange light with the spear low, where the man could see it.

"Kettamala holds this road," he said, in the coast tongue every trader half-spoke.

The picket's head came round. His hand left the basket and went to his hilt.

Eight years. It was a long time to keep a rule. In that time Devan had stood on a wall and given orders, and watched men he sent do the thing he would not. He had sworn on the west stair never to hold a throat. He had no oath about this, and that was the worst of it. There was only the spear in his hands, and the road, and the choice.

He knew a hundred places on a man's body. He knew the door under the skull, and the notch below the ear. He turned the point away from every one of them.

The thrust went in under the collarbone, high and to the side, where it would bleed and hurt and kill nobody if the man was lucky. It pinned the coat to the rock behind him. The picket cried out, short and furious, and Devan let go of the shaft before the man's knife came up.

It came up anyway, from a second man he had not seen, crouched in the lee of the basket. The edge went across Devan's right forearm, a hot thin line. He took the second man's wrist in his left hand and turned it until the knife dropped, and the bear came out of the dark past his hip like a landslide.

It did not kill. It put the man down and stood on him. That was all.

Devan stood with blood running into his palm and the picket pinned beside him, groaning. He had struck first, openly, on a stranger's road. The first time since the ford he had struck first, with nobody's life on the edge of it but his own choice. He felt it down to the soles of his feet, and what he felt was not shame.

Behind him, Kandan said, hoarsely, "That was clean."

"It was meant to be."

The bear's flank pressed warm against his left side. The thing that had killed his sister, and Raiu, kept the dark off him. Devan did not move away. He had decided that on the hill above the Resin House, with his back turned and a word in his mouth. Deciding it again was harder.

* * *

The bear's head swung toward the thorn before Devan heard anything. A low rumble ran through its ribs.

"You've taken the door man," said a voice from the scrub. "That's the smaller half of the work."

Every spear came round. Devan raised his hand, and they stopped.

The man who came out of the thorn was old and bent, in a grey wrap, with a staff he did not seem to need. He kept to the edge of the firelight and held his empty hands out from his sides. He smelled of woodsmoke and dried fish.

"Koman," he said. "From the village above the gorge. I walked the hill edge for the Resin House eleven years, reading the posts of those who weren't mine to name. The House has not paid me since the wood fell, two moons back." He sniffed, a long dry dig of the nose. "My nose is nobody's. I thought I might offer it to somebody."

Devan looked at him. A watcher. The man who had logged Neela's bare nights and carried them to Chathu. He could say it, and it would be fair. He could also see a thin man who had stood in the rain for two moons with nothing coming, and who had walked down a dark road to a stranger's spear.

"What does it tell you?"

Koman turned his face up into the wind off the coast. His eyes were half shut. "Twenty-three men, counting the two you hold. Twenty below the bend, sweating the long sweat of men who've marched three days. Fish, tar, unwashed wool." He stopped. "One more, not on the road. Up the lip, left of the cut. Still too long. Cold sweat, and brass. A man holding a thing he's afraid to drop."

"Your mirror," Vasu breathed.

Devan had not known where. Now he did. "What do you want for it?"

"The House's wage. Seven panam to the moon." The old man smiled thinly. "I do not expect it."

"You'll have it. From my pocket, not the crown's. I've no office to pay you from, so it's my word." Devan held out his hand, palm up, cut and bleeding. "It's cheaper than coin."

"I've found it holds better," Koman said, and put his dry hand in the blood and shook.

* * *

The spotted shape at Devan's right had already turned its head up the scree. He looked at it. "Left of the cut," he said. "Alive, if you can."

The leopard went up into the black, and the rest of the slope went with her.

Devan could not see them. He could hear them: the slide of loose stone, a pad of paws, then nothing. A good deal of nothing. He stood in the road with Kandan at his right and the dead basket cold in front of him, and counted. The sky over the pass went from black to the colour of wet slate.

The mirror was the whole matter. At first sun it would flash. At first sun, too, every odiyan on that hill would stop being what she was, in plain sight of anyone who looked up. He had thought that through at the boulder, and he'd asked no one to hurry. They knew.

There was a short grunting cry from the lip. A clink of metal on stone. Then a hush, and it went on too long.

Devan found that he had stopped breathing. He started again.

Nothing flashed. The east went from slate to pearl, and no light struck back from the cliff. A little while later a soft chuffing came down off the scree, and a scrape, and a leopard came out of the shingle with something wrapped in leather held in her jaws. She walked up to Devan and set it at his feet.

It was a bronze disc, the size of a plate, wrapped in oiled hide. He turned it over. On the back, scratched small and neat beside the handle, was a bird with its wings folded.

He did not know what he was seeing. He put his thumb over it, and put the mirror in his belt.

The leopard sat down against his leg. Her flank was shaking. The sky behind her turned gold at the edge.

"Face the road," Devan said. "All of you. Now."

* * *

They came down out of the cold light on two legs, in the cloths he had brought on Kuttu's packhorse and left wrapped behind the boulder. He heard the rustle and the muttering and Meenu's laugh, shaky and surprised. He did not turn his head. Kandan kept his eyes on the road and the veins stood in his neck.

When Devan did turn, it was because he heard a heavy tread on rock.

Vellan sat on the lightning boulder, wrapped in a grey blanket, his bad leg stretched out and his big hands hanging between his knees. His face was grey and old and very tired. He was looking at Devan, who had not asked him to look.

"The lad on the road," Vellan said. "Your corporal. He's the one who stood with Raiu."

"Yes."

"I could smell it on him. The grief." The big man's mouth shifted. "I'll not ask him to forgive me. I'd not ask you."

"Don't."

"No." Vellan rubbed his jaw. The gorge wind lifted the edge of his blanket. "She said something, your sister," he said. "At the end."

Devan went cold from the crown of his head to his heels.

He had waited six years for any word. He had a drawer of notes in a camphor chest and a slate with Unni's name on it. He had never had her voice, her last sentence, the one sound that was not in a box. And the man who had it was sitting on a rock in a blanket.

"Say it," he said. His voice did not sound like his.

"When I've earned saying it."

"You don't get to set a price on my sister's words."

"I'm not setting it on you." Vellan's eyes did not waver. "I'm setting it on me. I'll tell you when I've earned the right, and not before. If I'm dead first, I'll find a way."

Devan stood with his fists shut. He could have forced it. There were a dozen ways to force a man. He opened his hands.

"Then earn it," he said, and turned his back, because that was the only answer he could give.

* * *

His forearm had opened while he wasn't thinking. The sleeve was dark and stiff to the wrist.

"Sit," said Neela.

She had come up beside him in a cloth the colour of dust, her hair wet at the temples. She had a new bruise on her cheekbone and a look he knew, tired and amused and furious all at once. She held out her right arm. Along the outside of the forearm, red and raised and weeping at the edge, ran a thin shallow line.

"Third thing I felt on the lip," she said. "I had the mirror man's wrist in my teeth when you took that knife. I nearly dropped him."

"You didn't."

"I nearly did." She dropped to her knees on the stone and took his arm. "Sit, Devan."

There were ten men within five paces. Kandan had half turned. Vasu was staring, Sankaran had an elbow in Vasu's ribs, and old Koman leaned on his staff with his head tilted like a bird's. Devan had been taught how to take a wound. Take it alone. Wrap it behind a door. Let nobody see you need the cloth.

He sat down on the stone.

Neela tore a strip from the hem of her cloth. She cleaned the cut with water from Kandan's gourd. Kandan had held it out without being asked, and she took it without comment. Her hands were quick and rough and did not tremble. She wound the strip twice and tied it, and her thumb pressed once, hard, on the knot.

"It will stay," she said.

"It will."

She looked up, and not at him. She looked past his shoulder at the nine, one after another, with the face she wore when she counted a room. He watched what they did. Kandan's eyes were on the knot. Vasu grinned like a fool. Sankaran looked at his boots, then at the wall, then back. Not one of them looked away from her.

She saw it. He saw her see it. Something in her face loosened.

"Done," Neela said, and stood.

* * *

The man from the road sat propped against the rock with a rag stuffed under his collarbone, grey-faced, glaring. Koman had named him in a murmur: a sergeant, going by the cord at his shoulder. Devan crouched in front of him with the bleeding arm resting on his knee.

"You'll live," he said, in the coast tongue. "If the wound is kept clean. I'd like to know what you came for."

The sergeant spat. Then he looked at the bandage, and at the woman standing at Devan's shoulder, and at the old man with the staff. Perhaps it was the fact that nobody had yet hit him again.

"Cutting-saws," he said at last, thick-tongued. "Mortar lime. Barley for seed. Not for burning."

"For what?"

"For the hills." The man shifted. "The admiral's orders were plain. Take the pepper hills and hold them through the dry season. Not strip them. *Hold* them. We were told to leave the vines standing, and the villages that gave way."

Devan did not move. He had spent three weeks bracing for a raid, for fire on the coast and a quick hard push up the gorge. A raid came and went. It went back down to the ships with what it carried.

This one had come with seed.

"Hold them against whom?" Neela said quietly, in the same tongue.

"Against whoever comes." The sergeant looked up at her. "Your queen is a sensible woman. We were told she would not come."

Nobody on the road said a word. Kandan's face had gone white under the scar, and behind him the first sun cleared the east wall and fell full on the stones.

"Then they don't go home in spring," said Vasu, very small.

Devan looked down the gorge to the sea. It was forty paces wide at the mouth. He had ten men, an old nose, thirty-six who changed with the dark, and a day.
