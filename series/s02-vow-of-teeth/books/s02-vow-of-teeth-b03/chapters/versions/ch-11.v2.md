# Chapter 11: Ask Me Again

The first ladder came out of the grey at the hour Neela's horse would be reaching the third bend of the hill road.

Devan saw it from the lip. It rose out of the scree below the shelf, lashed ship-timber with a hook of black iron at the head, and it swayed there until the hook bit the stacked stone of the wall. Behind it came another, and another. Along the whole slope men bent under timber, and the grey showed their bronze caps and the white of their eyes.

Three hundred, by the count he had made through the night. Seventy-five on the lip, now that Biju was gone.

"Hold," he said. He said it once, flat, the way he had said it to eleven riders in single file under this same shelf in the spring. He did not shout. The men near him heard it and passed it on.

Kandan's volunteers held the right of the wall with their spears grounded and their faces set. The Company held the left, the odiyans in daylight cloth with spears in their hands. They were thirty-six now, all of them human at this hour, and none of them looked like what the night made of them. Thulasi had a salt-boiler's wrists and a stance she had learned from Kandan two days ago. Vellan stood at the far end where the wall bent, dragging his left leg a half step behind him, in a grip that had nothing of the kalari in it. It had been learned somewhere harder.

The hook bit. A man's head rose over the stone.

Devan took the spear in both hands and put the point through the gap under the man's arm. It was a clean thrust, and the man went back off the ladder with the weight of the whole line behind him. He did not look to see where he fell.

The spear was a wall's weapon. It gave him a length of ash between himself and everything that wanted him. His right hand stayed on the shaft, the two fingers that could kill a man in a breath folded into the grip like a pair of knives he had decided not to draw. He had not drawn them in eight years. Not since the captain at the ford. Not now.

The second thrust took a shoulder. The third was parried, and the parrying blade came sliding down the ash and over his left forearm. It was a light stroke, almost polite. Heat ran along the skin, from wrist toward the elbow, and beaded.

He knew exactly where she felt it.

An hour out, he thought. The loops of the road, wet and shining. She would have pushed back her sleeve. She would not turn her head. She would count the bends.

He drove the butt of the spear into the man's face and the blade fell away.

"Left!" Kandan's voice, hoarse. "Left, they're over at the bend!"

Devan did not look. He took the next ladder on the point of the spear and set his shoulder to the haft and pushed. The ladder went over backward with six men on it, slowly, like a tree.

Somewhere under all of it he was saying: If you bleed, I won't.

He kept the line.

* * *

At midday the line bent.

He felt it before he saw it, through the soles of his feet and the drag in the men on either side of him. The Thurai had stopped trying the whole wall. They had found the elbow where it turned at the east end, the place Vellan held, and they were stacking men against it as water finds the low place in a bank.

Two hundred and sixty down, perhaps. Devan had stopped counting the dead and counted the living. Sixty-one on the lip that could still lift a spear. Of the Company, three had come off the wall with arrows in them and been carried back to the sheds. Kamalakshi was among those who had not. Her white face turned his way and then turned back. She was not forgiving anyone yet. She was fighting.

He did not think the line would break. He did not think it would hold. Ask him for a number and he would have said a quarter hour, and he would have been wrong either way, and the men would have known he had said it.

"Warden!" Thulasi called it from close by. She was bleeding at the temple and did not seem to know it. "They're through at the corner."

"I see them."

He went. He did not run. He put the spear between two men who were not ready for it, and for a long time there was nothing but the push and the grunt and the hot red breath of someone's face an arm's length off.

Then, behind and above, the horn.

It was Kettamala's horn. He would have known it with his eyes shut: a long plain note, no code in it, the rally call that Bhaskaran blew at festival gates and at funerals alike. It came down the cut from the fort, a mile of rock throwing it back, and it did not stop. It went on and on, until it seemed a bad horn-player might simply be leaning on the thing out of spite.

The Thurai heard it. A ladder-man at the corner raised his head like a dog.

Down the cart road came thirty men in grey. They came at a jog in good order, spears level, and at their head ran Captain Muthu with his sword out and his mouth open, bellowing.

"Make way! Make way, you lot, escort coming through! Refugees!"

He ran straight into the gap at the elbow, where Vellan stood. He put his shoulder against a Thurai spearman and shoved. Behind him the thirty went into the line like a wedge into a log.

Devan said afterward that he did not remember how the next quarter hour went. He remembered Muthu's voice above it, bawling the same word over and over, with perfect joy.

"Refugees! Refugees on the shelf road! Mind the refugees!"

By the time the sun stood two hands past noon, the ladders had gone down to the scree and stayed there. Bronze caps went away along the slope, many of them dragging one another. Nobody on the lip cheered. They sat down where they were.

Muthu came over wiping his sword on a dead man's sash. His grey tunic was black from collar to belt. He had a cut across his chin and seemed delighted by it.

"There are no refugees," Devan said.

"It's in the post book." Muthu squatted beside him and looked at the line of living and dead with a face that had lost some of its roundness. "Black wax, plain ink, my hand: 'Captain Muthu sends thirty men down the shelf road to escort refugees from the pass villages.' The crown said the garrison stays inside the walls. It said nothing about what comes out of them to walk a woman and her goat home." His eyes were on the dead. "A pen is a good shield, my friend. Refugees are the reason I can write it down. If I'd written 'to fight,' I'd have had to hide the page."

"You'd have been hanged for the page."

"I'm still the one holding the seal." He grinned, a short mean grin. It faded. "I counted thirty of our men on the road up. I'd like to count them again later and not like the number."

"Bhaskaran?"

"Holds the wall with every cook who can lift a pole. Janu's got the pot on. I told her to feed anyone who looks like a refugee." Muthu got up with a grunt and held out his hand. Devan took it and was hauled to his feet. "And you owe me a seventh-day round."

"It isn't the seventh day."

"It's the seventh day somewhere."

Devan nearly laughed. He found that his face had forgotten how, and all he did was breathe out through his nose.

"After the pass," he said.

"I'll hold you to it," said Muthu. "Out loud. In front of the cook."

* * *

At the evening count, three volunteers were dead.

Kandan brought the names to him on a broken roof tile, chalked in his own square hand, because the tile was what Neela had left in the barracks and the men had taken to it. Devan read them standing on the shelf with the last light on the stone.

Sankaran. Chami, who had been a drover. A boy from Kalladi called Rajan who had arrived with a spear taller than himself.

"Sankaran took one at the corner," Kandan said. "Before the horn. He was in front of Vasu. I don't think he saw it come."

"His mother's at Neerattu."

"She came back to the village after the husband died." Kandan did not look up from the tile. He still had not forgiven the warden for the order he'd defied, nor for being right about it. But he said, in a low even voice, "You'll write her. I'd write her myself, but my hand's a sergeant's. It goes to the point and stops."

"I'll write it."

He wrote it that night in the stone sentry hut at the back of the shelf, with a lamp and a board across his knees. He had Asha's trick of the board in his head and none of her hand. She had written his letters for him from the age of fourteen. He had said what he meant, in six words or less, and she had dressed it in a clerk's cloth and sent it off, and nobody had known it was Asha speaking from behind him.

Now there was only the stick of charcoal and a clean leaf.

*To the mother of Sankaran, at Neerattu.*

He looked at that for a long time.

*Your son held the corner of the wall on the second day. He was in front of the man behind him. He did not suffer long. I did not know him as well as I should have, and I will say what I do know. He carried water for the others without being told. He laughed at the goat. He wrote his name large.*

He scratched out *large* and put *plainly*. He scratched out the next line, which began *He was a brave*, because Sankaran would have laughed at that too.

*I am sorry. I cannot give him back. The wall held because he stood where he stood. That is the whole of what I have.* He signed it with his name and no title, since he had none now. He sat with the leaf on his knee and thought that Asha would have fixed every line and made it worse.

He folded it small and gave it to Kandan for the next rider.

* * *

At noon the next day he was on the lip, and the sun stood straight above the shelf.

He felt it before he could name it, a small bright sting on the ball of his left thumb. Then another, and another.

He stopped. He stopped in the middle of an order, with the words half out. The men near him went on looking at the scree, because a warden stopping was not worth a look. He sat down on the nearest rock, a low flat boulder lichened over, and turned his hand up on his knee.

Three red beads, one beside another. Small, neat, a hand-careful row. They were thorn-sized, from a bush by a kitchen well.

Yesterday's noon had come and gone inside the line bending. He had felt a sting somewhere under his thumb with a spear in both hands and the wall giving way, and he had told himself two or three. He had told himself busy. All through the evening count he had not let himself ask which.

Three. Alive.

He sat there with the sun on the back of his neck and watched the beads fade from red to pink, and from pink to nothing. It took less time than he'd thought. It seemed a good deal longer.

He did not say a word, and nobody asked. When the last of the pink was gone he stood up, shouldered the spear, and went back to the wall.

* * *

That night Kandan found him in the pit they had cleared behind the shelf, a patch of packed scree and a post set in stone. Devan had been drilling alone, because there was nothing else to do with his hands.

"Warden." Kandan stood at the edge in the dark, with a practice staff on his shoulder and a gourd in his fist. "You'll teach me the guard you used at the corner? Left foot back, shaft low?"

Devan looked at him. Months ago Kandan had called Neela a witch at the barracks door.

"Yes."

They worked in the dark by the light of one shuttered lamp. The staff cracked on the staff. Kandan had a drover's patience and a sergeant's anger, and both made him good. After an hour he was nearly as fast as Devan on the low guard, and Devan said so. Kandan looked as if he'd been handed something heavy.

"Rest," Devan said. He walked to the post at the end of the pit.

He had no reason to. It was the end of the drill, the way he had ended every drill since the winter after the ford. Open hand, slow, from the hip. He took the form through the pivot and the turn and set the last stroke at the post, two fingers extended, aimed at a place on the wood that was not marked and did not need to be.

He stopped a hair short.

He held it there, the fingertips a thread off the grain, until his shoulder burned.

When he turned, Kandan was watching with the gourd halfway to his mouth.

"That's the one," Kandan said slowly. "The one from the ford. Muthu told us. Everyone knows."

"Yes."

"You used the spear at the corner. Three days, you've never used it. You had that in your hand every time, and a man came at you with a blade." He didn't sound accusing. He sounded like a man who has found a rock in his rice. "Why don't you use it?"

Devan lowered his hand. He looked at the two fingers, as though they might answer.

"Because it works," he said.

Kandan said nothing. The lamp ticked as the oil settled.

After a while he drank from the gourd and held it out. Devan took it. It was sour toddy, and it was the first time anyone in that fort had passed him a cup without being told to.

* * *

The days ran together after that.

The Thurai tried the ladders twice more and found the corner closed. They tried the back of the cut at night, with six men and a rope, and found Koman's old eyes waiting on the lip, and Thulasi's spear, and something that moved through the dark on four feet and was not looked at afterward. Devan kept to his orders. He did not watch the dusk on the shelf. He let the night belong to those who owned it, and in the grey he took the news from Vasu in a low voice with his back to the slope: three men taken, two ropes cut, a boat-sized timber dragged away.

Each noon he sat on the boulder. Three. Three. Three. On the fourth he found his hand shaking and put it under his arm until it stopped.

The burn on his forearm closed. The count of the lip went to seventy, then sixty-eight. A dead man's cloak became a bandage. Muthu's thirty settled into the line as if they had always stood there, and went home each night with wet faces to a fort that sent down hot rice in a cart. Once a goat was seen on the cart road, ahead of the rice, and Kandan swore it had been sent as an auditor.

On the fifth evening the Thurai herald came up the narrows.

He came alone, on foot, under a white rag on a pole, and he stopped a long bowshot below the shelf, on a flat of stone where the cart road bent. He was a small man with a big voice. He called up in a hill accent, so that no one could say they had not understood.

"Warden of Kettamala!"

Devan came to the lip. He stood in the red light, spear in his hand, and the whole wall behind him went still.

"The admiral sends this. He is a patient man. He has sat five days and lost his ladders, and he is tired of it." The herald paused for the echo to finish. "There are villages on this road. Seven are in his reach. At dawn he will burn the first. At the next dawn, the second. He will go on burning one a morning until the pass is open."

Nobody moved.

"The first is Kesu's. The one-eyed man's, at the foot of the goat path. The admiral hopes the warden remembers the way."

He lowered the rag. He turned and went down the narrows in no hurry.

Devan stood there while the last of the red went out of the stone. Kesu's was a half day below the shelf, behind the Thurai lines. There was a well in it, and a cattle shed, and an old man who had said *I'll hold the well* and meant it. It was thirteen hours to dawn.

Behind him, Kandan said very quietly, "Warden."

Devan did not turn round. He looked down at his left thumb, where nothing showed, and thought of the next noon, nineteen hours away.

"I know," he said.
