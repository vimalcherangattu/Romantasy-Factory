# Chapter 29: Plain Ink

Kandan came into the hall with a white rag in his fist and mud to the thighs.

"A herald," he said. "Alone, on a grey horse, with this wound round his spear." He held up the rag as if it might still be hot. "He stopped at the stakes and wouldn't come a step nearer. He says the admiral wants a truce. Three days."

Neela counted the hall without meaning to. Eight pillars, two doors, one window with the shutter back. Twelve people stood in it, and not one of them had slept. The queen sat in the warden's chair, because Devan had put her there, with Usha at her shoulder. Devan stood beside the table with his dead arm in its green sash. Captain Haran, grey-moustached and sour, leaned against a pillar. Muthu stood by the door.

"Three days for what?" said Devan.

"To bury their dead. To wait for the sea lords' word." Kandan's scarred brow pulled down. "I'll tell you what I saw, Warden. Down in the cut there are bronze caps with red crests among the dead. Officers. I counted forty crests before I stopped. When the host pulled back to the beach this morning, it moved under sergeants."

"Captains die first in a narrow place," Devan said slowly. "They go up the ladders to show the men how. He's lost his hands, and he never got the shelf."

"He's lost his nerve," Haran said.

"He's lost some of both. It isn't the same as losing the war." Devan looked at the queen. "I'd take the three days. And I'd double every picket in them."

"As would I." Nirmala folded her hands. "It is a gift I would be a fool to refuse, and a fool to trust. Send the herald back with a yes, Corporal. Tell him the stakes stay where they are."

Kandan went. The hall let out its breath, and Neela felt the stool under her again. She sat by the wall with Chathu's sheaf in its oilcloth across her knees. She had carried it so long that her arms had stopped noticing.

"Then let us use the three," said the queen.

She did not rise. She never needed to. The court of Kettamala had learned that about her in a single morning.

"The Company may exist," she said. "I say it here, before the pickets and the captains and the clerk with the lamp. A body of free odiyans, owning its name, taking contracts by its own vote. I will put my hand to that." A small pause, as clean as a cut. "On two conditions. The crown keeps the pages of the four guards who have not named. And the crown keeps Chathu's pages for the forty, all of them, named and unnamed. They were written at my table. They were written for me."

"They were written *on* us," said Pavi, from the doorway.

"They were written *by a witness*," the queen said, "who saw each of you turn. I do not say it to wound. I say it because it is true, and a page is a page. Whoever holds those leaves can read them at night. A charter that lets the Company hold them lets eleven people who have not chosen walk about with a leash on their necks and no one's hand to keep it. Better in mine than in nobody's."

Neela listened and found, to her annoyance, that part of it was an argument. A good one. Better a known hand than none. She had thought it herself, once, about Chathu.

She lifted the sheaf out of its oilcloth because her hands wanted something to do.

She laid it against her face.

She hadn't meant to. She was tired in the way that makes people press a cheek to cold stone. Forty stiff pale leaves, and she bent her head to them and breathed. Something in her went still.

Nothing.

No scorched tamarind. No pine sap. No bitter bite at the back of the throat, the smell that had followed her through every yard and shed of her childhood and that she could have found blind in a burning house. She had stood beside this sheaf at the well, and in the hall, and on the road from Kodiyur, and she had called its smell lamp-black and thought nothing. Lamp-black. The smell of a clerk's pot.

She put her thumb to the first leaf, to the *Madhavi* in Chathu's tidy hand, and pressed hard.

The ink was dry. It did not tack. It did not take her thumbprint, or cling, or pull. Resin-ink never dried. She had seen a page of her own, fresh as the day it was written, ten years after.

*Grey-brown fingertips.* She had seen them in the cell, and noted them, and let the queen's calm talk her out of them. *A steward's list with the names left blank, and no answer.* She had stood in his cell with the tin warm in her hand and asked herself whether it fitted. It hadn't, and she had put the question down.

"Your Majesty," she said. Her voice came out smaller than she meant. "Did you ever read one?"

"I beg your pardon?"

"A page. One of his forty. At night, aloud, to the person it names. Did you ever try it?"

Nirmala looked at her. "No. I watched him write them, four evenings running. I did not wish to test a leaf on a stranger." Her eyes went to the sheaf. "Why?"

"Because it's plain ink." Neela held up her thumb. It was clean. "It's a clerk's ink, the same as Thilak's pot at the well. He wrote forty pages in it and never once used resin. They've no more power than a laundry list. They never did."

Nobody spoke. Haran took his shoulder off the pillar.

And she saw the namings again, the scorch rising at each dusk. It had come off each member's own handed-back Resin House page, the true one, which she had held beside the sheaf on her knee. Chathu's leaves beside it had only curled in the heat, like anything near a flame. She had watched the wrong paper burn and believed it.

Neela heard the thing she was saying as she said it, and it took her a moment to understand what she felt. It was not triumph, or not only. A man who held every one of their cages, who had sold her at nineteen and bound her at twenty-seven and told her the ledger was mercy, had been handed forty fresh cages and a reed, and he had written forty empty rooms.

Behind her, Devan said very quietly, "Neela."

She did not answer. She sat there with the sheaf against her chest and her eyes burning, and the hall went on being a hall around her.

"We will break for the hour," said the queen, and her voice had gone strangely careful. "I should like to look at those."

* * *

The tin came at the end of the hour.

Neela had gone out to the well because the hall had too many walls. Devan found her on the curb. He sat down without asking, a little to one side, so she had the whole of the curb to her left. He said nothing. That was what he did.

A steward in the queen's grey-green came across the court. He carried a small flat tin in both hands, wrapped in a cloth, the way you carry a thing that might spill. He bowed to her and not to Devan.

"From the palace post, mistress. Brought up with the column's last rider. It's for you by name. The rider says it was brought to the gate warm and he kept it warm as ordered."

She took it.

It was warm. She didn't need to open it. She knew its heft, and the faint oily smell through the cloth, jaggery and sesame. Eleven times it had come to her before a killing, once in the kennels, once on a gatepost, and once she had carried it out of his cell. A folded scrap was tucked under the lid. The steward had done well not to read it, and had bowed himself away before she looked.

She read it.

*Written off. Paid in full. C.*

Common ink, grey-brown, in the neat hand she'd learned her letters from. She read it three times. Then she laughed, once, and it came out wrong.

"He knew," she said. "He sat in that cell and the queen put a reed in his hand and said *write them fresh.* And he wrote them in the wrong ink and said nothing and let her have forty empty pages." She held the scrap out flat in the sun. "He cheated a queen. For us. Devan, he cheated the *queen*."

"Yes," said Devan.

"It's the only thing he ever did that wasn't for himself. The only one." Her hands were doing something to the tin, turning it. "And he did it with a pot of ink and a straight face. He'd have loved that. He'd have liked to see her face."

Devan was silent.

"He taught me my letters," she said. "Did I tell you? Sitting at that long table with my feet not touching the floor. Pachan gave me his sweet and Chathu said, *Clever girl, you'll go far.* I was ten. Nobody had ever said I was clever. I thought it was the best day of my life." Her voice caught on something and she stood on it until it broke. "He held my wrist at the first resin and I screamed and he said *good girl, it's over.* He wept when Ammu died. I believe he did. And then he took the crown's money for me, and he put me in a cage, and he sent me a *tin.* Tin after tin. How can I—"

"You don't have to make it add up."

"I want it to add up."

"It doesn't." He said it gently, with the plain flatness she had learned to trust. "I won't tell you he was kind. He sold Asha. He sold you. He'd have sold me to the same bidder and sent a tin to my funeral. I won't say a word for him." He turned his good hand palm up on his knee, not reaching for her, only leaving it there. "And I won't tell you not to grieve him. I haven't the right, and I haven't the grief. Say it. I'll sit here."

She stared at him.

She had expected an argument. Not a hard one. Devan would never have been hard. But she had braced herself for some careful sentence about what the man had done, and how little right she had to mourn him. She'd have deserved it. He didn't say it. He sat in the sun with a dead arm and a face grey from no sleep, beside the well where Ammu's blue cloth should have been, and let her miss the man who fed her and sold her. He did not mistake it for love, or call it weakness. He left it a space the size of a body.

"I'm not sorry he's in a cell," she said, with her eyes shut.

"No."

"I'm sorry he's not the man I thought he was when I was ten."

"Yes."

"He was never that man."

"No," said Devan. "But you were ten. You were right to want one."

She put the heel of her hand to her mouth and wept for a while, quietly, with the tin warm in her lap. He sat beside her and did not touch her until she turned her head. Then his left hand came up and settled on the back of her neck, steady and dry, and stayed.

When it was over she wiped her face on Muthu's coat and set the tin on the well curb, shut.

"I'm not going to eat them," she said.

"I didn't think you would."

"I might give them to Kannan's goat."

"Pepper would bite him for it," said Devan, and she laughed, a real one, and the grey ghost of a smile moved over his face and went.

She stood. Her legs held. She picked up the sheaf, and the scrap of paper, and Ittan's oilcloth tally book from where it rode against her ribs.

"Come on," she said. "I know what I'm going to say."

* * *

The queen had not moved from the warden's chair. Before her on the table lay three of Chathu's leaves, and she had her good hand flat on them as if to keep them from lifting. Usha stood very straight at her shoulder. Her face said she had already heard.

Neela put the scrap of paper on the table. Nirmala read it. A line cut deep between her brows and was gone.

"He wrote you forty cages in plain ink," Neela said, aware of the hall filling at her back. "I'm not saying it to shame you. You'd have been right to want something to hold, with thirty thousand Thurai on the beach."

"Fourteen hundred," said Haran.

"Fourteen hundred. It felt like more." She let it stand. "So the forty are free. They always were. The pages are ash with a name on, and the only living witness to those forty turnings is a man in your cell with ink on his fingers. You hold four real leaves, Majesty, and I'll come to them. But first I want to read you something."

"I am listening."

"I want everyone to listen. I want it said where the hall can hear." She opened the oilcloth tally book to the leaf with a bit of thread marking it. "This is Councillor Ittan's. Every extra pass he sold has a hook beside it. Down the other side there's another column, in the same hand, with no hook. A little bird, folded wings. You know what it means. The ledger key says it means *by leave.*"

The queen said nothing.

"Twenty-one years back, in brown ink copied from an older book." Neela read. "*Nellimala. Second harvest. Two boats of Thurai men, the coast road to the foot of the pass. By leave.*" She looked up. "That is my village. I was six."

"Neela," said Devan, behind her. Not to stop her. Only so that she'd know where he stood.

"Ponni is an old fishwife sleeping in Kesu's shed, and she was there. She says two boats crossed the bar at slack water with no pilot, as if expected. Men in grey waited at the top of the goat path with a flat cart. There was a small new yellow bird on the tailboard. One of them counted the youngest children as they were lifted on." Her voice did not shake. She had said it to herself on the road so often it had worn smooth. "I was in that count."

A low sound went through the hall. Kuttu, near the door, had put his hand over his mouth.

"Then the crown leaf." She laid it beside the tally book, the green-threaded intake rolls, the thin brown birds in every margin. "Thirty rolls. I've read them all. Usha, a potter's daughter from a hill village. Sanu, tailor's son, of Pallikara, which burned this season, and its name stands in the ledger as an intake village the year he was taken. Every village on these rolls was raided in the year its child was taken. Every one. I checked them against the raids Devan has on his border roll." She breathed. "And a Thurai scout died at the shelf with eleven new Varanad silver coins in his purse, with the lowest pepper leaf doubled on the die. That's the same die as the widow's coin you paid out of your own treasury. The same weight, to a grain."

She stopped. She had not known how loud a hall could be with nobody saying anything.

"The raids made your knives," Neela said. "That is all I have to say. I don't say it to bargain. I'm not holding it over you. I just don't think we can write a charter on top of it as if it weren't there."

Nirmala did not look away. She did not glance at Usha, or at Haran, or at the door. For a long moment she studied Neela's face, and the warm, unhurried expression she had worn through every hour since the Salt Gate stayed where it was and meant something different.

"Yes," she said.

It was not loud. It dropped into the hall like a stone into a well, and Neela, who had braced for a denial, a lawyerly sidestep or a gentle *you mistake me,* found she did not know what to do with her hands.

"Yes," the queen said again. "To all of it. I will not make you prove the rest." She drew her good hand off Chathu's leaves. Her bandaged arm lay in her lap, and Neela saw Usha's shoulder move as though the welt had stung them both. "I think I am being asked, today, to put down several things I have carried a long time. I should like to say what I will sign. I would like the hall to hear it, and the Thurai to hear it, if there is a wind. I will sign a charter that—"

A horn sounded from the padippura.

One long note, then two short. Bhaskaran's code. Unarmed strangers at the gate, and not many.

Everyone turned. Behind her, Devan drew breath.

The note sounded again, and a third time, with something added at the end that she had never heard. Bhaskaran, who had a wife and four daughters and a soldier's discipline about horns, had let it run long.

Muthu was already at the door. He looked down the stair and back. His face was a man's who has read something in a clerk's round hand and hopes he's mistaken.

"Warden," he said. "It's Justice Rema. On your letter." A pause. "She's brought the high court's roll."
