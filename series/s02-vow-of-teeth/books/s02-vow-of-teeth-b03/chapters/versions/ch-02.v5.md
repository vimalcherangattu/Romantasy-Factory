# Chapter 2: It Passes

Devan stepped between the woman and the door before Neela could, and he did not apologise for it.

The sun had already gone behind the western ridge. What was left lay on the scree in a thin copper wash that would last a quarter hour. When it went, the forty would want the dark, the cup and the low room to themselves. He had said three words to the room the moment he saw the green sash: *Nobody smears tonight.* Kuttu had groaned. Nobody had asked why. They were learning what he was afraid of.

The woman stopped on the flat slab, six paces off. Vellan stood a pace behind Devan's right shoulder and said nothing, which for Vellan was loud. Neela stood at his left. He had not asked for that either, and he did not move away from it.

"I am alone," the woman said. "I carry no resin and no flask. You may look."

"I will."

She pushed her sleeves back, left and then right, and held out her wrists in the failing light. The left bore the single flat seam he remembered from the passage outside Ittan's guardroom, where a cuff had slipped for the length of a breath. The right bore its twin. Both had been pared, and neatly. Somebody had cut away a girl's worth of layered ridges and left a line a finger long.

Vellan breathed in through his nose, slow, twice. Then he gave one small shake of his big head. No tamarind. No scorched pine. She was clean.

"Your name," Devan said.

"Usha."

He waited for the rest of it. There was no rest. She had given it the way a person sets down a knife, handle first.

For three days her escort had slept in his south range and eaten Janu's rice. They had answered to First, Second, Third and Fourth. Janu had tried each of them with a plate and a scolding and got nothing back. Not one had offered a name to anyone at Kettamala. This one had walked up a mountain to hand hers over, and he could not guess what it was meant to buy.

"You came to give us a name," he said.

"I came to give you this." She took a letter from inside her sash. It was folded square and tied with green thread, and the thread was sealed under a thumb of green wax. "I was told whoever holds the hill would take it. The Company, if there is one yet."

*Company.* So the word had climbed down the hill ahead of her, or she had guessed it. He filed that, and took the letter.

Even in poor light the wax was clear. The sunbird was pressed deep, wings spread wide, and at the tip of the left wing sat the little chip he knew as he knew his own scars. The same chip marked the payment writ in the oilcloth at the bottom of his pack, which he had carried up the hill rather than leave it in the hollow behind the third slat at Kettamala. That seal had bought his death. This one was asking for something.

He turned the letter to what was left of the copper. Where the thread crossed the wax, a second bird had been pressed so lightly that he nearly missed it. It was smaller and faint, wings folded close to its body. He had seen that bird in the margins of Ittan's tally book, and on the stubs of thirty cut leaves. He kept his face still.

"Read it aloud," Neela said quietly. "They should hear it."

The doorway behind them had filled. He could feel the forty stacked in it, breath held. He broke the wax. It gave with the soft crack of a leaf.

The hand was a clerk's, round and even. The words were not a clerk's.

*To those who came down from the Resin House and have no master: I, Nirmala, Queen of Varanad, greet you. I do not call you rogues. I offer a pardon under my seal for every name on your plank, and the crown's vouching in place of a master's, so that no one need hunt you. Come to Kodiyur as my night-guard, and be fed, and be sworn, and be safe. The war will end. When it ends, those without a vouching will be hunted, and I will not be able to prevent it. I would rather you were mine than anyone's prey.*

*The crown will hold Kodiyur and the forts. The hill villages must bear the raiders as they always have.*

*I wait on your answer.*

Nobody spoke. Devan read the last lines again in his head, and then once more, slower, and the cold started in his hands.

*As they always have.*

Six boats. Twelve in a good season. A village burned at a time and a purse of silver coin in the right hand, and the queen telling herself it was bandits. *As they always have* was the treaty written in her own words, a line she must have thought no one could read. She wanted forty knives behind her walls. She had sixty ships on the beach, and she had written that the hill would bear it. Nobody in that sentence had been counted but him.

His goal, which had been a vote at a fire an hour ago, went hard as set mortar. He held the Monkey Pass, with no office and no seal and no leave from the woman whose chipped sunbird lay in his hand. Nothing in that letter would change it.

But the forty had just heard a pardon with their names in it.

"And our names?" Kuttu said from the doorway, hoarse. "It says every name on the plank."

Devan turned. Faces, stacked and pale. Thilak had his hooked knife in his fist and did not seem to know it.

"The pardon is yours," he said. "You'll vote it at dawn, hand by hand, and I'll abide what you choose. The pass is already carried. That one is mine."

Nobody answered. Somewhere in the dark of the room someone let out a breath that was half a curse. It was the sound of a Company finding the first crack in itself.

"The pardon is real," Usha said. "I would not say it if it weren't."

"I believe you believe it."

Her mouth moved, not a smile. "That is a careful way to say it, warden."

"I've no office. Devan will do."

He folded the letter along its creases. "How did you find the shelter?"

"The pass-village watcher walks the hill edge at dawn and reads the scent-posts." She said it like a woman reciting a route. "His log goes to a cell at Kettamala, and from there to whoever opens the prisoner's post. That is the queen's household. Every Resin House post went cold in a single night. Forty horses left dung on the goat path." She glanced at Vellan. "A child could have followed it. So I did."

"And Rema's escort left by that path."

"We did."

He waited again. She gave him nothing more, and he could not say whether that was discipline or a wall.

Behind him a boot scraped grit. Neela had moved, no more than a step, and now she stood level with Usha, close, her hand held loose at her side. Her voice came pitched low, but the evening was still and the slab was small.

"It passes," Usha said to her.

The same two words she had spoken in the padippura at dusk, about a girl's split nails. Devan had not been meant to hear them then, and he heard them now. Neela's head tilted. Her face went careful.

"Does it," she said.

"Not the ache. The rest." Usha's thumbs had found her cuffs again. "I heard a page burned of itself, at the Resin House, two nights past the new moon. In the household that is not a thing one hears twice. What did the naming cost?"

Devan did not move. He had never told Neela he would not speak of it. He had simply not known anyone would ask her aloud.

Neela did not look at him. She could have said *that is mine to keep*. She could have lied, as she had lied for twenty-one years to anyone with a face and a question. She answered in her dry flat voice:

"He cuts himself, I get the welt. I cut myself, he gets it. Nicked his chin shaving three days back and I found out over my rice." A short breath. "A deep wound goes both ways. A mortal one drags the other down with it. For life, both of us."

"All of it?"

"That's all of it. It's also everything."

She said it to a stranger in the open, on a rock, in front of forty people and a bear. She said it the way one reports the weather in one's own body. *My hand. His hand. The same hand.* Usha's face did a very small thing at the eyes and then stilled. She turned half away to look down the dark path, and Devan stood holding the folded letter and looked at her back, the green sash knotted at the hip.

He had known about the welts since the Resin House wall. He had felt a hook clip Neela's ribs and said *then it's mine*. That was not new. What was new was that the queen's own agent now held it, in Neela's voice, and would carry it back down to a woman who wrote *prey* in a kind hand. His body was a known lever on hers. A spear that went through him in the gorge was a spear aimed at Neela, and the queen's household could now count on that as surely as he could.

Usha turned back. "Understood," she said.

"I doubt it," said Neela. Her tone was not unkind.

The copper had left the scree. The first stars came out thin and grudging over the sea. Devan saw Usha look once at the doorway, at the faces there, and he stepped half a pace to the left so she saw him instead.

"You'll sleep under the lee wall," he said. "Thilak will bring you food. Nobody crosses the slab after dark. It's not a threat. It's a rule of the house."

"I know the rule," she said. "Better than you."

"Then you'll keep it better than we do."

She almost smiled again.

* * *

The fire burned low and the shelter slept in lumps. Thilak had given Usha rice and a blanket and watched her eat it. Vellan had gone to sit across the slab from her, his back against the stones, the pale wooden bird turning in his fingers. Neela lay on her side at the far end of the hearth with her hand under her cheek. Her breath hitched on the ninth count. He had learned it by heart.

Devan went out behind the shelter.

A roof post stood at the back, where the lean-to for the packhorse met the wall. It was a squared length of old chestnut, silvered and cracked, set deep in the turf, and it reached about a man's throat. In the dark he could not see the grain. He had no need to.

He stood square. He took a breath through the nose and let it go. Then he raised his right hand, flat, the first two fingers joined and the others folded under, and began the slow drill.

He had made it up the winter after the ford, when he had stopped hitting living things and could not stop hitting wood. It was a stripped form: one strike, a stride in, a turn of the wrist. Each step took a count of ten. At the end the two fingers stopped a hair short of the post, at the place where on a man the notch below the ear would be. A hair. He knew exactly how wide it was, because he had spent seven years measuring it.

He did it again. And again. He was not thinking of the queen, or of sixty ships. He was thinking about the width of a hair and how it differed from nothing, and how his hand, tonight, wanted to close the gap.

*Not yet,* he told it. *Not on a post.*

He finished the tenth strike and lowered the hand. His breath was even. The chestnut had not been touched.

The pack lay under the lean-to eave. He knelt beside it, undid the flap and found the oilcloth by feel. It was the size of two palms and tied with red thread. He did not unwrap it. He knew what lay inside without a lamp. A strip of hide, a chit split in two, and the queen's payment writ with its green wax and its chip at the left wing tip.

*Death by beast, in the warden's chamber, before the pass closes.*

He pressed his thumb on the knot of red thread, the way a man tests a scar to see whether it still hurts.

It did. The same woman had written *I would rather you were mine than anyone's prey*, and had meant it, probably. She answered every widow's petition with her own hand. Rema had said so. She signed death writs for loyal servants who got in the way. Both were true, and he had spent a night with his head full of the second, and tonight he knew the first as well.

He tied the flap and sat back on his heels. The hill was quiet. Somewhere far down, past the dark of the scree, a red smudge lay on the edge of the world, low and steady. Pazhayangadi, or what was left of it.

He went in and lay down at Neela's back and counted her breaths until his own fell into step with them. He did not sleep for long.

* * *

He woke before the light and did not move.

Through the smoke hole the sky had gone from black to the flat grey that came before colour. The shelter was a heap of blankets. Through the gap of the door he saw a figure already up and quiet at the lee wall, boots in her hand. Usha put them on without a sound and straightened her sash at the left hip.

Devan eased himself clear of Neela's sleeping weight. She was awake. He knew it by the way her breath did not hitch, deliberate and even. Her eyes were shut and she was listening, as he was, with the whole of her skin.

Outside, the cold had a bite. The turf was soaked grey and the sea lay under a mist, and the green sash was already at the lip of the slab. Usha did not turn to look at the door. She walked three steps and stopped, and Neela came past Devan's shoulder in her wrap with her bare feet on the wet rock. She had not asked leave to go first and he had not offered it.

They stood a pace apart. Usha said something. It took no more than a breath or two, and her lips moved too little for him to read from the doorway. Neela did not answer. Usha dipped her head, once, and then she was gone down the thorn path as lightly as she had come up it, a pin of green going to grey.

Neela turned and walked back to him. Her face had nothing in it at all.

"Tell me," he said.

"She said, *The leaf sleeps in the queen's room.*" Neela's voice was low and even, the way she spoke when she was counting something. "And then, *If you come, come by the sea stair at the noon gun.*"

"The leaf."

"A leaf of pages. One that could be read." She wet her lips. "The crown leaf. She means the thirty."

The wind lifted the corner of the letter in his pocket. He had not known he was holding it.

She had come alone, with open hands, and left a door.

He looked at the path where the green had gone. The mist was thickening into the gorge, and there was no knowing what waited at the bottom of it.

"Door or snare," Neela said. Then, flat, counting: "Kodiyur is four days at a hard ride. The raiders are in the gorge in three."

The same hinges served both. He had been in the border service long enough to know that, and married long enough to know that she knew it too.

"So the door costs the pass I swore to the fire I'd hold," said Devan, "or it costs you going alone. And if you go, a spear in me at the gorge is a spear in you, wherever you stand. I'd not have the road decide that for you."

"Yes," she said.

He looked at her bare feet on the wet rock, then at the path.

"I can't tell," he said.

It was the truth. It was the only kind of answer he had ever given her, and she took it, as she always did, and put her cold hand into his.
