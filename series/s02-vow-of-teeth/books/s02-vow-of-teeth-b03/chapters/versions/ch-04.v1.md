# Chapter 4: Walls Only

The bay went over the lip with his forelegs folded, and his scream came back off the rock a breath after.

Neela had the rope. Everyone near the first bad place had the rope. It ran from a thorn stump through Thilak's fists and round Devan's back, and it burned through Neela's palm before she knew she had closed it. Down on the scree the horse found his feet. Kuttu laughed once, high and cracked, and then there was only the sound of forty-two people breathing hard.

"Next," Devan said.

His voice was level. A red line was rising across the palm of his right hand. She felt the matching sting in her own, and neither of them mentioned it.

Rema's escort had strung three ropes on the goat path, in the dark, on the night they climbed to Kettamala. Neela had hated them for it. Now she could have kissed each knot. The first place was the lip. The second was a chimney of loose laterite where the horses went down on their haunches and the people went down on their backs. The third was the thorn shoulder above the old well, where the path narrowed to a foot's width with air on the left.

She counted all of it. Forty-one steps to the first rope. Nineteen horses, borrowed from the pass villages with a promise written on a tally stick. Two hundred and six breaths on the chimney. At the third place Madhavi stood with her back to the drop, talking to a grey mare in a low steady stream. It was a made-up song about turnips. The mare walked down it.

Vellan went last of the people. He took the third rope one-handed, his bad leg dragging, and said nothing the whole way down. Thilak waited at the bottom with a hand out. Vellan looked at it for a moment. Then he took it.

They slept one night under the thorn, with no resin and no fire. Devan walked the line twice in the dark. The second time he stopped by Neela's blanket, said nothing, and put his hand flat on the stone beside her head. She put hers over it. His palm was hot and raw. So was hers.

In the morning the langurs were gone quiet.

She noticed it on the long last slope, with the roofs of the lower village a smudge below. Every day for months the troops had barked their warnings off the gorge wall, a racket she'd learned to sleep through. Now the cliff gave back only the wind. Devan had stopped before she had. He stood with his head turned toward the cart road, which lay out of sight past the shoulder.

"Strangers," she said.

"On the road." He started walking again. "Walk faster."

* * *

Kettamala came up out of the red hill at dusk, the laterite walls the colour of old blood. Neela felt her shoulders loosen before she could stop them. She had never wanted to come back to this place. She had married into it with a knife sewn in her waistband.

The padippura gate stood shut. A horn sounded from the wall: one long note and two short, the code for unarmed strangers, not many. It was Bhaskaran's horn, and Bhaskaran's lungs, and it was the least welcoming music she had ever loved.

The wicket opened. Muthu came out alone.

He was the same big, broad man, with a grin already on his face and his arms already wide. "Fort's open! Come and be fed, you thieves, you walking disasters, you—" His eye went down the line of forty-two strangers, the lamp-black plank on its packhorse, Vellan at the back with his hood up. The grin held for a count of three and then it set, like wax.

The iron seal hung on a cord at his chest, a disc cut with a hill and a gate. Eleven wardens had worn it. It looked wrong on him, as a borrowed coat does.

"Devan," he said.

"Muthu."

"You look terrible."

"I've been told."

Muthu laughed, short, and the laugh did not last. He took a folded paper from his sleeve instead. Green wax, with a sunbird pressed deep. "I'd rather you heard it from me than from the wall. You'll have heard it already, I suppose, from the hill."

"Read it."

"You can read, I think."

"Read it to them, Muthu. They should hear it too."

Muthu held the paper at arm's length, like a man holding a snake by the neck.

"*Kettamala shall hold its walls only. The garrison shall make no sally beyond the padippura. The garrison shall harbour no rogue, unvouched, or odiyan under pain of the crown's displeasure.*" He lowered it. "It's signed by the queen's household, not by her. I'd like to tell you that makes a difference."

"Does it?"

"It makes me feel better."

Behind Neela somebody drew a short sharp breath. She did not turn. She was the rogue. They were all the rogue, forty-two of them, standing on the road with their own free names on a plank. Muthu knew it. His eyes passed over her face without catching, the way a man's do when he has decided not to look at a thing.

His mouth had gone flat. She watched it. This was not the stillness of his lying. She'd learned that tell from Devan, and she knew it. This was the other quiet, the one in which a man stands between two doors and holds his breath. It was a hard quiet to see in a man who had always filled a room.

"The lower court is the garrison's yard," Muthu said at last, to the air above their heads. "Drovers pass through it on their way up to the pass in a season like this. Drovers with horses, who want feeding. Nobody's put a drover on the rolls in my time. I'll not start tonight."

It was a hole in a wall, and a narrow one. Devan inclined his head.

"Thank you."

"Don't." Muthu turned, and the gate swung wide. "I'm not doing it for you."

* * *

Janu fed them as if she were feeding a wedding.

Neela had not known there could be so much rice in one place. It came out of the kitchen in cauldrons carried by a line of girls and Chinna with her bad hip, and it spread across the lower court under the wall in the last of the light. There was curd. There was a fish stew that smelled of tamarind and made Meenu cry before she had tasted it. Janu stood in the middle of it all in a stained white mundu, scolding everyone she could reach.

"Sit. You, sit. Not there, there's a drain. You'll catch a chill and then who'll hear the end of it? Not you. Eat."

Neela held out a hand for a bowl.

Janu put it down on the stone beside her.

She did it quickly, and kindly, and she did not look away. It was only that her fingers had stayed on the rim, and the rim stayed between them. When Neela reached again for the ladle, Janu had already passed it to Pavi. Her right hand was wrapped in a cloth, and she said that was a burn from the pot. The bandage was clean, and the burn was not fresh. Neela noted that, and the way Janu's eyes dropped, once, to the plain gold bangle at her wrist, and came up again.

*She kissed me on the forehead before we left.* Neela counted it. One kiss, then, and not a hand. She did not know what that meant, and she was too hungry to find out.

"Eat," Janu said, and went away to scold someone else.

The well stood at the north end of the court, with its low coping and a ring of wet stones. Neela had walked past it sixty times as a bride. She saw now that it was ringed with people.

Not garrison. Women, mostly, with a cloth spread under each cluster and a child or two asleep on bundles. Their heads were wrapped against the damp. There were about thirty of them, and they sat in the exact posture of people who had been told to wait and had not yet been told for what.

She took her bowl across to them. Devan watched her go, and did not follow.

"Sit," said a stout woman with a baby, and moved a bundle for her. "You're the warden's wife."

"Neela."

"Chellam. My man tapped toddy at Kalladi till the raiders came up the road." The baby spat. "We got out. That one nearly didn't."

Neela asked who else, and was told, one by one: Gouri, whose mother was somewhere on the road still; a carter's widow who gave no name but gave her a rice cake; an old man called Saroja's father, who wasn't Saroja's father at all but liked to say so, because Saroja sang at the pass-village well and he was proud of it. Neela said each name once under her breath. They went into her like pegs into a board.

She had reached the far side of the well when she saw the small face.

Minnu, six years old, with a rag doll under her chin and a smear of curd on her lip. Beside her sat a thin woman with a cough, holding a nine-year-old boy's hand in both her own.

Parukutty looked up and knew her at once.

"My lady," she said, and began to stand.

"Don't." Neela put out a hand, palm down, as if to a goat. "Please. I'm only eating."

Parukutty sat. She said, "The pass-village men told us the Thurai were two days off. We left our house last night. We don't know about the salt factor."

"Forget the salt factor," said Neela, and meant it, and did not know what else to say.

Chandu stared at her bandaged left hand. "Is it true my father's spot is in the gorge?"

"Yes," she said. She had never lied to a child, and she wasn't going to begin now. "The warden's going to stand there."

Chandu thought about this. "All by himself?"

She looked across the court. Devan stood at the foot of the wall with a bowl he wasn't eating, talking to Thilak, the grey in his hair gone silver under the torches. He had no seal, no office, no men beyond the ones the road had given him.

"No," Neela said. "Not by himself."

* * *

Muthu came for them after the bowls were stacked.

He led them up the east stair, not the west. Neela noticed, and said nothing. He took them through the long dark of the gate chamber and shut the door behind them, and it was only then that he dropped onto the bench under the arrow slit like a man whose strings had been cut.

"I can't do it," he said.

"I know," said Devan.

"Don't *know*. Argue." Muthu rubbed his face with both hands. "Sixty men on the rolls. Sixty. I can order them to sit on a wall, and I have. I can't order them out of it. The order's plain, and half of them are Bhaskaran's lads with mothers in the lower village, and none of them has seen an army." He looked up. "I keep my oath to the border. I swore it, on this iron, with you standing there. But I can't hand the garrison to a man who has no office, not even if it's you. They'd follow me, and they'd be hanged for it, and the pass would still fall."

"I haven't asked you to."

"No. You haven't." Muthu's mouth twisted. "That's what makes it hard."

Neela stood against the door and counted its hinges. Three. Devan did not argue. He did not do anything for a while. Then he laid his palm flat on the table, with the rope-burn red across it, and said:

"Then I won't ask tonight."

Muthu's eyes closed. "Ask me again," he said, "when you have a reason I can write down. Something with a seal. A writ, a count of ships. A thing the high court would read and not laugh at. I'll hold the pass for you, Devan. I'll hold it with a pen if I can't hold it with a spear."

"I'll bring you one."

"Don't bring me a speech."

"I never have."

Muthu laughed, finally, a rough bark. Then he stood and went to the door. He stopped with his hand on it, not looking at Neela.

"Janu's put the cloth on the south bed," he said. "I wasn't told. I didn't hear. If anyone asks I've given the warden's guests a roof for one night as a courtesy to the border."

"Guests," said Devan.

"For one night. It's all the word will stretch to."

The door shut. The seal on its cord had made a small dull sound against the frame, an iron note under the voice.

* * *

The guest room off the south range was the one Rema's escort had used. Someone had swept it and put fresh straw in the corners, and left a lamp. There was a bed, one, with a clean cloth on it.

Neela checked the door. One door, and the window on the north side, at a drop she liked. She sat on the end of the bed and unwound her wrap, and the wool smelled of thorn and horse and Devan.

He did not come in at once. He stood at the doorway with his shoulder against the frame and looked across the court.

The warden's chamber was at the end of the north range, up its own stair. Even at this distance she could see the teak shutters, shut, with one thin slit of lamplight under the sill. Muthu would be sleeping behind them, or not sleeping. Devan did not look at it. He looked at the well, at the sleeping women, at the dark where the horses were tethered. He looked everywhere except at the high window, and he did it like a man walking round a hole in a floor.

"You haven't looked at your door," Neela said. "Not once. Why?"

"I won't answer that."

He said it flat and quick, and she let it lie. They'd made the rule. One pass each, once in a life, and she'd spent hers on the worst question she could think of. He had a right to his. She turned and started on the knot of her sash.

Behind her, the doorframe creaked as he shifted.

"I miss the creak," he said.

She stopped.

"The third slat. Of the shutter." He was not looking at her. He had his hand on the wood of the frame, and his voice sounded as if it had been handed to him in the dark and he was reading it by feel. "For eight years I slept with my back to the wall and listened for something at that window. Then you came, and it was you. I heard it every night. The slat, and then the sill, and then nothing." His thumb moved. "And at dawn it creaked again, and I knew you were home."

"I pressed it on purpose," Neela said. "The first night. To see if you'd wake."

"I know."

"You didn't."

"I was awake."

"I *know*, Devan." It came out less dry than she meant. She got up, crossed the straw and stood in front of him. He still hadn't turned. "I've been a thief in your house for forty days, and I rang a bell on your window every night."

"It was the best sound in the fort."

She took his rope-burned hand. She did not hold it carefully, and a small twist of heat went up her own arm. He let out a breath that was almost a laugh.

"You told me you wouldn't answer."

"I know what I said." He looked down at her then. "I thought you ought to have it anyway. That's all I've got that's not a plan."

She did not say anything clever. She stood with his hand against her stomach, where the welt would rise on her if he cut it, and she put her forehead against his collar and breathed him in. Hill smoke, rope, salt sweat. It was a poor sort of home. It was the only one she'd chosen.

* * *

She woke to red.

It lay across the ceiling in a long shaking bar. She was on her feet before she had finished the thought, with her knife in her hand. It was a borrowed knife; she'd given hers away in a different life.

The beacon on the shoulder had gone up.

Bhaskaran's lads laid it each dry season, a stack of resin pine on the hill edge below the lightning boulder, to be lit if anything came up the gorge. A pass-village boy sat watch beside it. She had never seen it burn. Now it blazed, orange and wide, in the black square of the north window. Beyond it, at the foot of the gorge, a second smaller flame winked and went out, and winked again.

A horn started on the wall. Bhaskaran's. It began a long note.

Devan was already at the door.

He'd taken a spear from the rack by the lintel, one of Bhaskaran's, left as a courtesy and not a gift. His boots were on. He moved with his shoulder turned from her, fast and quiet, the way he did everything that mattered. He didn't mean to go without telling her. He meant to be in the court before the horn finished, and tell her from there. She had watched him decide things that way for forty days.

She crossed the room in three steps and caught his wrist.

He stopped, half through the door.

"Tell me before you go," Neela said.

The horn ended. In the quiet, she could hear the cistern chain clank once on the wheel, and the long dull roar of the fire on the hill.

Devan turned in the doorway with the spear in his left hand. His wrist was hot in her fingers. Behind him the lower court was filling with faces.

"The advance guard," he said, quick and low. "They're in the gorge already. Three days was wrong. I'm going to the boulder. I've no leave, and I've no seal, and I've nine men I can name and a Company I can ask." His throat moved. "I'm asking you first."

She felt it in her chest like a dropped stone. Not the order. The asking.

Neela let go of his wrist, and took up her knife.

"Then I'm coming," she said.
