# Chapter 3: Two Votes

They laid the plank across two hearthstones and sat round it like a table, forty-three people and a bowl of cold ash.

Devan drew in the ash with a stick. A long crooked line, the gorge, and across it a short bar. "The shelf," he said. "Forty paces wide at the mouth. Narrower under the shelf, where Raman died. A hundred men in single file can be held there by a few who know the ground, and not by many who don't. Behind that line are Kesu at his well, the pass villages, and Parukutty's children. Minnu is six. Chandu is nine." He set the stick down. "I hold the narrows. I ask the Company to hold it with me."

He said it plainly. He had bound himself to their hands, and everyone in the room knew what it cost him to ask.

Neela waited until the ash stopped moving. Then she took the stick.

"I want to go south," she said.

She made a second mark, well away from the first, at the bottom of the hearth. Not a line. A cliff. "The queen sent a letter and a woman with open hands, and she sent a door. *The leaf sleeps in the queen's room.* Thirty pages of the night-guard, in one place, while every eye in Kodiyur is on the sea. She will never watch her own room less than she does this week." She tapped the cliff. "Once the war ends, she can send those thirty anywhere. They'd be hers, or they'd be the raiders' bait. I want a small party, by the sea stair, at the noon gun."

"Four days there," said Thilak. He was a broad man who spoke slowly and meant every word. "Four back, if all goes well. The raiders are three days off. Neela, I'm a tapper. I tapped nightbark beside the sons of half the pass villages. If the narrows fall while we're counting stairs in a palace, I don't know how we look them in the face."

"They'll look at our backs," said Pavi, from the wall. "While we hold the gorge with forty and thirty sit in cages made of paper. Thilak, you were twenty-two years a member. You know what it is to have your name in someone's drawer."

"I know it better than you."

"Then you know it won't wait for spring."

It went round the plank. Kuttu said the villages were people, and the thirty were people, and he'd hold a gorge or climb a stair but he would not be asked to weigh them. Meenu, who rarely spoke, said she had been in a cage the width of a hunter's arm, and would give a season of nights to see the door of another one open. Somebody answered that doors did not matter to a village that had burned. Somebody answered that doors were all that mattered to those behind them.

Madhavi leaned forward. She had once closed her teeth on Devan's thigh, and he had not held it against her.

"Say it's a snare," she said. "The queen wants forty-one knives behind her walls. She sends a clean-wristed woman with a pardon, and a hint of a way in. Why does she send the hint? Because she wants us in the palace, where she can use the word." She looked at Neela. "She has seen every one of those thirty turn. She doesn't need a page to cage them. She doesn't need a page to cage *us*, if we walk in."

"Or," said Pavi, "Usha is one of the thirty. Her wrists were cut. Her cuffs came down over them like a girl hiding a bruise. A woman who is a prisoner sent that message and it is the one hinge she could reach."

"Or she's both," said Kuttu. "Snare and door. It happens."

"Or the queen means the door and means the snare," said Thilak. "She can want both. She has wanted both for twenty years."

"Then answer me one thing," Madhavi said, still looking at Neela. "Who knows the stair?"

The ash had gone quiet. Neela made herself answer without turning it.

"Usha's sentence."

"Which is?"

"*The sea stair at the noon gun.*"

"That's all of it?"

"That's all of it. It's thin." She did not look away. "I'd want better before I put a foot on it. But the vote is whether we go at all."

Madhavi sat back. "That's honest," she said. "I don't like it any better."

Neela let them argue on. Every guess was a good one. None of them had a way to be tested before the noon gun. She looked at Devan across the ash and found he was watching her, not the room, with the face he wore when he was seeing something exactly.

"Neela."

It was Kamalakshi. She had not spoken since the argument started. She sat with her knees drawn up and the carved wooden bird turning in her fingers, and her voice came quiet.

"If I hold my own page," she said, "can anyone else ever hold me?"

The room went still. Neela had read her page to her in the shed, with Pavi at her shoulder, and Kamalakshi had stood in the sun afterwards for a long time and then eaten two bowls of rice without a word. She had a girl's face still, and she was asking a plain question, as if the answer would be a floor she could stand on.

Neela said the rule as she had learned it, word for word, because anything else would be a lie in a kind coat.

"A page held by its own subject cannot be read against her by anyone else."

Kamalakshi nodded slowly. "Anyone else."

"Anyone."

It sat right. It was the same answer Ammu had given her in the straw, and Neela felt it settle into the room and take. But something under it rang wrong, like the eleventh step of the west stair. She had put her foot down and it had sounded hollow. A page, a reading, a voice. She turned it over once, and there was a gap in it somewhere, and she could not name the shape of what was missing.

She let it lie. The room had moved on.

"I'll say one more thing," Vellan said, from the door.

He had not spoken all morning. He stood with his shoulder on the frame and the pale wood bird gone from his hand, because Kamalakshi had it. He looked tired and old and very large.

"A gorge wants a wall," he said. "I'm a wall. I'm no use on a palace stair, and no use to a sneak, and no use at all with my leg to a woman who needs to be quiet." He rubbed his jaw. "I go where I'm useful. I go to the gorge. Don't ask me to like the stair."

Nobody did.

"Hands, then," Neela said.

She said it before Devan could, and he did not look as if he minded. In front of forty others, neither of them said husband, or warden, or master. Neither had those words left. They had been handed the same two words, *vote* and *hands*, and they used them.

"Those who say the whole Company holds the narrows, and none goes south."

She counted. Twenty hands. Devan's was among them, steady and open, and he did not look at her as it rose.

"Those who say a party goes south for the thirty, and the rest hold the narrows."

She counted twice. Twenty-two. One person had not raised a hand either time, and it was Thulasi, who was looking at her knees.

She put her own hand up for that one, and kept it there, and felt it shake.

"Carried," said Thilak, rough. "By two."

Nobody cheered. Pavi let out a breath and put her forehead on the heel of her curled left hand. On the far side of the plank, Devan folded his arms and looked at the ash, where his short bar and Neela's cliff sat eleven inches apart.

* * *

They sorted the rest in a quarter hour, because there was no time to do it slowly.

Six would go: Neela, because she had asked and knew the lanes of that city, or thought she might. Pavi, because the thirty were hers in a way Neela was only learning. Thilak, who said he would go with a face like a man swallowing a nail, because he had twenty-two years of knowing what a master looked like and the guard would trust it. Madhavi, because she distrusted the whole plan and would catch a lie in it. Kuttu, because he could carry. Meenu, because she asked, and had never asked for anything before.

The resin came last, and was nearly a quarrel.

"The gorge is cold and long," said Kamalakshi. "Thirty-six hold it. They'll need every pinch."

"The stair is a dark stair," said Pavi, "and a palace hasn't got the cover of a wood."

"The noon gun's daylight."

"The night after it isn't."

Neela raised a hand. "Even, as before. Every pinch the same. The six carry theirs in a leaf. Nobody takes a double for a good reason, because everyone has a good reason." She let them look at one another. "Hands."

The count came out thirty-six pinches for the gorge and six for the road. Devan took none, and nobody mentioned it. She wrote the marks on the plank herself, and Thilak cut the cup. Meenu, who had cried over a roll leaf in the Resin House, laughed out loud for no reason that anyone could name. It ran through the room like a spilled dish. For a moment they were only people eating and arguing in the rain's last week, with their heads full of ships.

* * *

Devan found her on the slab at the edge of the turf, with the lamp-black plank lashed on her knees and a coil of Thilak's twine in her hands.

The mist had cleared to the edge of the thorn. Down past the last ridge the sea was a long pewter bar, and the smoke over Pazhayangadi had thinned to a thread. A packhorse stamped somewhere behind the shelter. Someone swore at it in a rude song.

He sat down on the stone beside her, a hand's width off. For a while he said nothing.

"I voted against you," he said.

"I know." She did not look up from the knot. "I counted your hand."

"I counted yours." A pause, dry as chaff. "You voted for yourself."

"Somebody had to."

He made a sound she had heard him make three times: low, in his chest, hardly a laugh. "Pavi was almost sorry for me, I thought. She doesn't look at me like that."

"She looks at you like a wall she's decided to like."

"That's worse."

"It's the same."

Neela drew the twine tight, and the plank went flat against its backing, and she sat with her hands on it. The fisherman's hitch looked back at her.

"I didn't think you'd lose it so well," he said.

"I didn't think you'd lose it at all." She looked at him then. "The minute it went against you, you stayed in your seat with your hand where I could see it."

"I told them I'd keep to it."

"I know what you told them. I wondered what it would cost."

"Four days," said Devan, and the dryness went out of his voice as if a hand had closed on it. "You'll be on the road to Kodiyur and I'll be in the narrows. If a spear opens me there, you'll know it sitting in an inn with a cup in your hand."

"And if a stair finds me, you'll know it with a sword in yours."

"Yes."

Neither of them said the rest. It was in the plain facts: that they had chosen, in front of the others, to put a hundred miles of country between two bodies that could not be hurt apart. It would have been simpler to stay. It would have been simpler to tell the room.

"One question each," Neela said, "and no passes."

"That's not the rule."

"I'm changing it. You first."

He considered the sea. "Did you want me to say no?" he asked. "When you stood up. Did you want me to take it, and say I'd come with you, and leave the narrows to Vellan?"

"Yes."

He took that without moving.

"And I'd have hated you for it by the second day," she said. "I wanted you to offer, so I could be the one to refuse. Does that make sense?"

"It does." A breath. "Your turn."

She turned the plank over in her hands.

"Ammu would have told me not to go," she said. "She thought being unseen was the only safe thing in the world. She might have been right, for her. If the thirty don't want it—if they've been given a comfortable cage and a green sash and told it's mercy—" She stopped. "Do I have the right to walk in and open the door?"

He did not answer quickly. He had a habit, which she had learned, of answering a hard question only after he had held it a full breath.

"No," he said. "You have the right to *ask*. That's different. If you ask and they say no, then you walk out and leave a lamp." The corner of his mouth was crooked again. "I learned it from someone who counts servants."

Her face went hot, and she looked away at the sea. Four days at a hard ride. The noon gun. The sea stair.

*Usha's sentence.* She had said it in the council, and called it thin, and the room had still given her the vote. Now it sat in her head with nothing around it. Where did the stair start? Which side of the rock? Did it have a gate, a hunter, a drain? Was it one stair or a dozen, cut into the face of the cliff? She had walked the lanes behind the Kodiyur fish market once with a stick of cinnamon in her pocket and a dead man's name in her mouth. She had never seen the palace except as a pale shape from the quay.

And the only one who had given them the stair was the queen's own woman. If the sentence was a snare, it would be a good one, and a map drawn from it would be worse. Pavi would not know. Thilak would not know. Whatever Neela built on it, she could not check it against anything.

There was one person alive who could. One person who had sold the crown thirty pages over twenty years and must have carried them through that house himself, up whatever stairs the house had. Who knew the doors, and the sound each one made, and the hour the guard changed.

Chathu.

She had last seen him gagged against the dusk, a soft grey man with an uneven foot and a stare like a ledger. After the assize Rema had ordered him south in a closed cart, with her roll, to wait for the high court. Neela had watched the cart go. Then Usha had said that a cell's post at Kettamala ended in the queen's household, and that the leaf slept in the queen's room. A man the household could read was a man the household could keep. He was under the palace rock, then, or near it. She could not be sure. But if she was right, he was the one man who had climbed the stairs she meant to climb.

She sat with the plank in her hands and the sea in front of her, and felt a tin of jaggery-sesame sweets, still warm, sitting on a gatepost in her memory.

*A gift is the start of a bill.* Twelve tins, and then a thirteenth. Every kindness of his had come with a ledger line.

"What is it?" said Devan.

He had seen it. Of course he had.

"Nothing yet," she said. She had never lied to him since the night he named her the other one. She did not lie now. She said, "I've found the hole in it."

"Tell me."

She looked at him, at the grey in his hair, the seven pale scars across a right hand that would be four days of road away from her by the end of the week.

"Not yet," Neela said. "Let me see whether I can bear it."
