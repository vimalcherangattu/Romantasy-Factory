# Chapter 27: Keep the Light There

Devan woke to a hand he could not feel.

It lay on the tarpaulin beside him, palm up, and it looked like something a stranger had left there. He told the fingers to close. Nothing happened. The silence at the top of the cut was the second thing he noticed. The bear had stopped.

Grey light. Smoke and wet stone. Neela sat against his left side in Muthu's coat with a blanket over her shoulders and her bare feet drawn up. Her two fingers were still against the hollow of his throat, as if she had forgotten them there.

"Vellan," he said.

Her eyes came up. They told him she had been listening to that silence all night. "Muthu says he's sitting."

"Sitting where?"

"At the top. Against the rock. Nobody's gone near him." She swallowed. "Nobody knows what the bear left."

He tried to rise. The shoulder said no in a voice like a cleaver, and the dead arm dragged at his side. Thankam, asleep on her knees over her roll, jerked awake.

"Lie still, Warden, or I stitch you twice."

"I need to go up the cut."

"You need to lie flat for a week."

"A man is dying up there who held the narrow place for me." He heard how that sounded. "And he has something that's mine."

Thankam looked at him a long moment. Then at Neela, who said nothing and did not need to. The surgeon cursed in a coast dialect and called for the captain.

Muthu and Kandan did it with a door. It had come off a guard shed in the night, and it still had its iron strap hinge. They laid it by the tarpaulin and slid Devan onto it, and Thankam bound his arm to his chest with a green sash so the dead hand could not swing. Neela walked beside him. She was barefoot on the stones, holding the corner of the blanket with one fist. Every few steps her hand came up and touched his shoulder, then went away, as though checking a latch.

The cut climbed between walls of red rock. He saw it from his back, one ledge at a time. A bronze cap in the shingle. A ladder hook still sunk in a stake. Thurai dead lay in rows where the line had held them, and others lay singly where they had come over and not gone back. Past the stakes the river ran brown and quiet. Far down the road, toward the strand, a thin dust rose where the host was pulling away. The drums were still going, but slowly, like a heart that has finished running.

"They've drawn back," Kandan said, to nobody. "To the beach. They'll count us."

"Let them," said Muthu.

Devan said nothing. He was counting something else. At the narrow place the cut drew in until two carts could not pass, and there the dead lay thickest. They lay in a half circle, bronze and torn leather, and some of them had not been struck by anything he could name.

The bear had made a wall of them. Vellan sat against the rock behind it.

* * *

He was a man again, as the dawn had made him. He sat with his legs out and his big hands in his lap, in the grey wool of a Company tapper, and the wool was dark to the knees. Someone's hook had opened him from hip to ribs. He held his belly shut with one arm, and the other lay across his chest. The bad leg lay straight at last, with no drag left in it. A bird sat in his palm.

It was a bad bird. Devan saw that from the door, and so did the whole cut, probably. One wing was thicker than the other and the beak had split. Someone had cut it with a dull knife and no patience.

"Warden," Vellan said. His voice was a grumble with the bottom taken out. "You brought a door."

"Muthu did."

"Good man." His eyes went past Devan, to Neela, and stayed. "Seventeen. Sit. You're swaying."

Neela knelt in the blood. She did not argue. Kandan and Muthu lowered the door and stepped back, and Muthu pulled Kandan's sleeve until the two of them walked away down the cut, out of hearing. Devan was grateful. Nobody ought to see this.

"I can't do anything for it," Vellan said. "Thankam would say so. I'd not trouble her." He looked down at the arm across his belly. "Unbound. That was the point. Nobody bleeds for me. I'd have hated to hear that I'd cut anyone."

"You left the narrow place," Devan said.

"I did." The ghost of a laugh. "The line gapped. Somebody ought to fill a gap. It's an old habit."

"You told me you'd say it once you'd earned it."

"I haven't." The big hand tightened on the bird. "I've no way left to earn it. So I'll say it anyway and you can do what you like with the words. Is that fair?"

Devan found that he could not speak. Neela's fingers had come to rest on his left wrist. He turned the hand over and held on.

"Sit nearer," said Vellan.

They did. The cut was very still.

"She came down the west stair in the dark," Vellan said. "Late. Past the second watch. She had no lamp. I was low on the stair, in shape, by the grate under the eleventh step, back against the wall where the dark was thickest. I heard her before I saw her. She was counting."

Devan's mouth went dry.

"Out loud, under her breath. Forty-four, forty-three, forty-two. Slow. She had a hand on the rail. I thought, this one's afraid of the dark. It's no business of mine, but I noticed." Vellan's eyes had gone far back. "She came down a long way before she saw anything. Then, far below, at the foot, a little glimmer. A shuttered lamp, opened a thumb's width."

Neela made a sound. It was very small.

"Yours," Vellan said. He did not look at her. "You'd been told to open it twice if anyone came into the court. She wasn't in the court. She was on the stair. You opened it once, I think. Just the once, out of fright. A thumb of light."

"I don't remember," Neela whispered. "I remember counting. I remember the dark."

"You opened it. She saw it." Vellan breathed, carefully, around his wound. "And she called down. Not loud. 'Who's there?'"

Devan shut his eyes.

"You didn't answer. You were twenty-one and you'd been blindfolded and told to be a post. Quite right. And she waited. And then she smiled." The big man's voice changed. It wasn't an imitation. It was only as careful as a man handing over something that might break. "I saw it. I was close enough. She smiled at the light, and she said, 'Dev? Keep the light there. I can see the steps.'"

The cut was so quiet Devan could hear the river.

"She thought it was you, Warden. At the foot of the stair, with a lamp, for her. She thought her brother had waited up." Vellan swallowed. "She came on down, counting, with her hand on the rail. Easy. She wasn't afraid. She only saw me at the last, when she was nearly on me, and I'll tell you, she looked at the bear and then she looked back down at the light. As if to check it was still there. She never took her eyes off it."

Neela had made herself small beside him. Her hand had gone to her mouth.

"That's the whole of it," Vellan said. "I've carried it six years and I thought it would be heavier to put down. It isn't."

* * *

For a long moment Devan did not feel anything. That was how he knew it was coming.

Then it came all at once, from the place under his breastbone where the spear had passed. Six years. Six years of a wall at his back. He had slept with it, eaten with it, and never let a living soul stand behind him on a stair. He had told himself it was because he could not bear to be failed again.

But Asha had not been failed in the dark. She had come down forty-odd steps with her hand on the rail, afraid of the dark all her life, and she had looked at a thumb of lamplight and thought: *Dev's there.*

And someone had been. Not him. But it was a lamp, and a woman behind it, and a bear in the shadow of the wall that had waited, for no reason it could give, until she had smiled. It had been the worst night of his life, and for her it had ended in being sure someone was waiting at the bottom.

He did not choose to weep. The sound came up through his chest like water from a well, and the shoulder tore and the dead arm shook against the sash, and he could not stop. He wept as he had not at the pyre, or at the writ, or in the grey before dawn with Janu's lamp. It was loud and ugly. He did not turn his face.

Neela's arms came round him.

Both of them. She did not ask. She got her arms under his good shoulder and across his chest, below the wound, and pulled him against her, and held on with both hands as if he might go over an edge. He felt her cheek on his hair. He felt, through her, the shape of her own grief, which was not his grief and was the same. Her breath shook. A tear fell on his scalp.

"I opened it once," she said into his hair. "Once. I didn't know."

"You couldn't have."

"I know."

"Don't ask me to forgive you." He wasn't certain it was his voice. "There's nothing there to forgive. You gave her a light."

She said no word. She only held him tighter, and the two of them rocked together on the red stone, a man with a dead hand and a woman in a borrowed coat, over a thing neither would put down and neither would carry alone.

* * *

When he could look up, Vellan's eyes were on them.

"I'd have liked to hear what you'd have said," the big man murmured. "Well. It's said." The bird shifted in his palm. He closed his fingers over it with great care. "The girl's bird. Kamalakshi. Terrible, isn't it? She cut it with my own knife. I told her the beak was wrong. She said it was supposed to be." The grumble was fading. "Keep it where it is. I'd not like it taken."

"It stays," said Devan.

"Good. I've done what I could." The breath went in and out. "There's a Thurai boy at my feet. Fifteen. Someone ought to see that he's buried."

"I'll see it," said Neela.

"Aye. You would. You've the names." His head tipped back against the rock. His gaze went up, past them, to the top of the cut and the grey sky above it. "It's light," he said, a little surprised. "I can see the steps."

Devan did not move. Neela's arms had not loosened. After a long time, when the breath had not come again, she put two fingers to the big man's throat, the place where the pulse lay close. She held them there a good while.

Then she took her hand away and shook her head.

* * *

They carried him up the last of the cut on the door. Devan walked.

He had not asked leave. He had stood, with Muthu's arm under his good shoulder, and said, "Put Vellan on it." Nobody had argued. Four men lifted the big man between them, and his hand stayed shut on the bird, and the blanket over his face moved a little in the wind.

The cut climbed on in the new light, red and wet. Garrison men stood along it with their spears grounded. They did not salute. Some looked at the door, and some at Devan's sash, and Kuttu, the ferryman's son, stood with both hands over his mouth. Pavi had her arm round Thilak, who was not weeping but breathing like a man who had been under water. The Company had come out of the night, thirty-odd of them where there had been forty-two. Nobody counted aloud.

The lower court of Kettamala had been turned into a camp. Cook-fires, a row of cots, a well with a line already at it. Janu stood by the kitchen door with a pot in each fist, and when she saw the door and the blanket and the sash on Devan's arm, she set the pots down very slowly and did not come forward. He gave her a nod. She pressed her apron to her mouth.

Queen Nirmala was waiting by the well.

She sat on an overturned bucket, with her left arm in a bandage and Usha at her shoulder. In her lap lay a flat case of green-lacquered leather, scorched along one edge. She had it open. The leaves inside were thin and pale and mostly not leaves at all. They were black curls, ash, a drift of it on the blue cloth. A few remained whole.

She looked up as the door passed. She did not stand. She looked at the blanket, and her mouth moved a little, and she said, "The bear."

"Vellan," Neela said.

"I know his name. I paid for his kind for twenty years and never once asked it." The queen's voice was quite level. "I ask it now. It is a poor time to learn what a name weighs."

No one answered. Muthu set his end of the door in the shade of the wall, and the others laid it down with care.

Nirmala turned the case so that Devan could see.

"Twenty-six leaves have burned of themselves since the dusk," she said. "Each at its naming, as the witness entry went. Four are left, for the four who were not ready." She ran one finger along the cloth, and the ash came away grey on her skin. "Those are the four pages I can still be made to read. Mine is the voice that would cage them, if I were pressed, and every guard of the four knows it."

"Will you keep them?" said Devan.

"I have not decided. That is the worst thing I own." She closed the lid over the black. "You have your hand, Warden? No. Thankam told me. Then I will say it to the one who still has two." She looked at Neela. "You cannot hold what is left with a promise. A company needs a roll. It needs a first name on it, written by daylight where every witness can see, and someone to write it who is not afraid of the ink."

Neela stood very still in Muthu's coat, bare-footed on the court stones, with Vellan's blood to her knees.

"I will tell you what it costs, because no one told me what mine cost," Nirmala said. "The first name written binds that person to the Company's vote, whatever the hands decide. And it makes her the crown's first name to answer. Whatever the Company does, I will lay at that name. I would not ask it of anyone I did not mean to hear from."

She rose from the bucket, slowly, using Usha's arm, and the cold light caught the sunbird on the scorched case.

"Who signs first," she said, "I leave to you."

Devan looked at his own right hand hanging in its sash and thought of a pen. Then of a lamp on a stair.

Somewhere above the court, the first cistern chain clanked the hour.
