# Chapter 17: The Price of an Office

Noon came and went and nothing happened to his thumb.

Devan stood on the shelf with a sheaf of arrows in the crook of his arm, careful of the stitches at his ribs, and counted them for the third time. Forty. There was no gun here to mark the hour. There was the sun climbing over the east lip of the cut, and the shadow of the shelf wall shortening to nothing at his feet. He had watched it shorten with the whole of his attention while he told off arrows.

He held his left thumb in his right hand beneath the sheaf, where nobody could see him do it.

*She's on the road.* Two hands on the reins, a horse under her, a lime thorn in a sash she couldn't reach. That was one reason. There were two more and he had built all three with care. The fourth he had not built, and it stood outside them like a man at a door.

A welt meant a wound. No welt meant no wound. That was all the silence could tell him, and he made himself take only that and put the rest down.

"Thirty-nine," said Muthu.

"Forty."

"That's the third time you've counted that sheaf, and the second time you've got forty." Muthu sat on an upturned bucket by the handcart he had dragged down the cut at midmorning. Eleven sheaves, bound in wet cloth. He had brought a flask of the gate-house tea too, and his big laugh, which the shelf had needed more than the arrows. "I've known you twenty years. You don't miscount. You hold the number and let it go."

"The sheaf's short."

"The sheaf's fine." Muthu grinned at him. Then the grin went out of his face, because down the cut, thin and clear, a horn had started. Two long notes. A short. A long.

The crown's riders. Bhaskaran's code, carried a mile down the stone.

Devan set the arrows in the cart and let go of his thumb.

* * *

The rider came up the cut leading a horse that had stopped being a horse some miles back and become a lather on four legs. He wore a green sash and the grey of the road. He had changed mounts at four posts, and he said so the way another man would show a warrant.

"For the Warden of Kettamala," he said, and stopped. His eyes went to the plain brown cloth Devan wore, and to the captain with the iron disc at his belt. "Captain. Sir. I was told—"

"Devan."

"Devan." The rider drew a case from his saddle. "Her own hand. I was to put it in yours and no one's."

Green wax. The sunbird, spread. Devan thumbed it up and read standing.

The body was in a clerk's round hand, tidy as a row of stitches. It offered what he had known it would offer, because he had been waiting for it since the day Rema left the fort. Kettamala restored. The seal, the office, the garrison's grey. The charge of harbouring struck off the roll. And three hundred crown spears sent up the cart road to hold the pass. They would climb on the day his wife took command of the night-guard, as one company under the throne's protection. *The same is put to her in Kodiyur.*

Below it, a few lines in a quick slanting hand.

*You will think it a lever. It is. I would sooner you thought so than that I pretended. N.*

He read it twice. Then he read the middle again, slowly, as if the words might be a different size the third time.

The trouble was not that it was clever. The trouble was that he wanted it.

He wanted it the way a man wants water. The iron seal on its nail in the hall. Janu's kitchen his again. The grey coats at the padippura, and the west stair, and the cistern chain that clanked the hours so that he slept through them. Raiu's pyre had been lit in that court. Every night for five years he had told himself he did not mind being warden, and he had not known until a sheet of paper laid it out in black ink how much he minded losing it.

And the arithmetic wasn't pride. He did it, because arithmetic was what he had. Fourteen hundred on the road. A shelf wall held by thirty-six odiyans, each one spending a night in shape and a day in shaking hunger, and by garrison men who had never been asked to be anyone's wall. The chalked tile in Kandan's hand already ran to four names, and it had been a week. Three hundred spears at the narrows would be a different war. They would be a different kind of morning for Kesu's people, and the hill villages. Too late for a boy from Kalladi who had carried a spear taller than himself, but not too late for the next one.

He knew the shape of the thing anyway. Vouched, unhunted, and a knife in a drawer. The old cage with a better roof.

"Well?" said Muthu, quietly.

"Read it."

Muthu read it. He read it with his lips still, the way he did when he was working a hard line. Halfway down he looked up and said nothing, and it was not the quiet of a man lying. It was the other quiet, the one Devan had learned to tell apart. A man holding his breath between two good things.

"Devan," he said at last. "That's three hundred spears."

"I can count."

"Seventy-odd of you against that host. There's Kesu's people behind the line. There's a goat-boy at the fort and a woman at the well with a baby. If it's a yes, they live." Muthu touched the iron disc at his belt. "And this is mine now, and a yes takes it off me. I'd hand you the disc tonight and be glad of it. So hear me as a man with nothing to win." His big hands opened. "Nobody's asking you to like what you'd say yes to. It's the pass."

"I know what it is."

"Then say yes and let her sort the rest."

"That's the part I can't do."

He said it flatly, because it was the only way he had of saying anything. The rider had gone very interested in his horse's girth.

*Let her sort the rest.* Her name in the queen's mouth, and thirty lives in the Company riding on how she answered it, and the first thing the queen had done was price his fort against her yes. If he wrote *yes* to buy the pass, the answer would be his before it was hers. If he waited for her to come up the cut with the fort lying on the table between them, she would answer with it on her back. Whichever way she went, she would carry what it cost him.

"I'll answer now," he said. "Here, in front of you, and the rider. Muthu, you'll witness it."

"As seal-holder?"

"As a man who watched."

* * *

He wrote it on the lid of the arrow cart, in plain ink, with the rider's pen. He read each line aloud as he made it. It was not for the rider's benefit. A thing said where others can hear it cannot later be made smaller.

*To Nirmala, queen of Varanad. I have your letter. It is not mine to answer. My wife's answer is hers. I will not buy the pass with it, nor sell her to buy back a fort. I will hold the pass with whoever chooses to stand. If three hundred spears come with no price on her, I will thank you for them. If they come with one, I will hold without them. Devan, son of the warden of Kettamala.*

Muthu read it over his shoulder and said, "You've put a hole in your own wall."

"Yes."

"You could say *with conditions*. Take the spears and let her decide the command after."

"A condition is a yes with her name on the price."

Muthu breathed out through his nose. "She's two days up the road, if she rides as she climbs."

"The rider goes at first light."

"Then wait one night. Ask her."

"I would." Devan heard how it sounded and said it anyway. "We made a term, she and I. Say it before you do it. It was a good term. I'm doing it first."

The words hung over the cart lid between them. He had made that term in a tack room with rain coming through two roof leaks, and he had been proud of keeping it. It had held through a ledger, a raid, a hearing, a road. Now he would put his thumb to a letter she hadn't seen. She would hear it from the queen's mouth, or a clerk's, or his, and the order of it would matter more to her than the words.

*I owe her this.* He added it to the list he did not like.

He had no seal. The disc was Muthu's. He melted a bead of plain black wax over the rider's lamp and pressed his left thumb into it, the thumb she pricked. The print came up whorled and plain.

"Witnessed," Muthu said. He signed under it with the pen held like a tool. He didn't laugh when he gave it back.

Devan took a second leaf from the rider's case and wrote shorter.

*Justice Rema, Kodiyur. A roll is wanted at the Monkey Pass, north side, within the week. I am without office and the crown gives me no leave to call you. Come as recorder. Whatever is done here, held or lost, should be written by one who writes what is. Devan.*

"Rema?" said Muthu. "You'd call her here?"

"She entered Asha by name. She wrote down an execution she couldn't stop." He folded the leaf. "She records against the crown. That's the only kind of recorder I'd trust with this."

The rider took both, tucked them into his sash, and went down the cut leading his spent horse. He walked like a man who had just carried something heavy and did not know what.

* * *

The light went amber in the cut. Down the shelf, the wall pairs changed. The Company had its human night by rotation, and half were out of their shapes and hungry, hunched round a pot of rice that smelled of very little.

Vellan sat in the lee of the wall, on a flat stone near Thulasi's blanket. The big man had a knife and a block of pale wood, and the beginning of a bird in his hands. Thulasi slept with her cracked leg propped on a saddle. Vellan did not look up when Devan sat down.

"They're asking each other," Vellan said, after a while.

"Who?"

"The Company. Who they'd name, if they named. It's the sally. All that talk of shared wounds, and Neela's welts on your thumb, and who you'd trust to carry yours." The knife took a curl of wood. "Kuttu asked me."

"What did you say?"

"That I'll not bind to anyone." Another curl. "I've handed enough people my wounds."

It lay there between them. Devan knew the sum of that. Everyone on the shelf who had heard that sentence would hear it differently, and he, who had stood at the bottom of a stair at twenty-six with Janu holding a lamp, heard it the one way he could. He turned a pebble over in his pocket and found nothing to do with his hands.

He had not asked for Asha's last words. They would come when Vellan decided they were earned, and not a day before. He had let a man walk away from a reading with his back turned, and counted forty-one steps. He had not forgiven him. He had never said so, and the man had never asked.

"Then I'll mind your back," Devan said.

Vellan's hands stopped.

Slowly he turned his head. His face did what Devan had not seen it do in all the weeks: it slackened, and then it broke. A low sound came out of him, rusty, like a gate that had not opened in years. It took a moment to know it for a laugh. Thulasi stirred in her blanket and muttered. Vellan put a hand over his mouth, and the sound went on in his chest.

"You," he said.

"Me."

"I know whose back you'd be minding."

"I know whose it is." Devan kept his voice level. "I'm not forgiving you. I'm standing behind you. Those are different."

Vellan considered that for the length of one slow breath. The laugh had gone out of his face and something steadier had come in.

"They're different," he said. "That's what I'll have."

He went back to the bird. Devan stayed on the stone while the light drained out of the cut and the first oil lamp was lit down the line.

* * *

At dusk the old watcher called him to the lip.

Koman lay on his belly on the rock above the shelf, his nose lifted into the wind, the way he had done in the pass village for eleven years. Devan lay down beside him. Below them the cut dropped to the river, and beyond the narrows the cart road climbed in a long grey switchback.

Something was coming up it, and the noise of it reached them before the sight. Oxen. A groaning of axles, and a rhythm of men on ropes, sixty at least, chanting low in a language he did not have.

It rounded the bend like a house climbing a hill.

Ship-timber, black with tar. Three men high on a cart bed, lashed hides hung wet from its flanks against fire, a roofed platform at the top and slits cut in its face. A tower. It rolled a hand's breadth at a time. Behind it came a flat supply cart with a pale square on its tailboard, and painted there in fresh bright yellow, a small bird. Clean paint. He looked at it a moment longer than he needed to.

*Three men high.* He measured the shelf wall by eye. An archer on that platform would look down on every man behind it.

He opened his mouth to say so to Koman, and the old man's hand closed on his sleeve.

"There," Koman said. "In the river."

The river ran below the narrows, brown and quick, past the shingle where the cut met the road. Something broke its surface. A long dark back, low and slick, with no head to speak of. Then a tail, thick at the root, trailing like a rope. It crossed against the current with no hurry, the way a thing swims that has done it all its life. It was longer than an otter and heavier than any river-beast in the hills.

It came up on the near bank, our side, onto wet stones, and stood in the last light. The water streamed off it. Then it went into the thorn below the shelf, and the thorn closed.

"Is it ours?" Devan said.

"None of ours is that. None of ours would be out." Koman's nose was working. Eleven years he had walked the hill edge reading what the House's shapes left behind, a civet from a hare, a jackal from a fox, a bear's old musk three days cold. His voice had gone very small. "I've a name for every scent the Resin House ever made, warden. I've no name for that."

Devan let him have the word. He had given up the office that morning and the old man hadn't noticed.

Above them the tower groaned another hand's breadth up the road, and the sun went off the lip of the cut.
