# Chapter 20: One Name Each

Devan had a mile of cart road to say it in, and he spent all of it not saying it.

Neela walked beside him with her hands bound to the wrist in strips of her own hem. Behind them the Company came up the cut in a long dusty line, the queen's riders mixed among them, two wounded to a horse. The ram's horn had called a council. Muthu had the gate open before the third blast died. He met them under the padippura with his hands open and the iron disc swinging at his belt.

"The lower court's yours," he said. "As long as it takes. The crown's order says no rogues inside my walls. It says nothing about drovers who held the shelf for me."

Kandan, at the back, made a noise through his nose. Muthu did not look at Devan. He looked at the ground, and he was quiet. It was not his lying quiet. Devan had learned the difference late and was glad of it now.

So they sat in the lower court of Kettamala at midmorning, under the tamarind, on the stones where he had learned to ride. The garrison stood along the steps. Janu sent a pail of rice water across the court with a kitchen girl, and a ladle, and no scolding at all, which frightened him more than scolding would. Chellam hushed her baby by the well.

Neela stood in the middle with an upturned grain measure in front of her. She laid things on it one at a time, like a woman paying a debt.

A single stiff leaf in a slanting hand. "Usha's page. The queen wrote it fresh the night before I left, and gave it to me." Then a thick sheaf in black ink, tied with green thread. "Forty pages. Chathu wrote them at the queen's table. One for each of you."

"Ours?" said Madhavi.

"Yours. I'll read each one aloud at noon, in daylight, to its owner alone or with whoever they choose. After that it's yours to burn or keep, and nobody can read it against you." Neela's voice was flat and careful. "But everyone in the room hears your shape when I read. That's new mouths. And a master who saw you turn can write you again. Chathu told me that when I was twelve, over Kirri's rat-eaten page. The queen saw every one of her guards turn. Chathu saw every one of you. Both of them are alive. A page is a leaf. A witness is a mouth."

She gave them the offer next. Command of thirty knives with no pages on them, vouched under the crown, no hunter within a day's ride. Three hundred spears for the pass. Kettamala given back to its warden. Safe, unseen, and the queen's.

"It would work," she said. "Nobody would bleed for anybody. I want you to hear that, because it's true."

Lata's chin came up a fraction. She had braced for a lie and not been handed one.

"There is one thing that frees a person from every witness at once. Only one. It's the only way to be rid of the new mouths as well as the old." Neela set her bound hands flat on the measure. "You say your shape aloud, in your own human voice, in the minute you turn at dusk, to one person who is looking at you. Once in your life. It voids every other witness. The queen. Chathu. After that nobody can cage you without your voice joined to theirs."

"And the price," Sanu said.

"The witness page burns where it lies. You're rogue, and the crown may hunt you. And you and that person feel each other's wounds for life." She did not look at Devan. "A mortal one pulls the other toward the ground."

She stopped there. She did not say *so you should* or *so you shouldn't.* He watched her not say it, her jaw set, and thought it was harder than anything she had done on that shelf.

"I won't ask anyone to do it," she said. "I'm asking you to know what it is."

The court stayed quiet. Then Kuttu, from the stones: "She's told us her half. Somebody ought to tell us his."

Every head turned.

Devan's hand had gone to his belt pouch, for the pebble. He stopped it. A pebble kept words behind the teeth, and he needed these out.

"I've been on the other end of it since the night she named herself to me," he said. "I'll say what I have."

His voice came out rough from the smoke. He stood, because sitting felt like hiding.

"A hook opened my ribs in the narrows at noon. The same minute, it opened hers, in the queen's house, among people who would have used the sight of it. I sat on a broken ladder rung and put nine stitches in myself with Kandan's needle, and I could not tell which of us I was sewing." On the steps Kandan looked at his boots. "For four mornings I counted whether she lived by whether I woke to a welt. I couldn't ask a rider. I read my own thumb."

Nobody moved. He kept going, because stopping now would be worse.

"Mine go to her. Last night at dusk I took a burning ladder in my hands to keep it off your wall. She came down the cut with blisters to the wrist. She'd touched nothing. I did that, and I did not ask her, because there's no asking. When I swing a blade I send the blow to the one person I would never choose to hurt."

He made himself look at Neela. She was looking at the measure.

"There's the other side. I know she's breathing. It's the only reason I slept at all. I can't tell you it's worth it for you. I can tell you what I paid and what I got. Don't do it for the wall. Don't do it because I stood here and said it was bearable. If you do it, do it because there's one person you would rather bleed with than be safe without."

"And if there isn't anyone?" Lata said.

"Then you stay vouched, with a roof and a lamp, and I would say that to the queen's face. It isn't nothing."

Lata studied him. "No," she said. "It isn't."

"So we vote on it," Madhavi said. "On who goes with who."

"No." Neela lifted her head. "No vote can choose whom you bind to. You ask one person. They have to be looking at you at dusk, in the minute. Nobody can look for you, and nobody can say yes for you."

For a time nobody did anything. A hen walked across the court.

Pavi stood first. She was small and quick, twenty-four, with a white scar on her chin and an old-broken wrist she held a little crooked. "Thilak," she said.

Thilak was turning a cup in his hands. "I'm forty-one," he said. "I bleed slow and mend slower."

"I know."

"You'll have my knees."

"And you'll have this." Pavi lifted the wrist. "You've been bringing me tea over it for a month."

He laughed once, an ugly, surprised sound. "Yes," he said, and set the cup down as if it might spill.

Kuttu didn't stand. He crossed the court to the gate arch, where Bhaskaran stood with his horn on his belt and his arms folded. "Sergeant," Kuttu said.

"I have four daughters," said Bhaskaran.

"I know."

"Their mother will have words." He looked at the horn for a while. "Twenty years I've carried this. Never carried anyone." He unfolded his arms. "Yes. Ask me when it's dusk."

Kamalakshi was on her feet before the murmur finished. She turned in a slow circle, taking in the whole court. "No," she said. "Not you, or you, or you. I've been looked at by enough people who could do something about it. I'll look at myself." She sat down, and from somewhere near the wall came a short, cracked laugh that might have been hers.

Vellan sat in the doorway with his knife and a scrap of pale wood. He did not lift his head. "No," he said. "I told the girl once."

Sanu was watching Sajan. Sajan put one hand on his brother's shoulder and said nothing, which was not a yes, and was not a no. Sanu looked at the hand as if it were a letter.

"That's all anyone has to say today," Neela said. "The vote on the queen's offer is tonight, after we eat."

* * *

Gopan had been oiling the cistern wheel when they came up the east steps. He took one look at the two of them and found somewhere else to be.

The housing was cool and smelled of wet iron. Neela stood with her back to him and her bandaged hands pressed to the stone, as though it were a fever she was trying to draw out. Somewhere below, a ladle rang in a pail.

"How will you vote?" she said. She asked it of the wall.

"I don't need to. I've answered."

"Answered who?"

There was no more road to put it off on. "The queen. Her letter came while you were still on the road. I wrote back within the hour. I refused Kettamala. I wrote that the spears weren't mine to take or leave, and that the Company would answer for itself. Muthu witnessed it. The rider was on the road by morning."

She did not move. He counted the chain through two links.

"You tried to say it at the wall," she said. "The horn cut you off."

"It would have cut me off. I was working up to it. That's not the same."

"No." She turned. Her voice dropped and slowed, each word set down apart from the next. "In the tack room, with the buckets going. *Say it before you do it.* You held the string while I tied that."

"Yes."

"Do you know what I carried down the road?" Her voice stayed low. "Your fort. Four days. If I said no to her, I was saying no to the roof you were born under. I decided I could pay it. I'd decided before Kodiyur was out of sight. I was going to pay it with my eyes open, in front of the Company, and you'd already paid it for me, with a pen." She drew a breath. "I carried a thing that was gone. And now I can't know what you'd have said if you'd waited. Or whether it was a no said for my sake."

He had meant to start with the excuse and put the apology after it. Hearing her, he saw there was no room for the order.

"I broke it," he said. "There's a reason. The rider was ready, you were four days off, the queen wanted an answer. It's true, and it makes the breaking no smaller. I could have held the rider a night. I could have sent him back with nothing. I didn't, and I should have. I'm sorry."

"Don't say it like a report."

He tried again. "I took the price out of your hands before you'd chosen to pay it. That was yours. I'm sorry." The words were not any better, but they were truer. "I said no because the fort isn't worth the Company's vote. That's the whole reason. I won't dress it up as sparing you. You'll have to take that on trust. If I can't tell you first, I'll tell you that I can't, and why. Before."

She watched him. Her thumbnail dragged along her forefinger nail, the bandage leaving the tips bare.

"Hear the rest," she said. "I'm still angry."

"I'm listening."

"Tonight, when the Company votes, I'll say what I think of the offer. If I think you're wrong, I'll say that too, in front of all of them. I'll speak against you if I must." She kept her palms on the stone. "And tomorrow at the gate, you don't answer for me. Not a word. Not to spare me, and not to spare the fort."

It cost him something to hear it, and she watched it land. "Yes," he said.

"You said that fast."

"Then I'll say it slower. I will not answer for you at the gate." He made himself go on. "Here's what I mean to say to her tomorrow, so you have it before I do. That I refused Kettamala in writing, and that was my act alone. That the Company hasn't answered, because the Company hasn't voted. And that I'll say nothing of what you are unless you say it first."

She was silent a while. "Good," she said. "Stand a step behind me when you say it."

"A step."

She turned back to the wall and set her palms on the stone again, and he set his on it a hand's width from hers. The cold of it went into both of them together. He felt the answering ease in his skin half a breath behind her own, and knew she felt his.

"It's mending," she said, after a while. "It isn't mended. It isn't forgotten."

"I'd think less of you if it were."

She nearly smiled. It went out of her face again at once, because someone was coughing at the foot of the steps.

Koman came up with his hands clasped before him, like a man at a naming. He was old, thin, and sunburned to the colour of a roof tile. Nobody had hired him to climb a mile of road, and he had done it anyway. "Warden," he said. Devan had given up correcting him.

"Say it, Koman."

"The long shape is in the river again." He kept his eyes on the floor. "Same bank as before, below the shingle. It came in after the tower fell, at the middle watch, and lay in the shallows with only the eyes above until the stump stopped smoking. It took nothing. It didn't touch the dead on the bank, or a horse. It watched the shelf. When the smoke thinned, it went under." He shook his head. "Eleven years I've read that water. Nothing the Resin House ever made smells like that."

Neela's eyes found Devan's. Neither of them said anything. Whatever lay in that river wanted nothing from the dead. A thing that watched and did not take had been sent, or had come to count.

"Thank you," Devan said. "Go and eat."

Koman bowed, badly, and went down. At the landing he stood aside for a woman coming up with her sleeves dragged to her knuckles.

Usha stopped inside the door and did not come further. She looked at the two of them, and at the hand's width between their hands, and took the news into the room like a stone she meant to set down gently.

"The column reaches Kettamala tomorrow at noon," she said.

"Koman said a day and a half."

"Koman read the dust at grey. I went up the lip with him at midday and read the outriders." Her voice was level. "When she rides with her people, the outriders go in pairs, always, on both flanks. The banners say her household. The pairs say her."

Neela's bound hands closed on the stone.

"She's with them," Usha said. "The queen is riding in her own column. She'll be at your gate by noon."

The chain clanked the hour. A queen who sent a letter and a lever did not climb a hill road to hear no. She came up it to answer. Devan found he had already counted the steps from the gate to the pass, and did not like the number.
