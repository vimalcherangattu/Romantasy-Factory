# Chapter 7: Ammini's Girl

The old woman's needle did not move, and neither did Neela's hands.

Eleven people sat in the doorways of the village. She counted them without meaning to, and counted the two men by the well and the child on a hip. The knot had fallen out of her sash. Her fingers had made it again and stopped halfway, like a guilty dog.

Ten paces back, Devan stood with Kesu at the well. He had seen her stop. He did not come. He turned his head and said something to the one-eyed old man, and the two of them became very interested in a bucket.

"Again," the old woman said, in the coast tongue. She did not look up. "Slowly."

Neela undid the hitch and tied it. Loop, loop, pull.

"Who taught you that?"

"A net-mender," Neela said. "Behind the sheds, where I was raised. That's what I was told."

"Told." The old woman's mouth worked. "Sit down, girl. Sit like you're buying fish. Nobody here is watching a woman buy fish."

Neela sat on the cloth beside her with her knees drawn up. The woman picked the net off the dust and laid it across both their laps, so that two pairs of hands bent over one tear in the mesh. It was a neat trick. From the doorways they were two women haggling over thread.

"Ponni," the woman said. "Fishwife. Nellimala, till I was put out of it. Now I sleep in Kesu's cattle shed and he says I snore."

"Do you?"

"Like a boat on shingle." The needle went in and out. "You ask questions fast. Ammini did that. She'd answer a thing with a thing, and you'd walk away having told her your whole week."

Neela's throat shut. She made herself breathe through it. "Who was Ammini?"

"The best net-mender on that coast. She had hands like yours. She'd tie that hitch on a fishing line, a pepper sack, a child's shoe." Ponni glanced up at last. Her eyes were clouded at the edges, and very sharp in the middle. "She had a girl who'd copy her. Sat on the sand at four years old with a bit of cord, tying and tying. Cross as a cat when it came out wrong."

"What was she called?"

"You know what she was called."

Neela did not answer. Across the road a woman rocked a baby, and a boy fed a crust to a thin dog. The well chain clanked. She said, "Tell me anyway."

Ponni looked at her for a long moment. Then she bent over the net.

"Indu," she said.

It went through Neela like cold water. She had read it off a leaf in the dark. She had said it to Devan across a lamp. She had never heard it said by a mouth that had said it before.

"The second harvest," Ponni said, low and even. "You'll want that, I think. Twenty-one years back. We were drying fish on the racks. Pepper was in. The boats came at dusk, two of them, straight over the bar at slack water with no pilot. Nobody crosses that bar without knowing the marks. They knew the marks." Her needle stopped. "They came in like men who'd been told the way. Like men who were expected."

"Expected by whom?"

"If I knew that, I'd have said it to somebody." Ponni's lip curled. "Nobody asked. The women got the children up the goat path. The old ones stayed with the fires so the raiders would go for the fires. I had my own two boys by the collar. I ran with you in my arm for the first bend, and then Ammini took you."

*I ran with you in my arm.* Neela's hands lay dead on the net.

"And at the top?"

"That's what I've carried." Ponni's voice dropped further. "There were men at the top. Not raiders. Raiders came from the sea, in hide and bronze. These were men in grey, standing in the road, quiet as posts. They had a cart. A big flat cart with a tailboard. A little bird was painted on it, yellow and small. The paint was new."

Neela said nothing. The word *grey* sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

"They didn't hurt anyone. That's the thing I can't set down. They took the youngest, the ones who'd stopped crying out of shock. They lifted them like sacks of rice. One of them counted. I remember he counted. And I ran, with my boys, because I was a coward." Ponni's face did not change. Her hands did, a small tremor in the left. "I never saw where the cart went."

"I know the path," Neela said.

It came out of her without leave.

"I know it. Left of the lightning boulder, up the scree, across the shoulder, down through thorn to an old well. I've walked it twice this year without a map, and I couldn't say how." Her voice thinned. "I was carried up it. On someone's back. I remember the thorns on my legs. Somebody was breathing very hard."

"Ammini," Ponni said. "She went up after you. She had her net weights still in her apron. I heard them clink."

"And she came down."

Ponni's eyes met hers. They were wet, and she let them be.

"She went up," she said. "She did not come down."

Neela sat quite still. A fly walked along the net. Somewhere a pot lid rattled. She had thought she would have to ask for a mother, a name, a face, and she had been handed all three in the same breath. They sat in her like stones in a bag.

"My name is Indu," she said.

Her voice shook, and she let it. "I was Indu. They gave me a number. I've been a number for twenty-one years. I have not said that name to anyone who knew me before."

"I know," said Ponni gently. "Your hands said it. You needn't have."

"I wanted to."

"Then it's said." The old woman slid the net aside and put her knotted hand over Neela's. "I'm not telling this to the village, girl. You've the look of a woman who has to be somebody else by evening. Go and be her. I'm here. I snore, but I'm here."

Neela turned her hand over under Ponni's and held on.

* * *

The Company read its rolls by the fire at the lightning boulder, because nobody could sleep.

It had started with Thulasi. She had her page and the long strip fastened to it, and she had sat with it in her lap through the whole of supper, not reading, only holding. Then Kuttu had said, "Well? It's your village," and she had unfolded it with Pavi's help and said *Pazhayangadi* aloud as if it were a coin she feared was false.

Now forty people sat in a ring with scraps of leaf. Thilak had burned his own. He listened, one hand flat on his knee. Meenu read hers with her lips moving, a letter at a time. Neela went from one to another with the lamp, helping where the cipher stuck.

She was good at it. She could hold a stranger's loss in a lamp's yellow circle and keep her hand steady. She had got through the evening so, with Ponni's voice in her ear: *a little bird, yellow and small.*

She had told the gorge nothing. A pass village could hold its own secrets, but she could not ask it to hold hers.

"Mine's gnawed," Kuttu said. He held up the roll. A corner was missing, ragged as a torn cheek. "Rats. Look. They've had my father's name."

"Let me see."

She took it. The edge was chewed through a line, and the leaf was thin at the break. She turned it to the lamp.

And a locked door shut in her head.

She was twelve. Perhaps thirteen. Chathu's room smelled of lamp oil and jaggery. The door stood a hand's breadth open, because she had been told to wait with a tray. Inside, Kirri sat on the stool with her chin up, seventeen and proud and white about the mouth. Chathu was bent over a table. A rat-eaten page lay in front of him, brown at the edges. He uncorked a pot of resin-ink and wrote, line by line, in a hand she could have read from the passage.

"There," he'd said, so softly. "Not to worry, child. A master who saw you turn can always write you again."

Kirri had smiled up at him.

Neela blinked. Kuttu was staring. The lamp had tipped, and she righted it.

"It's mended in my head," she said, "that's all. Chathu did it once for a sister. Wrote a page out fresh, after rats."

"Kind of him," Kuttu said, without any irony at all.

"Yes." She handed the leaf back. "He was kind."

She filed it where she had always filed it. Under mercy, under the soft hands that wrote the cage. Chathu was in a cell in Kodiyur with his sweets taken away. It was an old kindness and he was a cell's worth of mercy. She did not look at it again.

When she raised her head, Devan was watching her across the fire.

* * *

He found her afterward at the edge of the light, sitting on the ledge of the boulder where the stone was still warm. The pebble was gone from his cheek. He sat a hand's width off and stayed quiet as long as it took her to realise he meant to.

"You went somewhere," he said.

"Kuttu's roll. A memory." She dragged her thumbnail along her forefinger. "Nothing. Chathu once mended a page. It's nothing."

"You counted at the well."

"Did I?"

"Lips moving." There was no smile in it. "And you sat with the old woman an hour. Kesu says she's called Ponni. I didn't come over."

"I know. Thank you."

"I want it said I could see you from where I stood."

"I know that too." She drew breath. "Her name's Ponni. She's a fishwife from Nellimala. She knew my mother. My mother's name was Ammini, and she mended nets, and she tied my knot." It came out in a rush, in order, like a list of exits. "The boats came over the bar at dusk without a pilot, as if they'd been told the marks. The women sent the children up the goat path. At the top there were men in grey, with a cart. A tailboard with a little yellow bird painted on it. They took the youngest. And my mother went up after me, and Ponni never saw her again."

She had said it all in one breath. Afterward she sat with her mouth shut, nails dug into her palm.

Devan did not move.

"Men in grey," he said.

"Yes."

"I'll say what I thought of, and then I'll say what it's worth." He was looking at the dark where the road ran down to the sea. "The man who came to Muthu the winter Asha died. He wore a grey cloak. He asked for a monthly letter *to keep the warden safe*. That's what Muthu told me, in the kalari pit, after Ittan." He turned his head. "But the Kettamala garrison wears grey. Half the wardens' men in the hills do. Half the pass villages dress their ploughmen in the same cloth. It's cheap and it takes the dye."

"So it means nothing."

"It means I thought of it, and you should know that I did. I don't know more." He said it slowly, each word set down like a stone on a wall. "I won't build on it. I won't ask you to."

She let out the breath she had held since the well. It was not a shout, or a laugh. It was something near both.

"You could have said it was the crown."

"I could have. I'd have been guessing, and you'd have had to carry my guess as well as hers."

"Twenty-one years ago," she said. "You'd have been eleven."

"I was learning to fall off a pony."

"I was learning to be Seventeen."

He took that in. After a while he said, "Tell me the rest, if there's any."

"There's the path." She looked at her hands. "I knew it the way I know my own teeth. I walked it for the first time this year and I knew where the thorns were. Somebody carried me up it and I didn't remember. My body did. The lantana, the knot, the path." She laughed, dry. "I've been tripping over my own childhood for months and calling it luck."

"It isn't luck."

"No."

The fire popped behind them. Down the bank someone was singing, low and off the note, the way Ammu used to. It might have been Pavi. It might have been the wind.

"Devan."

"Yes."

"We leave for Kodiyur at first light. Six of us. I don't know what's at the end of that road. I don't know who is at the end of it." She made herself look at him. "If I don't come back—"

"Neela."

"If I don't, I want somebody to have it. Not on a slip. Not in a ledger anyone can seize." Her voice cracked. "Someone who'll keep it as it was said. My mother's name was Ammini. She was a net-mender of Nellimala. She went up the goat path after her daughter, and she didn't come down."

It was a gift, and it cost her. A name could be written down. A name could be read aloud against you. Every leash she had known began with someone knowing a thing and holding it. She held it out anyway, on the open palm of her mouth, and did not look away.

He took it as if it had weight.

"Ammini," he said.

He said it right. He said it once, with the broken vowel at the front of it, as Ponni had said it, as if he had been listening at the well after all. Then he stopped. He did not say it again, to be sure of it. He did not make it a promise, or a ribbon, or a lamp.

"I'll keep it," he said. "You'll take it back from me yourself."

"That's not a promise."

"No." He looked at her, level, grey-eyed. "It's what I'd like. I won't swear to what I can't make true. But I'll keep this. Nobody will have it from me."

She leaned until her shoulder was against his. The stone was warm beneath them both.

Far down the road the sea wind came up through the gorge, bringing salt, and under the salt a thread of green and bruised sweetness, rank and ordinary and her own. She breathed it in. She did not hate it.

*Ammini. Indu.* A yellow bird on a tailboard.

Someone had counted the children as they were lifted. She lay awake a long time wondering who had held the tally, and for whom.
