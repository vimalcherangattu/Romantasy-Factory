# Chapter 12: The Noon Gun

The noon gun fired while the thorn was in her thumb.

Half the quay ducked. Neela, who had been waiting for it, only pressed. One. Two. Three small stings, bright as pinpricks of lamplight, and she dropped the thorn into the gutter and curled her hand shut. Four days behind her, in a cut full of broken ladders, a man would be sitting down on a rock.

*Three. Alive.*

The echo rolled off the harbour tower and out across the water. Kodiyur went back to its shouting.

Four days of road had gone by in a page of dust. The sea road was dry, and the horses were good, and she had counted the milestones because counting was what her mind did when the rest of her wanted to turn round. Pavi rode close and said little. Meenu snored under the cart. Every noon Neela had got down in some roadside field, pricked her thumb three times, and climbed back up without a word. Nobody asked. By the second day Kuttu had started to look away at noon, the way you look away from a person praying.

Now the road had run out at salt water.

Kodiyur stank gloriously. Split pepper sacks, tarred rope, drying fish racked on poles to the horizon. A clove-and-wet-sack smell came from the north warehouses, where a Kunjan Chirayil had once kept a ward who never existed. Cranes of rope and timber leaned over the quays. And across the mouth of the harbour, a chain as thick as a man's thigh hung up out of the water on its floats, with grey sails waiting beyond it. She counted eleven before she stopped.

Thurai, sitting off the mouth like crows on a fence.

Nobody on the quay looked at them for long. A porter spat. A fishwife crossed two fingers over her basket.

The five had gone in at the Salt Gate that morning behind a pepper cart, with Thilak driving and the rest in the sacks. They would lie in a salt store near the tanneries until she sent word. Neela had taken the Hill Gate alone. She had done her face and her wrists in a gully at first grey, kneeling over a pool.

Lime from the pass village, ochre from a potter's cutbank, mixed with water to a grey-gold mud. She had smeared it thick across her throat, over the ridged scars, and let it dry. It had gone chalky and tight. It cracked when she swallowed. Over her wrists she had buttoned two stiff cuffs of green cotton, starched with rice water, pegged at the knuckle the way Usha's were. The wedding bangle sat under the left one, a hard gold line against the bone.

*Don't touch it,* Usha had said, at the shelter. *Not once. A hand at the throat is a tell.*

Neela kept her hands down and walked.

* * *

The woman who sold dried mackerel at the third stall from the weighing house was called Rukmini, and her scale-boy was Pappu. Neela knew it before she reached them. She had stood at the next stall for a quarter hour, buying nothing, listening to the boy being scolded. It was a habit the guild had never broken her of. A woman who needed to stay unseen should know no one's name. She knew all of them.

"You're early," said a voice at her shoulder.

Usha wore her green sash at the left hip and a plain brown wrap over it, like any palace servant on an errand. Her sleeves were pulled to the knuckle. She didn't look at Neela. She looked at the mackerel.

"The roads were dry," Neela said.

"The chain's been up eleven days. Nobody's coming in by sea, and nobody's going out." Usha took up a fish by the tail, sniffed it, and set it down. "Don't turn your head. Two men by the salt-weigher. Port watch. They're counting who walks in from the hill."

"I counted them at the gate."

"Good." Usha almost smiled. "Walk with me. You carry yourself like a woman with a sword. Lose that."

They went up through lanes so narrow the washing hung across the sky. Neela dropped her shoulders. She let her stride go short and pinched. After forty paces Usha grunted, which she took for approval.

"The others call me First," Usha said. "I'll bring you to three of them. They'll want to see you before they decide anything. They won't use names."

"I will."

"You'll have to ask."

"I'll ask," said Neela.

A drying shed stood at the end of a lane behind the fish market, long and low, hung with split fish gone amber in the dark. Salt and smoke were laid on the air like a second roof. Off-duty guards used it, Usha said. It was the one place in Kodiyur the palace did not listen. Nobody who valued their dinner went near it.

Neela went in and counted the doors. Two. A third in the back wall behind the nets.

Three people waited among the racks.

* * *

A man sat on an upturned basket with his sleeves down to his knuckles and a face like a closed shutter. A woman a little older leaned on a post and looked at Neela's throat. A third, younger than both, sat apart on the floor with his arms round his knees. He had the sulky, scrubbed look of a boy who had been told to wait outside.

"Second," said Usha. "Third. Fourth. This is Seventeen."

"Neela," said Neela. "And you are?"

The man stared. "We don't—"

"I know. Tell me anyway."

A silence. Fish swung overhead on their strings.

"Sajan," the man said at last, as though handing over a coin.

"Lata," said the woman.

The youngest drew a breath. "Sanu."

"Sanu." Neela went down on her heels in front of him. "Usha says you've something to tell me."

He looked at the floor. His hands were pulling at his cuffs. "I carried notes," he said, in a small cramped voice. "For a year. Not to the palace. Out. A man in a grey cloak gave them to me in the lower court. I sealed each one in a pot of salve, and I gave the pots to the ninth-day post rider bound for Kettamala. They were always for the physician at the fort. Eravi." He swallowed. "I only went up myself once, with the escort the queen's clerk sent. I had a blistered heel. I left a pot at his dispensary, as thanks, and I never read a note. I swear I didn't. I thought they were medicine orders. The Keeper's wife was sick."

"Which pot?"

"Green clay. Bark salve. It smelled of..."

"Scorched tamarind and pine sap," Neela said. "I kept the pot."

He flinched. Sajan looked at her sharply.

"I was glad of the salve," she said. "I'd have used it either way."

It did not seem to help. Sanu pressed his forehead to his knees. "A man died at Kettamala," he said, muffled. "On the stair, in the dark. I've done sums on it. The lamps. If I'd read one note..."

"His name was Raiu," Neela said. "And you'd have read one note. They'd have found another boy." She had said it often in her head, over Ammu. It was as flat and true as a floor. "Eravi's held. He's told us what he sent. You're the first of them to say it to my face. That's worth something."

He lifted his head. His eyes were red.

"What did the man in grey say?" she asked. "When he gave you the notes."

"Nothing. He never said anything." Sanu shook his head. "He just counted me. Every time. Like I was a coin."

*Counted.* She put it away with the rest.

* * *

The door at the front banged and a woman came in with a leather satchel. She was fifty or so, brisk and broad, grey hair scraped under a cloth, and she carried herself like one who has put her hand in a great many wounds and thought nothing of any of them. She looked at Neela once and past her.

"Thankam," Usha said, with no warmth and no coldness. "Palace surgeon. She sees us here. The infirmary has ears."

"Everything with a roof has ears." Thankam set the satchel on the basket Sanu had left. "Lata, your arm."

Lata rolled back a green cuff. The forearm beneath was a mess of angry red, split along the muscle by a bad scrape. Thankam washed it, humming, and then she opened the satchel and took out a knife.

It was small. A hand's length, the blade thin as a leaf and curved at the point like a claw. It was the kind of knife used for peeling fruit or paring a hoof. Neela watched it come out of the leather, and her whole body went still.

Thankam did not use it on Lata's arm. She laid it on the basket beside her gently, as one might lay a spoon. While she bound the wound she talked of the tide, and the price of linen, and the bad temper of the Keeper's widow, who still kept his window open in the rain. Her bare forearms showed under the rolled sleeves. There was no seam on them.

But Lata's other wrist had slipped from her cuff as she shifted. Neela saw it. A flat pale line, a finger long, no ridge to it, no layers. Smooth as a stitched silk edge. Somebody had cut away twenty years of burned skin and sewn the rest back neat.

Her own scars lay thick on the inner wrist, ridge on ridge, the first laid down at sixteen. She felt every one now, under green cotton.

"Looser at the wrist," Thankam said, without turning. She had her eyes on the bandage. "That cuff will chafe. It'll chafe worse on the next fortnight, on a hard wall."

It was said to Lata, and it was not. Neela kept her face still. After a moment, Thankam picked up her knife and wiped it, clean and unhurried, on a cloth.

"You'll want a fortnight to heal after," she said, to the room. "The palace is not generous with that." She shut the satchel and went out, humming, and the door banged behind her.

Nobody spoke for the length of a long breath.

"She's kind," said Lata, rewrapping her cuff. "People forget that. She is."

"She is," Sajan said. "She cut it off me with a clean hand and told me I'd feel better." His voice had gone flat. "I did feel better. That's the worst of it."

* * *

They sat on baskets. Usha poured water from a clay jar and gave Neela the first cup. Lata drank hers standing.

The thirty, Neela learned, were not one mind. She had hoped for it on the road without quite saying so. She heard the shape of it in the first quarter hour: the thirty were close to evenly split, and not about courage.

"Some want their pages," Usha said. "They've heard what you did at the Resin House. Some have heard it as a rumour, some as a hymn. They'd go to you at a run if they thought the gate was open."

"And some want to stay," Neela said.

"Some love her," Lata said.

Sajan made a sound. Lata turned on him.

"I'm not a fool. I know what she is. I know what I am." She spoke quickly now, like one who had said it in her head a hundred times. "But she answers every widow's petition herself. In her own hand. Every one, from the border, from the pepper villages. When my brother's wife wrote about the death-gift, it came back in a week. A letter, Neela. Not a clerk's. I know her hand." She stopped. "That is the woman who kept me out of a kennel. That is the woman I'd be sending to a gallows."

"She also signs the writs," Sajan said.

"I know that too."

"She held the lamp," said Sajan. "Ask them that. Ask them what she held."

Neela looked at Usha.

Usha was quiet a moment, turning her cup. "She watches each of us turn," she said. "The first time. In her own chamber, with the shutters closed. She holds the lamp herself. She won't let a servant do it. She holds it high so she can see every moment, and then she sits the night through in a chair and says nothing at all. Not a word. At first light, when you're a woman again on her floor, she kneels down and tells you your shape."

"To you," said Lata. "Gently."

"So you know what you are," said Sajan. "And so she does. And so you're caged by the only person alive who's ever held a light to you."

"She's never said it at night," Lata said.

"She'd never have to start. She only has to open her mouth after dusk."

Nobody answered that. Neela felt her own scars drag under her cuffs. She thought of Chathu, a long time ago, sitting a girl down at a table. A kindness first. Then the lamp.

"Tell me about the sea stair," she said, because the room needed a different floor.

"It leads from the lower cells to the water gate," said Usha. "There's a landing halfway where the cliff bends. Two guards stand it. They change at the sixth bell." She answered at once, like a woman reading it off a wall. "The change takes sixty breaths. The new pair comes down from the top while the old pair goes up by the chain stair, and for the last twelve breaths there's a gap where no one stands the landing. It's twelve if the rain's off and nine if it's blowing."

Sixty breaths. Twelve. Nine. Neela noted it. It was an uncommonly exact thing to know, and Usha had said it with no pause to count. She turned it over once, the way you turn a coin to see which side the maker pressed hardest.

She did not ask.

She had a number for every door in this city already. If Usha wanted her to have this one, Usha could say why. In the guild, a woman who asked too early was a woman who got told lies.

*Later,* she thought. She put it at the back of the shelf, beside the green-sealed quire and the man in grey who counted children.

* * *

It came as she lifted the cup.

A line of fire opened across her back, shoulder blade to hip. It wasn't a prick or a scrape. It was long and hot, as if somebody had drawn a hot iron down her spine and then down it again.

She did not drop the cup. She set it on her knee, very carefully, with both hands. She breathed through her teeth.

*Devan.*

Not a graze. A blade, or a ladder-hook, laid across his back in a long clean pull. She felt the welt rise under her shirt, stinging, pushing at the cloth. It went on being cut, a slow count of five, then six.

Then it stopped.

Nothing followed. No second line, no tearing. No pull toward the dark. Only the welt, swelling, and her pulse beating in it. Whatever it was, it had not been the kind that kills. It might still be the kind that does.

"Seventeen." Usha had put down her cup. "You've gone grey."

"It's nothing."

"Is it him?"

Neela gave no answer. Sajan's shuttered face turned toward her with a new look in it. Lata glanced from one to the other.

If you bleed, I won't. She had said it in a warm room with rain on the roof. She had said it twice, so that it would stay said. She sat on a basket in a shed full of fish, with her fingers dug into the starched cotton of her cuffs, and for a long, awful moment her whole body leaned toward the door. A hundred miles of road. Four days of it. She would arrive for the burying.

She did not stand.

"He's living," she said. Her voice came out odd and low. "If he weren't, I'd be on the floor. It's a cut. It's long. It isn't deep." She looked at her hands. "I can't send him anything. There's nothing to send but the thorns, and the thorns are for noon. Three, tomorrow. That's all he'll get."

"That's all?" said Sanu, in a frightened whisper.

"That's the bargain." She lifted her head. "It's a good one. It's holding."

Usha was watching her. For a moment Neela thought she saw something in that watchful face go very soft and very sorry, and then it was gone, as if a hand had passed a cloth over it.

"There is another thing," Usha said slowly. "I wasn't going to say it till tonight."

"Say it now."

Usha looked at Sajan, who looked at the floor. She drew a long breath through her nose, like a woman about to lift something she knew was heavy.

"Chathu has asked for Seventeen," she said. "In his cell. Three days now, to anyone who'll carry it. He won't say why." She turned her cup a quarter turn. "He says you'd come."
