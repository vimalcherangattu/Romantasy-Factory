# Chapter 22: The Queen at Kettamala

Neela counted the spears from the padippura roof and got to three hundred and four before the first of them reached the shingle bank.

They came up the cart road in a double file, green pennants limp in the noon heat. Grey dust rose behind them. At the head rode a woman on a grey horse, one hand loose on the rein. No woman who knew she was going to be refused rode like that. Usha walked at her stirrup, bareheaded, sleeves pulled to her knuckles. Behind them, in a tight knot, rode seven guards in green sashes. Neela knew every one of their names from the drying shed in Kodiyur. They had been offered their pages and had said no. The eighth who had said no, Lata, was not with them. She had ridden north with Usha's riders and was already up on the shelf, and she had not looked at the road once.

"Three hundred and four," Neela said.

"Three hundred and three," said Devan, behind her. "One's a boy with a drum."

The garrison lined the court in grey. Bhaskaran's horn stayed at his belt. Nobody had told the Company to hide, so the Company did not. They stood at the back of the lower court, forty-one of them, in their own mud-coloured cloth, and let the queen look.

She looked. The gate took her through under the tamarind, and she slid from the saddle without help. She was fifty-four, small, her hair gone iron-grey under a plain white cloth. She wore no crown. Her hands were ink-stained at the second finger, like a clerk's.

The garrison began to kneel. It went along the steps in a ripple, spears grounding.

"Stand," Nirmala said, in the warm unhurried voice Neela had heard through the screens outside the sea room. "This is a warden's court, and I'm his guest. Neela. I'd rather see your face than the top of your head."

Neela stayed on her feet. She had planned no speech for it. She stood where she was, hands bound in clean strips, and said, "Majesty. Kettamala is glad you've come. It was not told you were coming."

"It wasn't. I hoped to be forgiven." The queen's eyes went past her to Devan and rested there with what looked like real warmth. "Warden."

"Majesty." Devan did not bow lower than a warden bows. His voice was level and carried to the back steps. "I hold a paper with your wax on it. I'll read it when my wife has answered you. Not before."

Spear butts shifted on the stone along the steps, then went still. Neela kept her eyes on the queen's face and did not turn, because they had agreed he would say it and she would hear it, and she heard it.

The queen's brows lifted a little. "Then I'll wait on your wife," she said. "I'm told it's the only way to get anything done in this house."

Muthu came down from the gate chamber stair with the iron disc swinging at his belt and his big hands empty. He did not bow. He touched two fingers to his brow, as a captain does to a guest, and stood straight. The queen studied the disc, and then him.

"You hold my seal, Captain."

It was a test, laid so lightly that half the court would not have felt it.

"I hold the border's, Majesty." Muthu's voice was quiet and steady. He was not lying. "It was given into my hand by the man who wore it five years, and I hold it for the villages. If the border wants it back on a different neck, the border will say so."

Somewhere behind him a spear butt scraped a step. Neela watched the queen weigh the answer, as she had watched men weigh a blade at a throat. Then Nirmala smiled, and let it stand.

"A good answer. I need a man who holds the pass more than I need one who holds my seal." She turned. "Is there a table in this house? I have a petition waiting."

* * *

The hall had eight pillars. Neela had counted them on her first morning as a bride, and counted them again now, because it kept her mind steady. Janu had put out rice and curd and plain water, no better than for any guest, and stood against the wall with a tray in both hands.

The queen sat in the warden's chair. Devan did not take the seat beside it. He came and stood behind Neela's bench, near enough that she could hear him breathe. He had said he would stand at her back and not be her wall, and he stood a step back, as she'd asked.

"Before anything else," the queen said, "I'll hear who is waiting."

Neela had expected a ceremony of speeches. Instead a thin woman in a washed grey wrap was brought up from the court, coughing into her sleeve, and held out a tally stick as if it might burn her.

"Parukutty," said Neela, before she could stop herself.

The woman flinched at her own name. The queen looked from one to the other and said nothing of it.

"Soldier's widow, lower village," Parukutty said. "My husband was Raman. Spearman. I've a girl of six and a boy of nine." Her voice shook and straightened. "Majesty, the Justice paid mine early, and I thank you. But there are three more on the wall rolls, and the lower village will burn when the Thurai come through. Their death-gifts are due at harvest. There won't be a harvest. Could they be paid now, so they can buy bread on the road? And Chandu wants to carry arrows on the wall. He's nine. I'd rather he carried rice."

"Raman," Nirmala said. She said it as one says the name of someone one has met. "Killed in the spring, in single file under the shelf. The patrol of eleven." She did not look at a clerk, because there was none. "Minnu and Chandu. Is the girl's cough better?"

Parukutty stared. "It's mine, Majesty. The girl's well."

"Then you should have a physician's willow bark, and not wait for a harvest." The queen drew a sheet of paper toward her, took the reed Janu had brought, and wrote in a quick slanting hand. She signed it *Nirmala* in full. No stamp, no seal. "Three widows on the wall rolls, paid now, from my own purse. And Chandu does not carry arrows. He carries rice to the arrow-bearers, and eats first." She blew on the ink and held it out. "If anyone tells you otherwise, show them this."

Parukutty took the paper in both hands. She did not kneel. She seemed to have forgotten how.

Neela watched the queen's ink-stained finger tap the table once, then rest. This was real, the whole of it. It was the same hand that had kept a dead spearman's name and his children's names since spring, and had reached for a cough the moment it heard one. It was also the hand that had sealed a writ in green wax and sent Vellan up the west stair, where Raiu had died.

Both are hers, Neela thought. She could not make them into one woman, though she had been trying since Kodiyur. Behind her, Devan's breath stopped for a moment, and started again.

* * *

When the widows were gone the queen turned to the rest of the hall. "Now to the thing I came for."

A guard set a red lacquer case on the table. Neela knew it by its gold bird and its farm lock. The queen lifted the lid. Thirty stiff pale leaves lay on blue cloth, each with a clerk's copy of its intake roll looped on in green thread.

"The crown leaf," Nirmala said. "Fresh pages, in my own hand, written the night before your wife left Kodiyur. Usha's I wrote twice; your wife carries the first. I mean to burn them in front of you all, once you've heard my terms."

Neela reached into her wrap. She laid Chathu's sheaf beside the case, tied with its green thread, forty pages in black ink. "Mine," she said. "Chathu wrote these at your table. They're not burned. I told you why."

"You did." The queen did not seem hurt by it. "Look at them together, if you like. I'd be a poor queen if I minded being read."

Neela bent over the two piles. She did it for the Company and for the garrison, and because it kept her face busy. The queen's leaves gave off a bitter smell, scorched and sharp, like resin gone too long on a flame. Chathu's smelled like a lamp-black cake, dry, the smell of a clerk's ink pot.

She frowned. The two should have smelled alike. They were both pages in the same resin-ink, written by true witnesses. She turned the thought over but could not make it come out. When she straightened, it slid off like water off a leaf.

"Well?" said the queen gently.

"Nothing," said Neela. "I wanted to know them again."

"Then know them." Nirmala's gaze moved over Neela's shoulder. "Warden. I'll be plain, in your own hall, before your own people. The offer I sent still stands. Kettamala is yours. The disc, the pass, the garrison, your father's chair. Muthu may give back what he's held, with my thanks and a captaincy for it."

Every head in the hall turned. Neela kept her eyes on the pages and listened to the quiet behind her. This was the moment where a man could reach past his wife for what was his by blood, and the whole house knew it.

Devan said nothing.

Sandals stopped shifting on the stone. Janu's tray rattled once against her wrist. Devan did not step forward to take the chair or to refuse it. He did not so much as look at the disc on Muthu's belt.

The queen waited. Then she nodded, slowly, as if he had said a great deal. "Think on it," she said. "You'll have your answer when your wife has given hers."

The hall let go. It was a small noise, sandals scuffing, a cough, a spear butt set down hard in the passage. Without turning, Neela moved her bound right hand back along the bench, and Devan's fingers closed over the strip at her wrist, warm and dry. His thumb found the blister under the cloth and stayed there. Neither of them looked down.

"That was a great deal of nothing," she said, low.

"You told me not to speak for you," Devan said, just as low. "I took it as wide advice."

Her mouth twitched. His thumb pressed once on the strip, then he let go, and she faced the room with her hand in her lap and the print of his fingers still on it.

"Janu," she said.

The steward jumped. "Lady?"

"Sit down."

"I'll not sit while the queen—"

"It's your hall," Neela said. "You fed her. You've kept it standing all these years, and you stood against the wall while she wrote a widow's bread on your table." She pulled out the bench beside her. "Sit."

Janu looked at the queen. The queen inclined her head a finger's breadth, a small permission, her mouth dry with watching amusement. Janu put the tray down on the stone with a clatter that rang off the pillars. She sat. Her hands went flat on her knees. Around the hall, a dozen kitchen women found sudden business with their sleeves and their sandals.

Neela kept her face still. She had never in her life been so sure that she had taken something and given it away in the same moment.

* * *

The west stair had forty-four steps. Neela went up them at dusk, because the queen asked it.

"Would you walk with me," Nirmala had said, after the meal, after the spears had been settled on the terraces and Usha had gone to see to the seven in green. "The warden's wife may choose the stair."

Neela had chosen this one. She had gone wide of the parrot newel from her first week, without knowing why. She knew why now. She had stood at its foot with a shuttered lamp, six years ago, and counted. She laid a hand on its broken beak and felt it smooth as a river stone.

They climbed together. Nirmala's breath was short at the tenth step. She stopped on the eleventh, and it rang under her sandal with a hollow note.

"Here," she said.

Neela's hand tightened on the rail. Pala wood, worn.

"Rema's roll took down the physician's account: the postern, the eleventh step. I came up knowing which one to stand on." The queen did not look at her. "She was Devan's sister. I met her once, at the Kolathur fair. She argued with me over the price of pepper-sacks, and was right."

Neela's fingers went white on the rail. Her mouth had gone dry, and for one breath she had the whole sentence ready: *I held the lamp.* The queen had Rema's roll. She would know it already, and it would be no gift to her to hear it. Neela shut her mouth on it. She would say it where it cost the speaker, in the open, and not on a dark stair to the one who had paid for it.

"Ittan paid for Asha," she said instead. Her voice came out very even. "Not you."

"Not I. That was Ittan's coin, and Chathu's knife, and a bear's. I'd have stopped it, had I known. I will not pretend I never would have signed a thing like it." The queen turned from the stair and faced her. In the grey dusk her face was tired and honest. "I sealed the payment writ. Usha saw the bundle in your husband's pack on the hill, and she has told me what such a bundle holds. He carries it up and down my hills. He was loyal, Neela. He was also a week from opening a box in Ittan's keeping, and what was in that box held a price I had paid for twenty years. Ships off this coast. Boats that stayed on the sea. That price is not paid now. It failed this season, when the fleet crossed the bar, and the Thurai are at the cut."

Neela heard it land, and heard what it took with it. Devan meant to read that writ at the gate and watch the crown's own spears hear it for the first time. The queen had just said it herself, in a half-lit stair, as calmly as a price at market. There would be no gasp now. Anyone who heard of this talk could say the queen had owned the thing already.

It would still have to be read. A confession made to one woman on a stair was a secret with a kind face. Said before three hundred spears, it was a fact.

"So you killed a loyal man to keep it paid."

"I tried to. I'd do it again, if the arithmetic stood the same." There was no cruelty in it, and that was worse than cruelty. "A realm is kept safe by what it never has to see. A widow in a village sleeps because she has never watched a knife go in. I've signed death writs for people who loved me and had done nothing but get in the way, and I have not slept well after any. But the villages slept. Until this season, they slept."

"Ammu is dead."

"Ammu. Twenty-five. Her name is in Rema's roll." Nirmala's voice did not change. "I'm sorry."

It was not an excuse. Neela had expected one, and could have fought an excuse. She stood on the eleventh step with a dead woman's name in her mouth and no place to put her hands.

"You'd have kept me unseen," she said. "Thirty knives, no pages. You said it kindly."

"I meant it kindly." The queen almost smiled. "It is the only gift I know how to give. I am sorry, too, that you don't want it. I think you're right not to."

A cistern chain clanked in the dark. Down in the court somebody laughed, and it was Kamalakshi, and the sound of it carried up the stair like a thrown pebble.

"My spies tell me two nights," Nirmala said, her voice shifting into a quieter, harder register. "The main assault, not the scouts. Fourteen hundred, with a new tower and ladders, up through the cut. Two nights from tonight. I'll have my captain give the order and mean it, whatever else stands between us." She drew a slow breath. "I'd have chosen a private room. He's chosen the court. I'll allow it."

"I'll answer at the dusk before the assault," Neela said. "In the open."

"In front of whom?"

"Everyone. Your captains. The garrison. The Company. Whoever else is left in this house." She heard her own voice, flat and slow. "You said the realm is kept safe by what it never sees. I'd like it to see. All of it, including what you've just told me."

The queen looked at her for a long time. Then, unexpectedly, she bowed her head, not as a queen to a subject but as one woman who had run a house to another who meant to.

"Then the realm will hear you choose," Nirmala said, "between what it never saw and what it cannot unsee. I would not envy you, if I were not fond of you." She turned and began to climb. At the landing she paused, one hand on the rail. "I did not lie to you in the fish shed, Neela. I have never needed to. It is the cruelest thing about me."

She went up into the dark passage.

Neela did not wait for her steps to fade. She went down the forty-four steps two at a time, past the newel without slowing, and across the lower court where the Company sat in knots around their cook-fires. The great ram's horn hung from the well post, where Kuttu had slung it after the council. She lifted it down. It was heavier than it looked, and the mouthpiece was cold against her lip.

She blew one long bellow. It rolled off the walls and up the terraces, and every head in the court came round.

They came to her in ones and twos, then all at once: Kuttu with his bowl still in his hand, Thilak wiping his knife, Vellan limping up from the gate arch. Bhaskaran's sentries leaned over the wall. At the edge of the firelight, Devan stopped a step behind her, and said nothing.

"The queen has given us two nights," Neela said. Her voice shook, and she let it. "I'll give her my answer at dusk, the day after tomorrow, at the gate. In the open, with all of you standing where she can see you." She looked from face to face. "Every one of you hears it before she does. Tonight, at the cairn fire, I'll tell you what I mean to say. Then you tell me if I'm wrong."
