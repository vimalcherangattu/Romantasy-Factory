# Chapter 19: Fire on the Shelf

The tower was burning against the shelf wall, and Devan was at the lip with a ladder on fire in his hands.

Neela saw it from the last bend of the cut, over her horse's ears. Black timber, three men high, wearing flame like a coat. The wet hides on its flanks had gone to hissing rags. A Thurai ladder hung from the platform to the wall, its iron hooks bitten into the stone, and it was alight from the rungs to the lashings. A man stood at its foot with his bare hands on the burning wood. He heaved. The ladder swung out, hung, and fell away into the dark with its climbers screaming.

Her palms screamed with him.

"Down," she said. "Walk them. Don't gallop on the scree."

Nobody argued. Twenty-eight horses came down the cut in a line, and the Company's wall pairs turned to stare. Someone shouted *Riders!* in a cracked voice. She did not stop to learn whose. She counted the steps from the stirrup to the stone, fourteen, and Thilak was beside her at nine, holding out his hand.

"The pot," he said. "Noon trees. Even pinches, the way we voted."

He had a clay bowl on a flat rock behind the wall, black and shining. She tipped her banana-leaf fold into it, all six pinches she had carried to Kodiyur and back. Then she took one from the bowl with a fingertip, the same as anyone. It was too fair a thing to argue with. Behind her, Usha cracked the green wax off a flat tin she had carried from the palace store, and the queen's guards crowded round it. Crown resin. It smelled the same. The hands that took it were pared smooth at the wrist.

The sun went off the rim. The cut turned blue.

She ran along the wall with the pinch in her fist. Devan was on his knees by the lip, his back to her, his hands held out from his body like a man carrying hot bowls. The skin of both palms was gone to a raw red shine. He was breathing through his teeth.

"Don't touch anything," she said.

He turned his head. His face was black with smoke, and he looked at her as though she were a sentence in a language he was relearning.

"You're early," he said.

"I'm late."

"You're here." He glanced at her hands, which she was holding exactly as he held his. "It's the same."

"It's the same."

There was no time for more. Over his shoulder a second ladder was swinging up out of the dark, and the tower groaned as its platform leaned. She crouched where she was, in front of him and in front of the wall, and rubbed the resin hard across her throat and wrists. It burned like it always did, like a hot coal pressed to the old ridges. She did not look away from him. He did not look away from her.

He had no pebble in his cheek. He had no time to find one. He only watched her, and that was a better pledge than the stone had ever been.

A minute is a long time. It was long enough for the hook of a ladder to bite the stone beside his knee. Her spine bent. Her hands went. The cut turned from blue to the bright, hard grey of the other one's eyes, and every smell on the shelf stood up and spoke at once: tar, scorched hide, wet iron, sweat, a man's blood.

His blood. Under the smoke, close, a thin line of it.

*Behind you,* she thought, and could not say it, and went past him to the lip.

* * *

The other one did not think about sides.

She thought about edges. The wall had one, the platform had one, and between them was a gap of air a man could cross in a leap. Six Thurai came over it with bronze caps and short swords, half-blind in their own smoke. She met the first before his feet touched stone.

Behind her the shelf was changing.

She felt it more than saw it. The queen's riders were turning along the wall: Usha, Sanu, Sajan, Lata, and the nineteen who had come with them. Gold eyes in the dusk, and low long backs, and the sound a body makes when it comes out of itself. A fox. A heron, struck on one leg and coming down like a dropped knife. A dark creature with a snout and a thick, patient gait. She had never seen them in shape. They had been palace knives for years, kept in cuffs and paste, and they had never fought for a thing they chose.

They fought as if they had been waiting.

No page. No hand on a lead. No voice behind them that could say a word and cage them. They went along that wall in a loose line and nobody told them where. She watched the fox take a man at the hamstring and be gone before the sword turned. She watched the heron strike a hook-rope and cut it with her beak.

*Unbound,* she thought, and was glad past all reasoning.

Then the second wave came over the gap, and she stopped watching.

Her left flank was open. She knew it as she knew a bad stair. A Thurai spear came at her ribs, low, and she twisted from it, and a weight she knew landed at her back.

Devan.

Spear in his scorched hands. He should not have been able to hold it. She smelled the blood from his palms as he gripped the shaft. He stood with his shoulders against her hip, not touching, then touching, then pressing the way a man leans on a wall he trusts.

"Left," he said, over her head. "Four. The big one."

She went left.

He did not call her name, or the other word, or any word that mattered to the dark. He called targets. They had learned that from the sally. She went where he told her, and where he did not, and each time she moved he moved with her so that her back was never open for longer than a breath. The spear came round once and took a man's blade on its haft just as her jaws closed on another's arm.

A sword caught his forearm. She felt it before he did. A bright line of heat in her own foreleg, shallow, a welt and no more. She did not miss her step. He grunted, and the grunt told her it was nothing, and he drove the butt of the spear into the swordsman's face.

Across the shelf a heavy shape had stopped to look. Kandan, with a sword in one hand and a chalk tile stuck in his belt. He was staring. Not at the Thurai. At the two of them.

Beyond him, the fox.

Usha had a man by the wrist and had forgotten to bite. She looked at the pair with her pale muzzle lifted, and Neela understood why. The guards had been told for years that a witness is a leash. That is true. Here was a man who held the leash, the whole leash, and had set his back against hers and asked for nothing. She could not tell Usha what it was. She could only fight, and let her see.

The tower gave way at the middle watch.

It went all at once, with a long cracking groan, as the burned beam under the platform let go. The top of it folded outward and came down across the gap in a rain of sparks. Men fell with it. A roar went up from the shelf, and under it, from the river road, a ragged horn calling the Thurai back.

She stood on the wall with her ribs heaving, and Devan stood at her back, and neither moved for a long time.

* * *

The first grey came up the cut like water into a footprint.

Neela turned back crouched behind the wall with her teeth chattering. Hunger hit her the way it always did, hollow and shaking, as if her bones had been scraped. She felt for the cloth. It was already in front of her, held out behind a man's back, warm.

She wrapped it round her. It had been inside his coat. The warmth was his warmth.

"Is this the third dawn?" she said. Her voice came out cracked.

"The sixth," said Devan, to the wall. "I've kept it warm since you left."

"You couldn't have known I'd be here."

"No." He turned, then, and offered her a leaf packet of cold rice and curd and a cup. The water in the cup steamed. "I brought it anyway. You said cold water hurts your teeth."

She had said that in the dark, a long time ago, on a night when neither of them had meant it to matter. She took the cup in both hands, and the blisters cried out against the clay, and she drank anyway.

They sat with their backs against the wall and watched the tower. It was ash. A tall black stump of beam stood in the middle of a wide grey scar, with smoke feathering off the top. Garrison men were poking through it with spears. The Thurai had left a ladder-hook in the stone, still bright. Someone had chalked a tile, and Kandan was standing over it, not writing.

"How many?" she said.

"Two of ours. Chami's cousin, and a woman from Kalladi I didn't know." He said the names carefully. "Seven hurt. The wall held. That's all I can count to."

"The guards?"

"All twenty-two. Three limping." His mouth moved. "Lata got a man off the wall with her bare hands, in shape. I saw her. She'll tell you she didn't."

She almost laughed. She held out her hands instead.

Her palms were white and puffy, the skin lifted over the heel of each hand. His lay beside them, on his knees, and they matched down to the line across the base of the thumb. He saw her looking. He turned his over, and she turned hers, and for a moment neither of them could tell which of them had been burned.

"Yours were first," she said.

"Yours came while I was holding it."

"Both, then."

"Both." He found a pot in his coat, Pavi's green salve, smelling of crushed leaf and smoke, and began, with his teeth, to unstop it. She took it out of his hand and did it herself. She laid the salve on his palms with the flat of her fingers, light, and felt the answering coolness in her own, a half-breath behind. He shut his eyes.

"The noon signal," she said. "I know you waited."

His eyes opened. "I'd have waited longer."

"I couldn't. The queen was at my elbow." She kept her voice flat, because it was the only way to say it. "Noon, the day after the gun. She'd sent for me to the sea room, and she stood next to me the whole hour. She's gentle. She doesn't need to be anything else. I had three thorns in my sash and I couldn't put my hand to them in front of her. If I'd flinched, she'd have wanted to know what I was reaching for."

"You did right."

"I know. I hated it." She wiped the salve from her fingers on the cloth. "You'd have stood on the shelf with a sheaf of arrows and a thumb that did nothing. I knew that, and I stayed still."

"It did nothing," Devan said, "and I counted the arrows three times." His mouth twitched. "Muthu says I'm the worst liar he's ever known when I'm worried."

"You don't lie."

"I do arithmetic."

It was the closest to a joke he'd made in days, and it was bad, and she took it like bread. She leaned her shoulder against his. He did not move away. The sun was a thin line on the east lip, and the wall beside them was warm already where the stone had held the night's fire.

"Neela," he said. "I have to say something before the others come. I should have said it first, and I've been working out how."

"Is it about the queen's offer?"

"She sent me a letter. Three hundred spears, and Kettamala. The same she's put to you." He swallowed. "I'll tell you all of it. But I need you to hear this part first. I answered it. I answered it here, on the cart, before you could—"

A horn went off behind them.

It was not Bhaskaran's code. It was the great ram's horn the Company kept at the cairn fire, one long bellow that rolled down the cut and came back off the cliff. Every head on the shelf came up.

It blew again. Then Thilak was running along the wall, a hooked tapping knife in one hand and his grey wrap flying, and he shouted before he reached them.

"Council! Everyone to the fire!"

"Why now?" Devan said. He was already on his feet.

"The vote. The queen's offer." Thilak stopped short, hauling air. His eyes went from one blistered pair of hands to the other, and he looked away. "Usha has word. A column on the road behind her riders. Crown banners. A day and a half back, no more. If we're going to say yes or no, we'll say it before she's at the gate."

Neela got up slowly. Her legs shook with the hunger, and her palms had not stopped burning, and the rest of the sentence was still in Devan's mouth.

*I answered it before I.*

Before he what?

He looked at her. Whatever it was, it was written plainly across his scorched face, and he did not say it. The horn blew a third time, and she took the hand he held out to her and went to the fire, with half a sentence in her ear and forty-two voices ahead of her.
