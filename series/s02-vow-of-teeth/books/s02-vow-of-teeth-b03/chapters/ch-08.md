# Chapter 8: The Shelf

"Fourteen hundred," Devan said to the rock.

The rock did not argue. It sat in the cut above the lower narrows, a slab the size of a cart bed, and Thilak's pry-bar was under it. Devan put his shoulder to the bar beside the tapper's. The slab groaned, tipped, and went down the scree in a long grey roar. Dust rose from the shelf and hung there.

Raman had died under that shelf. Eleven riders in single file, a bow on the lip, and a man with a nine-year-old boy at home. Devan had ridden past it every day for a week and had not looked up. Today he looked up all the time.

"Fourteen hundred what?" Kandan said.

The corporal stood below with a rope round his waist and a spear across his back. The scar through his brow was white with dust. Forty garrison volunteers worked the face behind him, cutting and prying and hauling. Muthu's order had said the garrison stays inside the walls. Kandan's forty had not found that order interesting.

"Thurai." Devan wiped his palms on his thighs. "Sixty hulls on the water. Twenty-odd to a hull."

"And us?"

"Forty-two odiyans. Six ride south at the next light. Thirty-six who stay." He kept his voice flat, as he would for a ration count. "Thirty-six and forty. Seventy-six."

"Eighteen apiece," said Kandan.

"Nineteen. Near enough."

"You count like a man who has already lost."

"I count like a man who wants the wall." Devan tipped his head at the shelf. "Look at it."

The lower narrows ran forty paces from cliff to cliff. On the east side the shelf jutted out over the cart road, a stone lip with loose scree piled behind it. Raiders could not climb it from below. But if the face came down, the scree came with it, and the road would be choked with a bank of broken rock chest-high across the whole gorge. Spears on top of that bank, and stones thrown from the lip above, and nineteen Thurai for each of them trying to climb.

Not a wall. A bad day made of rocks. It was the only one he had.

"You want to bury the road," Kandan said.

"I want to make them climb it."

"Under spears."

"Yes."

Kandan chewed that over. He did not ask what happened when the number of Thurai went past nineteen apiece. He was an honest man, and Devan was grateful for it.

Thilak set the bar again. He was a broad, quiet man, who had tapped bark for twenty-two years before anyone gave his hands another use. When he leaned, the rock moved. Vellan worked on the far side of him, hauling slabs from the cut in his arms like a man carrying sleeping children. He did not talk. He had not said Asha's last words since the pass, and Devan had not asked. They had the work between them, and that did for now.

Down the road, on the verge where the gorge opened toward the sea, Neela's six stood by a pack pony. She had come as far as the shelf with them at first light and then stopped, with her head tilted back, looking up at the lip. *Show me what you mean to do,* she'd said. He had shown her. Then the sun had climbed, and no one had left. It was the kind of delay that happens when two people are each waiting for the other to say go.

A pebble ticked.

Devan heard it as a soldier hears a cough in a quiet hall. Scree falls down. This one had rolled sideways, then dropped, from the high side of the cut where nobody was working.

He looked up.

* * *

A man stood on the lip.

He was thirty paces off and a body-length above, a lean shape in hide and bronze with a short bow already drawn. The arrow pointed down at the work gang. Beyond him, on the slope, two more were coming down the scree at a run. They went light-footed, stepping on the stones that did not slide.

"Down!" Devan shouted. "Scouts, the lip, left of the cut!"

Men scattered. A volunteer named Vasu was too slow, bent over a rope with his back to the slope, and the nearest runner came for him with a sword held low. Devan's spear lay against the pry-pole, where he had set it that morning. He took it and went up the scree.

It was twelve steps. He had counted them that morning, because he counted everything now.

He had not struck first at a living man in eight years. He had trained the habit out of his hands. Let them come. Hold the line. Stand and be the wall. And a man had died under this shelf while he stood and was the wall, and the wall had not been enough.

The runner raised his blade. It was a clean, practised motion. He had not yet turned toward Vasu's back. He was still a body in the air, between one stride and the next.

Devan drove the spear into him under the ribs.

It went in the way it was taught to, and stuck, and he let go of it. The man looked down at the shaft. Then he sat down on the scree, quite gently. He was dead before the stones he'd dislodged finished rolling.

Something bright bounced from his belt and spun on the rock. A small coin. It flashed in the sun and lay flat.

The arrow from the lip sang down past Devan's ear and cracked on stone. Then Vellan went up the slope on all fours with a roar that had nothing human in it, though he had not turned, and Thilak was a half step behind him. The bowman dropped his bow. He tried to go back over the lip. He did not make it.

It was short. When it was over, four Thurai lay on the scree, and two had run up the slope toward the cut, and Kandan's men were already after them.

Devan stood over the man he had killed. He did not feel the weight he'd expected. He felt a coldness at his left arm and looked down. A strip of skin was laid open from shoulder to elbow, thin and shallow, where the bowman's second shot must have grazed him. He had not noticed it. It began to bleed freely, and he pressed his hand over it.

*Neela.*

"You struck first," Kandan said.

The corporal had come up beside him, breathing hard, his spear dripping. There was no accusation in his face. He looked like a man who had been waiting a long time to see something and had now seen it.

"Yes," said Devan.

"Eight years, the garrison says. You never raised a hand first."

"I raised it first today."

"Aye." Kandan looked at the dead man. Then at Vasu, who was sitting on a rock with his head between his knees, alive. "Better you than him."

He went to see about his runners. Devan found that his hand had begun to shake against the cut, and he pressed harder until it stopped.

* * *

He was kneeling beside the dead man when Neela reached him.

She had run the whole length of the narrows. Her left hand was clamped to her arm in the same place as his, and her hair had come out of its knot. Pavi was two paces behind her, with her bad paw of a hand held out like she meant to catch something.

"It's nothing," Devan said.

"I felt it open." She dropped beside him and peeled his fingers off the cut. Her own forearm had a welt on it, red and weeping, in the same place. She looked from one to the other, and her mouth went tight and level. "Shallow. Good." Then, quieter: "You killed one."

"Yes."

"Openly."

"In front of forty men."

She said nothing to that. She took the strip of cloth from her sash and bound his arm and her own, one after the other, with quick sure turns.

"Look at his wrist," Devan said.

He had pushed back the dead man's cuff before she came. He had meant to look for a tally, or a brand, or a mark of rank. What he found made him sit very still.

Pale, ridged lines ran across the inside of the wrist. They were old and thick, laid over one another. Every odiyan in the Company wore the same pattern.

"Thilak," he said.

The tapper came over, wiping his hands. He crouched. He turned the arm over and put his nose close to the skin, as Devan had done, and breathed in. His face did something complicated.

"Salt," Thilak said. "Clove-bark. Dry and sharp." He straightened. "Not nightbark. There's no tamarind in it, no scorch. I've smelled nightbark on my own hands for twenty-two years." He frowned at the dead wrist. "I don't know this tree. I'd say it's been put on this man more than once, though. And a long time."

Devan looked at Neela. She was staring at the wrist.

"The one by the tower," she said. "Same smell. Same scars."

"Same *kind*," he said. "We don't know what he is."

"A Thurai who burns himself."

"Or a man who was made to." He did not know what a Thurai odiyan would mean. It might mean nothing. It might mean that every fear he had tried to count was too small. He put it down in his mind next to the grey cloak and the yellow bird on a tailboard, and he did not build on any of them. "We'll hold it. We won't guess."

Thilak went to tell Kandan. Vellan had been standing a little way off, saying nothing, with his back to them. He turned his head once, looked at the scarred wrist, and turned away again. His shoulders had gone strange.

* * *

The purse on the dead man's belt held eleven coins.

Devan poured them out on the flat of a stone. They lay in a small bright heap, with the sprig on one face and a sharp milled edge round every rim. They had not been handled. He had seen coins like them in a satchel once, under the lip of this same cut, and had taken them for evidence.

"New silver," Neela said.

"Varanad's."

"Look at the sprig." She had gone very quiet. She reached into her sash and drew out a cord with a single coin knotted on it. It was bright, too. "Parukutty's. Rema paid it to her. I traded her a worn panam for it, and I told you I was being sentimental."

"You said you wanted to weigh it."

"I weighed it. It weighed the same as the scouts' coin, to a grain." She laid it beside the heap. "Turn them over."

He turned over three. The lowest leaf of the pepper sprig was doubled on each, a small stamped stutter, as if the die had slipped when it struck. He turned over the fourth, and the fifth. Then he picked up hers and held it to the light.

The same doubled leaf.

"One die," he said.

"One die." Her voice was careful. "The scouts had it in spring. The man by the tower had it now, and the widow's gift. Rema's coin, from the crown's own chest." She breathed out. "The crown paid them."

"Once."

"What?"

"The crown paid them once," Devan said. "The coin tells us where it was struck. It doesn't tell us who put it in his hand." He set her coin down beside the others. "Rema's chest was filled from the same mint as the scouts' purse, and that's all we have. The mint is the crown's. Everything else is a guess." He looked up at her. "Who pays them *now*?"

She held his gaze. He saw her turn it over, the way she turned over a door before she crossed it.

"Someone who has the die," she said at last, "and the tally book and the cart with a yellow bird. And a man who wore a grey cloak to Muthu's door the winter Asha died."

"And grey is cheap."

"And grey is cheap." She let it go. It was not quite a smile that crossed her face, but it was kin to one. "You'd make a good clerk."

"Rema says I'd make a bad one."

He gathered the coins back into the purse and tied it. He took a long breath. Then he said what he'd carried for days, since the night at the Resin House, when he'd lain awake and listened to the wounds on his body rise up on hers.

"Neela."

"Yes."

"I killed a man today. First. I took a cut for it, and you took the same cut." He kept his eyes on her face. It was harder than the spear had been. "It's the third wound this week that I opened on you. I put the Company on this shelf, and the Company is on the shelf because I decided, and every fight I choose is chosen for your body as well. You never agreed to it. I let it sit because I didn't want to hear no."

She was very still.

"So I'm asking. Straight." His voice stayed even. He didn't know how. "Do you consent? To what my war puts on you. The cuts, and worse than cuts. Because I'll hold this pass whether you say yes or no. I'm not asking so I can feel clean. I'm asking because you have the right to say it to my face, and I've been careful not to give you the chance."

A wind came down the gorge, and it carried dust and the smell of crushed lantana, rank and green. Pavi had retreated twenty paces and was studying a pony's hoof with enormous attention.

Neela looked at the coins, and at the dead man's wrist, and at his bound arm and hers. She drew in a breath.

"Devan." Her voice was low and sure. "I chose that the day I—"

A horn sounded.

It came down the gorge from far above, from the gate of Kettamala, a mile and more up the cut. It rolled along the cliffs, and the langurs on the east face went silent all at once. Two long notes. A short. A long.

Not one long and two short. Not unarmed strangers.

Bhaskaran's code for the queen's riders.

Neela's mouth was still open on the end of her word. Devan was already on his feet, with the purse in his fist and the cut on his arm bleeding through the cloth. He was thinking, absurdly, of Muthu. He was thinking that Muthu held that gate.
