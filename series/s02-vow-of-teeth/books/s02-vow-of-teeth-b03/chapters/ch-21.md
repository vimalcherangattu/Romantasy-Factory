# Chapter 21: The Third Slat

Muthu was waiting at the foot of the north stair with a lamp in one hand and his other arm full of bedding.

"I've been told I snore," he said. "So I'm sleeping in the gate chamber tonight. Which is a kindness to everybody."

Neela looked at the bedding, then at him. He was loud when he lied and quiet when it counted, and he was neither now. He was just a big man holding a pillow like a shield.

"The warden's chamber," she said.

"It was always his. I only kept the bed warm." He held out the lamp. Sesame oil, full to the lip. "One night. Janu's changed the sheets and sworn at me while she did it. There's rice and curd on the sill and water in the kindi, spout toward the pillow." He stopped. "I didn't do that last part. She says that's how it's done."

Devan had come up behind her. She felt him there, a step back, as she'd asked.

"Muthu," he said.

"Don't." Muthu raised the pillow an inch. "The slat still creaks. I didn't fix it either."

He went down the stair, whistling something that wandered off the tune, and did not look back.

* * *

The room had not changed. The teak shutters faced west, closed and latched. The long bar hung on its hooks. The bed stood where it had always stood, and on the floor beside it lay a sword in its scabbard, hilt toward her side, because Muthu had been told once and had never asked why.

She counted the doors. Two. The little one by the hearth made three.

The vote had run until the lamps burned low. She had spoken, and so had he, from a step behind her. They had settled on one thing: nobody would answer the queen until the queen had spoken in the open, at the gate, with the Company standing where she could see them. It was a delay. It was also a door held ajar. She had gone from the fire with her hands wrapped and her head ringing, and had not smeared. Dusk had come on with the old pull at her throat, ten counts of burn in the scars, and gone. She was human. The ache would count the night against her. She let it.

Devan set the lamp on the table. Then he went to the pack he had hung on the peg and took out an oilcloth bundle tied with red thread.

"I carried it up a hill and down again," he said. "It can sleep one night where we hid it."

He knelt at the third shutter and pressed the housing of the slat. A piece of the frame swung in on a hidden pin, no wider than a hand. He laid the bundle in the hollow and closed it, and the slat gave its small dry creak under his thumb.

She had pressed that slat on the wedding night to see if it would wake him. It had. He had told her so, much later, and she had not laughed.

"Sit," she said. "Please. I'd like to say a thing before I lose it."

He sat on the edge of the bed. She stayed standing, because the telling wanted her on her feet.

"You asked me at the fire what I thought of the queen's offer. I gave the Company the reasons. I didn't give them the rest." Her bandaged hands were clasped before her. "I wanted it."

He said nothing. He watched her, and he waited, and it was the waiting that let her go on.

"In the sea room. She put her hand on the shutter and said *leave it, I like to hear the water.* She was kind about it. And she offered me thirty knives with no pages on them and a roof with no number on it, and I stood there and I wanted it the way I want water after a turning." She heard her voice go flat and let it. "Command, Devan. Nobody over me. And nobody looking. That's the part I'm ashamed of. I could have been unseen again. No one saying my name in a court. No one with a power over me who'd chosen not to use it, only a quiet house where no one could. Twenty-one years I answered to a number. It was safe. I missed it for an hour, and I hated that I missed it, and I came home and I put my hand to the thorns and I still missed it."

"Is it gone?"

"No." She made herself look at him. "It's smaller. It's not gone."

He did not tell her it was nothing. He reached out and turned one of her bound hands over in his, palm up, and set his thumb on the strip of hem where the blister was. Not pressing. Just there. She felt the warmth of that, and the answering warmth in his own palm, a half-breath apart.

"Thank you for telling me," he said. "I won't make it smaller than it is."

It was what she had wanted. She had not known she wanted it until it was said.

"Now you," she said. "I know the answer you gave the queen. I don't know the one you gave yourself."

His jaw worked. He looked at the lamp.

"Muthu asked me in the yard if it hurt," he said. "To refuse her. I said no. That was a lie, and he knew, and he let me have it." He drew breath. "It hurt more than I let him see. Her letter said *Kettamala restored,* and I stood in the gate chamber and smelled the pepper on the terraces and I wanted it back. All of it. My father's chair. Asha's door. The shutters open for the air, the way he kept them. I wrote my refusal at the same table, and my hand didn't shake, and then I went to the kalari and hit a post for an hour."

"You didn't tell me."

"I told you I wouldn't dress it up as sparing you. I left out that it cost me." His thumb moved on her palm. "I wanted the fort. I still do. I would trade it again for what I got. Both are true."

She sat down beside him then, close enough that the bed gave under them. "That one's easier to carry than the other."

"Which other?"

"The one where you're only noble."

He laughed. It came out short and cracked, and it startled him. She leaned into his shoulder and felt it in her own ribs.

"I'll stand at your back at the gate," he said. "I won't answer for you. If you tell the queen no, I'll stand there and hate every word and be glad of it. If you tell her yes, the same. I'm saying that now so you have it."

"I won't say yes."

"Then say what you will."

She had been working it out for days, in pieces. It came whole now, as the lamp steadied. "I won't take the command. That's settled. But I won't refuse it in a corner, either, the way I'd refuse a tin of sweets. She hears my reasons in the open, with the Company standing there, with you behind me and not in front. If she's wrong, she can be wrong where people hear."

"And if she's right?"

"Then I'll say that too."

He nodded, slowly. "Yours to speak," he said. "Mine to stand behind."

"Yes."

It was a smaller thing than a vow, and it settled in her like one.

* * *

She did not remember who moved first. She remembered undoing the strips of hem from her own hands, one wrap at a time, and his hands taking over when her fingers fumbled. The raw skin of her palms shone in the lamp. His matched it. He turned them side by side and looked at the pair for a long moment.

"I'm twenty-seven," she said, because it was a thing she liked to say plainly, and because it felt like part of the asking. "You're thirty-two. We're both grown, and nobody here is paying for either of us."

"No one." His voice had gone low. "Neela, if you want to stop, you say. Any time. No reason."

"I know." She laid her cheek against his scarred knuckles. "Leave the lamp."

He went still.

"Keep it lit," she said. "I don't want the dark tonight. I want you to look at me. All of it. The whole time." She swallowed. "I've been seen in a hundred rooms by people who owned the seeing. I want to choose this one."

He took a breath that shook. "I'll look."

"Then do."

His hands came to her face first, careful of the blisters, and he kissed her like a man learning a path. She pulled at his shirt. He lifted his arms so she could drag it over his head, and the white seam along his ribs showed under the lamp, and the new welts across his back from the shelf, red and angry. She put her mouth to the seam. He made a sound she felt in her own chest.

When he bared her throat, he stopped. The scars lay there, pale ridges under the lamp's light. He looked at them the way he looked at the pass from the wall, and then he put his lips to each in turn. She had once let a man see them as a lie. She let him see them now as a fact, and her breath caught.

"Look at me," she said again.

"I am."

He was. His eyes did not leave her face as he laid her back on the bed that had held the worst night of her life and the best. His hand went under her mundu, slow, a question, and she answered it with her whole body. She reached for him, blistered palms and all, and the burn of her own hands bloomed across his skin at the same moment, and he gasped. Then warmth came through it. The wound-share carried that too, she had learned, whatever it was given: a heat in her that she felt twice, once in herself and once in his shoulders, bright and shared.

"You feel that," he said, roughly.

"Both ways."

"Both ways."

It slowed after that. He moved above her, and the lamp showed her his face, the grey in his hair, the line between his brows, which eased and came back and eased. She watched him watch her. She did not close her eyes. When the pleasure came up in her it came up in her face, open, helpless, undefended, and he saw it. She felt him see it. She felt the shock of being known pass through her like the turning, but without the burn.

She said his name. He said hers. Not the number. Not the cover. Her own, the one she had chosen, with the other one folded behind it and still unspoken.

The bed creaked under them. The slat did not. It was the one thing in the room that stayed quiet.

* * *

Afterwards she lay with her head on his arm and the lamp burning down. Her palms ached, pleasantly now. His breathing slowed, and she counted it, as she had counted it for forty nights without telling anyone why.

One, two. The hitch came a third of the way through the long count, soft and unmistakable. She counted it again to be sure, and it came again.

"You're counting," he said, into her hair.

"You're awake."

"I'm lying very still so you can." His arm tightened a little. "Is it right?"

"It's always right."

On the sill by the shutters sat a grey river pebble in the dust, exactly where he had left it a lifetime ago. Muthu had not moved it. Janu had cleaned the sill around it. She looked at it for a long time, in the thin, steady light. It had stood for a lie once, and then for a silence, and then for a habit. Tonight it was only a stone.

The creak of the third slat had been a warning, once. *I've never fixed it.* She had heard it as a threat. She had been wrong about a great many things in this room.

She slept.

* * *

She woke before the first clank of the chain. The lamp had burned out. Grey light lay under the shutters. Devan was asleep, on his back, one hand open on the sheet between them, and she lay and watched him for a while without counting anything.

Then she got up, wrapped a cloth around her, and went to the third shutter on bare feet.

The housing gave under her thumb with its dry creak. She winced, and he did not stir. The hollow opened.

Inside, the oilcloth bundle lay where he had put it. She untied the red thread with fingers that no longer shook. The hide strip of the contract chit, with the warden's chamber and *death by beast* in a hand she knew. The payment writ, folded small, dated seven weeks before her wedding. And the gift seal, cut from the sandalwood box, green wax with a sunbird and a small chip at the left wing.

She laid the three side by side on the sill, next to the pebble. In the grey they looked small. A hide strip. A folded paper. A piece of wax. They had killed Raiu, and Ammu, and Asha before them. She did not think the queen knew what they weighed. She thought she knew exactly.

"It's all there," Devan said behind her.

She had not heard him wake. He was propped on one elbow, hair on end, his eyes already clear.

"You can't leave a bundle in a wall and expect me not to count it."

"I'd have been disappointed." He sat up. "She comes at noon."

"With three hundred spears."

"And the eight who refused, probably. And Usha's sister knives, whichever ones she's kept." He rubbed his face. "She'll offer you the command at the gate, before the whole fort. She'll offer me my office. She'll be kind about it. She has never once been anything else."

"I know." Neela touched the writ's green seal, lightly, with one finger. "What will you do with these?"

He was quiet long enough that the chain clanked the hour below them.

"I'll read the writ aloud before her," he said. "In daylight, at the gate, with the Company and the garrison and the village standing there. The whole of it, her wording, her wax. Not hidden. Not whispered in a stair." He swung his legs over the bed's edge. "I won't spring it on her. I'll tell her first, to her face, that I'm going to."

"Why?"

"Because the last person to keep a thing in her sleeve against me was Chathu." He met her eyes. "I said I'd say it before I do it. I mean to keep it."

She wanted to ask what came after the reading. She wanted to ask whether he would use it as a rope or only as a light. She held the question in her mouth for a moment and found she did not need the answer yet. She had her own words to find.

She folded the bundle back together. Her hands knew the knot.

"Then I'll have mine ready," she said. "Hold the pebble."

"Why?"

"Because I want to see you hold it," she said, "and not put it in your mouth."

He took the stone from the sill. It was cold, and it sat in his scarred palm like something small and honest, and he closed his hand around it and did not lift it. Down in the court the cistern chain clanked again, and the first cocks answered.
