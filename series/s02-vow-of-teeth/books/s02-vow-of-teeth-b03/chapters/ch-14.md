# Chapter 14: The First Village

Vasu came up the cut at a run and did not lower his voice.

"Torches," he said. "On the lower road. A mile below the mill ford, coming up."

Devan was already on his feet. He had not been asleep. He had been sitting against the shelf wall in the black before grey, with yesterday's ladder-hook cut pulling across his back every time he breathed, watching the east for a colour it did not yet have.

"How many?"

"I stopped counting at sixty." Vasu bent over his knees. His mother kept ducks at Kalladi, and Kalladi had stopped answering. "Warden, the village. I got to the well. Thirty of them won't leave. Old Kesu sat on the curb and said he'd held that well sixty years and wouldn't be told by a boy with a spear. The rest won't go without him." He wiped his mouth. "And the fishwife. Ponni. She sat down in the dust beside him. Said if he stayed, she stayed. She'd done her running."

"Did you tell them what the herald said?"

"I told them. Kesu said a herald was a man who had never held a well."

Devan looked down the dark of the cut. The herald had named that village first to burn at dawn. Torches a mile below the ford meant the Thurai would reach the thatch at first grey. The goat path was half a day up and a hard two hours down in the dark, for a man who did not mind his knees.

He had thirty-six odiyans. He did not have one to spare.

"Wake the Company," he said.

* * *

They came to the dead fire in ones and twos, hair flat on one side, blankets round their shoulders. Kandan's volunteers stood off to the edge. They were not part of the vote and knew it. Nobody asked them to be.

Devan said it plainly, because he had no other way to say anything.

"Two choices. We hold the shelf as we are, and the people in that village burn with their well. Or fifteen go down in the dark, in shape, and break the Thurai horse lines at the ford while the rest of us go round by the goat path and bring them out."

Nobody spoke. Somewhere a pony shifted.

"I want to go," he said. "I'll say that first, so you can weigh it. Now the cost."

He counted it on his fingers because he trusted his fingers more than his voice.

"One. Fifteen pinches of resin. We've no spare in this week. Tomorrow night's wall is fifteen shapes thinner for it." He folded a second finger. "Two. Those fifteen are the best of you. They turn back at first light below the village, human and bare, with a half-day climb ahead of them and the morning hunger on top. They'll be no use on a wall until dusk." A third. "Three. There are seventy horses in those lines and a hundred men by the torches. I'd put it at one chance in three that we all climb back whole. Someone pays in blood, and it may not be a life."

"Lower," said Vellan.

He sat by the embers with a half-carved bird in his hands. He did not look up.

"Lower, then," Devan said.

"I'd go anyway," said Vellan. "I'm good at lanes."

A dry sound went round the Company that was nearly a laugh.

"Not my order," Devan said. "Yours. Hands. Sally, or hold."

Kandan counted, since Kandan could not be accused of favouring anyone. It took a long time. Hands came up slowly and some came up and went down again. Thulasi's went up high and stayed there, and she did not look at anyone. Kamalakshi kept hers in her lap.

"Twenty," said Kandan. "Sixteen."

Four voices.

Kamalakshi looked at Devan across the embers. "I voted hold," she said. "I'll take the wall. If any of you come back, you can thank me for the dull part."

"I will," Devan said, and meant it.

He turned to Kandan's men. They had gone quiet in the way soldiers did when they knew they would be asked something.

"Corporal, you and your ten come with me by the path. We go in behind the shapes and take the people out. This is my order, under the Company's vote, and I'll answer for it." He said it to all of them. "If it goes wrong, the name that goes first on your tile is mine."

Kandan's scarred brow moved. "It goes on the tile either way, warden. Question is whose goes under it."

"Then keep the tile dry."

Fifteen odiyans went down the slope to a flat of rock out of the wind. Devan turned his back on them. He had a reason and a better one: what they were was theirs to show, and he had not been asked. Behind him he heard the cork, the small wet sound of resin on skin, a sharp hiss through teeth. Then the bone noises, which he did not look at.

When he turned round the rock was empty. Cold air smelled of musk and of scorched tamarind.

* * *

Something went past him in the dark on the goat path, low and quick and warm as a hearth. Then another. He felt them as weather on his face. Kandan's men pressed against the cliff and said nothing, and the shapes went down and were gone.

He did not see the horse lines. He heard them.

The first scream came from the ford, high and wrong, a horse that had met a thing it had no word for. Then the whole line took it up. Picket ropes cracked. A torch swung and fell and a second bloomed beside it, and in that jumping light he saw seventy horses come up the lane in one dark wave with their eyes rolled white. Behind them something huge and slow put a shoulder to a loaded wagon. It went over like a dropped plate. A raider's shout turned into something else.

Vellan's bear went through the picket like a man through a bead curtain. Where it passed there was a lane.

"Now," Devan said, and ran.

Spears slapped behind him. The lane stank of dung and crushed barley and fear. A raider rose in front of him with his sword half out and Kandan's spear took him under the arm. Devan did not stop. He struck once, a haft to a knee, because a knee on the ground was a man who stayed down. He kept his blade for when it was needed. It was not yet.

The village lay above the ford on a hump of red earth, a dozen roofs of thatch, a cattle shed, a well with a stone curb. Raiders already stood among the lower huts with torches. Two thatches were burning.

Grey had come into the east, thin as a knife. Behind him in the thorn the shapes had gone quiet, drawing off to wait out the light. He could not feel them go. He knew they had by the way the noise changed. In a little while there would be fifteen people down there in the brush, naked and shaking, and nothing he could do about it yet.

Kesu was sitting on the well curb with a billhook across his knees.

He was a small man with one eye gone to a seam. He looked at Devan with the other and said, "Late."

"Come down off that."

"I hold this well."

"It's held." Devan crouched in front of him, because shouting would not do it. "It'll hold if you're dead. It'll hold better if you aren't. You can't draw from it from the pyre."

The old man's mouth worked. Behind him, in a ring of women and grandfathers and a boy with a bad foot, the rest of the village watched the thatch burn. Ponni sat on the ground with a blanket over her head. She pushed it back.

"Told him," she said. "I told him the warden had a wife who ties knots, and she'd skin him for sitting on a well against an army." Her eyes went over Devan's face, hunting for someone else's. "Where is she?"

"Four days off."

"Hm." She got a hand on his sleeve and hauled herself up. "Then you'll do."

Kandan picked Kesu up bodily, billhook and all, and slung him over a shoulder like a bag of grain. The old man called him four kinds of fool. He did not struggle. Devan thought he had known all along that he would be carried.

The women pulled blankets off their own beds and left them in a heap at the thorn's edge for whoever came out of it. Then they went up the path in a long ragged line, Kesu and Ponni and thirty more, and eleven soldiers, the boy riding on a volunteer's back. Devan took the end. Halfway up the first bend he looked back.

The thatch went all at once. A roof caught and the fire ran along the ridge-poles and the whole village stood up in a column. Raiders were running between the huts with torches in both hands, not even needing to hurry. Beside him Kesu said nothing at all. Ponni took the old man's hand and kept it.

The well curb stood in the middle of it, grey stone, untouched.

* * *

They climbed all morning. The sun came up into a hard white sky and the path turned to oven. Women carried children and old men carried each other. Devan walked the rear, and every time he looked behind he counted, because counting was what he did when he was afraid. Thirty-two. Thirty-two.

Near noon, in the lower narrows, the Thurai horse found them.

Not many. A dozen riders, the ones who had not bolted, with a grudge. They came up the cart road under the shelf where Raman had died, and the first Devan knew of them was the sound a rope makes in the air.

He turned. That was the mistake: he turned toward the sound and offered his side.

The hook was a boarding hook, iron, three-pronged, the kind a ship's crew throws at a rail. It took him across the ribs under the right arm and went on dragging. He felt cloth part, then skin, then something deeper that he decided not to name. The rope snapped taut and he went over on his face in the dust.

Everything went white and ordinary. The sun was straight overhead. His shadow lay under him no bigger than a hand.

He tried to push up and his arm would not hold.

Boots. Then a spear came over him, very close, and a man planted himself astride him with his feet wide. Kandan. The Corporal who had called Neela a witch at a barracks door. He did not look down. He said, to the riders, in a voice with nothing in it, "Come on, then."

Devan lay with his cheek on the path and let him.

It was not a small thing. For six years he had held every back he stood behind and none had held his. He could have said *I've got it*. He had said it to Muthu a thousand times in a hundred ways. He lay under another man's spear and did not say it, and the not saying was louder than the hook.

The riders had seen enough spears for one morning. They went down the road without any hurry.

"Up, warden," said Kandan, still facing the empty road.

"One breath."

"Take two. I'm not going anywhere."

* * *

The shelf at last. The wall, the ladders stacked and broken, Kamalakshi on the lip with her sleeves rolled, giving him the long look of a woman who has been right and is too polite to say it. She did not say it. She said something better.

"Fifteen went down," she said. "Fifteen came up the cut at mid-morning, in what blankets they'd found. Human, bare, shaking so hard they could hardly hold the rock, climbing the back of the cut one behind the other. I counted every one in. They're in the cistern shed eating everything Janu's girls left us." She jerked her chin. "Thulasi took a hoof on the foreleg. It's cracked, not broken through. She walked, but she can't stand a spear human, not this week. And Vellan won't leave her side."

Devan did the sum before she could. Fifteen hollow and flat on their mats until dusk. One more with a leg, and one more to sit by it.

"That's sixteen hands off tomorrow's wall," Kamalakshi said. She did not say *I told you*. She did not need to.

Everyone alive, and the shelf thinner for it. He had said *whole*, and this was the part of the word he had meant. He found he had to look at the horizon for a moment.

"Thank you for the dull part," he said.

"Sit down before you fall down."

He sent the villagers to the cistern shed beside the fifteen and ate nothing. Then he sat on a fallen ladder rung with his shirt off and Kandan's curved needle in his teeth, a hank of waxed silk in his lap, and sewed himself shut.

It was not clean work. The hook had gone in at the side and out toward the back and left a ragged lip of skin that he had to pull with two fingers. He kept his eyes on the horizon so as not to look at it. Kandan stood a polite pace away holding a lamp he did not need, in broad daylight, because no one had told him what to hold.

"It'll scar," Kandan said.

"It'll join the others."

Third stitch. Fourth. He counted them. He counted everything.

He counted the hour, too. It was well past noon by the sun, and he had not pricked his thumb. Hers had come early, three small stings on the climb, a half hour short of noon, and he had not known what early meant.

Three small stings, every noon, from her or from him. It was the whole of their bargain across four days of road. If one of them could not send, the other waited, and if the waiting went long, the waiting itself told you nothing good. He had missed it. He had been on his face in the dust at the exact hour.

And Kodiyur had a gun.

He knew nothing of its stairs. He knew only that, the night before she left, she had told him she would go where the city was loudest, because in a loud place no one hears a quiet thing. In Kodiyur the loud thing was the noon gun.

*If she was on a stair at noon.* He pulled the fifth stitch through. *If it came while she was on a stair.*

The wound-share carried hurt, not words. It would not say *I'm hit across the ribs, it isn't deep, I'm well*. It would raise a welt along her ribs, hot and weeping, in exactly the place and at exactly the hour, and the pain would feel to her as if something had torn her open. She was a woman in a palace who had to stay silent. He had no way to reach her. He could not even say sorry.

Eight stitches. Nine. He bit the silk and knotted it.

His hands had begun to shake, so he did the last thing with care. He took the leopard tooth from the cord at his chest. It was yellow, old, curved like a hook of its own. He set the point against the pad of his left thumb and pressed.

One.

Two.

Three.

Blood came up in three small beads. He watched them and thought of a girl's thumb somewhere with three welts rising on it, late, a whole hour late. *Alive.* It was the only word he had. It wasn't enough, and it was all there was to send.

"Warden?" Kandan was looking at the thumb.

"Nothing." Devan closed his hand over the beads. "Count the people in the shed again."

"I've counted."

"Count them again."

Kandan went. Devan sat in the white heat with a wet needle in his lap and his side stitched like a torn sail, and listened for a pain that was not his own.

The ribs throbbed. His thumb burned. Somewhere south, a woman who had walked into the palace with a throat full of lime had a welt rising hot along her ribs in the wrong place at the wrong hour, and had already decided whether to scream.
