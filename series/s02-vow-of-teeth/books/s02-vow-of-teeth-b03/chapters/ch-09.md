# Chapter 9: The Unsealed Letter

"Said it," Neela panted, "the day I—"

"Climb first," Devan said. "Finish it at the top."

So she climbed. The cart road up from the lower narrows ran a mile and more, and they took it at a half run, with Pavi and the rest of the six behind them and the pony's hooves clattering. The horn had not sounded again. She counted her steps instead. Four hundred and twelve to the first bend. The wounds on their two arms pulled at each other with every stride.

She had not finished the sentence. She knew how it ended. I chose that the day I said the word to you. But there was no breath left in her for it, and she was glad. Some answers wanted to be said standing still.

At the padippura the gate stood open to its width. In the yard sat a grey horse with a green sash across its saddle and a boy holding its bridle as if it might bite. The boy was Kannan. Beside him stood Pepper, the white goat, studying the saddle with the face of a creature planning.

The rider was a thin man in a dusty green sash. He stood on the gate chamber stair with a leather case in one hand, and Muthu stood two steps above him with his arms folded.

The Company had come into the lower court from the barracks side, forty-odd people in drovers' cloth, very quiet. Garrison men filled the rest of the yard. Neela found Kandan's face, then Janu's at the kitchen door, then Bhaskaran's. Everyone was listening.

"By the crown's order," the rider said, in the voice of a man who had said it three times already that morning, "Captain Muthu, holding the seal of Kettamala, is to bring forty men of the garrison to Kodiyur for the harbour wall. Kettamala's walls to be kept by twenty. The captain to ride at the head of the forty. Departure at first light tomorrow."

He held out the case.

Muthu took it. He broke the green wax with his thumb and unrolled the leaf. He read slowly, with his lips moving on the hard words. Neela watched his hands. They did not shake.

Halfway down the leaf his thumb stopped on one line. He read it again. His eyes dropped to the brass-cornered pouch at his belt, and came back up. Neela marked it and put it down to the hard words. The clerk's hand was round and crabbed, and she would have stumbled on it too.

Then he finished, and went still. The stillness came from the chest outward, as if someone had lowered a lid.

Muthu was loud. He laughed from the belly and cursed the weather, the cook, the pass, and himself with the same relish. She had never seen him silent for longer than a breath. Now the yard waited on him, and he said nothing at all.

"He's lying," a man behind her muttered.

No, Neela thought. She had seen Muthu lie, at the feast, over a letter. That quiet had been thin and quick, like a hand drawn off a hot pan. This one was heavy. It had two weights in it.

On one side were six years of grey wax and an oath to a household he had never seen. On the other, Devan in the yard with a bound arm, and forty men at the shelf who had not found his order interesting.

He was not hiding a choice. He was standing between two. She knew exactly what that looked like from inside.

Her first impulse was knife-fast. She could drop a pot, start a quarrel, make the rider's horse bolt, and buy an hour. She dismissed it in a heartbeat. Any hour she bought him would be an hour in which he had not chosen. The crown would call it a plot, and it would stick to Muthu for good.

Don't push, she told herself. Let him stand there.

Devan had stopped at her shoulder, breathing hard from the climb. She kept her eyes ahead. She felt him decide, as a person feels a change in the wind.

He stepped out into the yard.

No one stopped him. He was not the warden, and no one called him by it. He walked to the middle of the court where the sun lay on the stones, a man in a sweat-dark shirt with a blood-stiff sleeve, and spoke to Muthu on the stair.

"I need nothing from you."

The yard went still.

"I'll tell you the cost, so that you choose with your eyes open." He did not raise his voice. He never had to. His eyes went once to the rider, and came back to Muthu. "There's a host three days off and the Company on the road between. This morning we lost Biju, who was seventeen. If you take forty men south, the road has fewer. If you keep them, it has more. I'm not asking for either. The seal is yours, and the oath on it is yours. I wanted you to know what stands on the other side of it."

He stopped. He did not say please. He did not say remember. He never glanced at the garrison to see how it landed.

Neela noted what he had left out. Not where the cut was, nor how deep, nor when it would come down. He had given the rider's ears a price and nothing a rider could carry south. She thought of the coin with the doubled leaf, and her chest eased a little. He was still counting.

He turned his back on the stair and walked to the edge of the yard, and stood, with his hands open at his sides, to wait. It was the hardest thing she had ever seen a man do with his hands.

Muthu stood motionless a moment more.

Then he said, quite quietly, "Bhaskaran. Give the rider a cup of buttermilk and a stool in the shade. He'll want an answer in writing."

"Yes, Captain."

"And find me the black wax," Muthu said. "Not the grey. The black."

* * *

The gate chamber over the padippura was small and hot. It smelled of lamp smoke and tallow. A table stood under the shuttered window with a stone inkwell on it, and above the table, on a peg, hung the iron seal of Kettamala. The disc was cut with a hill and a gate and had been worn by eleven wardens. Muthu had hung it there to write; it had ridden on his cord since Devan put it in his hand in the hall.

She had followed because no one had stopped her. Devan stood at the back, against the wall. Bhaskaran watched the stair. Muthu sat down and drew the ink toward him.

He wrote for a long time. Neela did not stand where she could read it. She watched the pen instead. It did not stall once.

When he finished, he sanded the page and folded it in half, and no further. He took a stick of black wax from Bhaskaran, warmed it at the lamp, and let one dark bead fall at the corner. He lifted the iron disc down from its peg. His hand shook as he brought it to the page, and he waited until it stopped. He pressed. Hill and gate came up clean in the wax, plain as a coin.

Beside the inkwell lay the grey stick he had carried for six years. He did not look at it. It lay where it was.

"Come down," he said.

They went down together. The rider was on his stool in the sun with the buttermilk in his lap. Muthu held out the letter open, with the fold lying flat across his palm.

"Read it," he said. "Here, in front of the yard. I'd rather you didn't carry it blind."

The rider glanced at him, then read. It took him half a minute. Neela read over his shoulder, because she was close enough, and because Muthu had asked.

From Captain Muthu, holding the seal of Kettamala in trust, to the crown's household. I have your order of this day. I keep my men at the pass. I swore to hold this seal for the border and the villages, and that is the oath I answer to. A host is on the coast road and this garrison stands between it and the hill farms. When the road is clear, I will bring the forty to Kodiyur myself and lay the harbour wall brick by brick. Not before. This letter is open. Let any man who wishes read it. —Muthu.

Black wax. Hill and gate. No grey.

She let out a slow breath. Around her the yard had begun to murmur, a low and ragged sound like a field of barley turning.

"The household will not like this," the rider said.

"No," said Muthu. "Tell them I said so."

He sounded like himself. The loudness was back in it. It was not quite a laugh, but it lived in the same house.

The rider folded the letter along its crease. He went to his horse and gathered the reins, and then he stopped and swore. Pepper stood at the saddle with her neck stretched. The green ribbon that had tied the order case lay in the dust, and the goat was eating it, steadily, in long silk strands that hung from her mouth like a fisherman's moustache.

"Get away, you filthy—"

Pepper bit him on the hand.

The yard broke. A gasp came first, then a snort, and then Kandan, at the back, let out a bark that set off Vasu, and the whole garrison went up together. Kannan clapped both hands to his face. The rider swore again, in a thin high voice. The goat went on chewing, with an expression of perfect virtue.

Neela laughed until her side hurt. Beside her, an arm's length off, Devan laughed too. She had not heard it in days. It was low and short and a little hoarse, as if it needed oiling.

The rider mounted with a dignity he did not have. He rode out under the padippura with Muthu's open letter in his fist and one hand wrapped in his sash.

"Thirty to one that goat is stew by evening," Bhaskaran said.

"Take the wager," Janu said from the kitchen door. "I'll hold the stakes."

* * *

Supper was rice and a hot green curry in the long hall, with the Company along one wall and the garrison along the other and the middle left empty by a kind of courtesy. Janu moved between the tables with the pot braced against her hip.

She set a bowl before Devan, then Muthu, then Thilak. When she came to Neela she put the bowl down on the board without looking at it. Then she put down a second bowl beside it.

"For whoever comes after," Janu said, to the air. "There's always someone."

Her eyes stayed on the pot. She did not speak to Neela. She went on down the board, her bandaged hand tucked in against her ribs as if she were holding something small.

Neela sat looking at the second bowl. In all her weeks in this house she had never seen Janu set out a bowl for someone who might come after. She understood it. Forgiveness would have come with words. This was a door left on the latch.

She ate slowly, with both bowls in front of her.

* * *

Muthu found her afterward on the west landing of the barracks, where the light from the kitchen made a yellow square on the boards. He had a cup of toddy in one hand and had not drunk from it.

"A word," he said. "Not here. Come along."

He took her to the lee of the water jars. The cistern chain clanked the hour above them and they let it finish.

"You watched me in the yard," Muthu said.

"I did."

"You didn't say a thing." He rubbed his jaw. "You could have. A word to Devan, a nudge. I've watched you move people by a hair, my lady. You stood still."

"You weren't lying."

"No." He said it heavily. "I wish I had been. Lying's easy." His eyes slid to the toddy and back. "That wax is dry by now. Six years that household had my silence, and I've just spoken. I don't know what they'll do with that. I'll say the rest of this while I can still say it. You're going to Kodiyur."

"Yes."

"Into the palace, if the door Usha spoke of opens."

She said nothing. He nodded as if she had.

"Then listen. The escort were three days in the south range, and every night they took a cup at my table. I counted their cuffs, because that's what I do with men I don't trust. Look." He set the cup on a jar and pulled his own sleeve to the wrist. "Buttoned to the knuckle. Two pegs, never one. A stiff cotton band, pale green. They don't push the sleeve up to drink or to wash. They turn the wrist in toward the body when they take a cup." He showed her. The motion was neat and soft, a habit worn smooth. "If a cuff slips they don't snatch at it. They close the hand and let it lie."

She watched. She had seen all of it before, in the First Guard's hands, and had not read it.

"There's a surgeon at the palace," Muthu said. "I don't know her name. The escort's second was drunk on the last night, and he said it as a joke. She pares the guards' wrists each season. Takes the ridges down with a thin blade, until there's one flat seam and no more. She keeps their throats pasted with lime and ochre." He looked at her sleeve. "She'll know your wrists before she knows your face. Whatever else you are, you won't pass at an arm's length."

Neela opened her hand in the lamplight. The ridges stood thick and layered, the way a tree shows its years. Eleven years of nights, burned and scarred again. She had never been ashamed of them.

"Thank you," she said.

"Don't." He picked up the cup at last and drank. "I wrote that letter today with the whole yard looking. I'd like one thing in this fort that I did on purpose." He wiped his mouth. "That's all. Go on. He's waiting at the cistern, and he's been waiting an hour, and he'd sooner swallow his spear than say so."

* * *

Devan sat on the rim of the cistern housing with his sleeve rolled and the bandage off. The cut on his arm had closed to a long dry line. Hers had closed with it, a pink thread from shoulder to elbow. They had both stopped bleeding at the same time, without saying a word about it.

"The yard didn't want you to apologise for the plan," she said.

"I didn't."

"I know. I was there." She sat beside him, a hand's width off. "I'm finishing something."

He turned his head.

"On the road. 'I chose that the day I said it to you.' That's the end of the sentence. I chose it, and I choose it again, and I'll tell you if that changes." She breathed in. "But that isn't what I came to say."

"Say it."

She had rehearsed nothing. She picked at the edge of her bandage. "When I came here, I thought you didn't touch me because you were afraid. Of me, of what I'd do. Of yourself. I counted your restraint as fear, because mine was." The chain clanked above them, one, two. "I watched you stand in that yard today with your hands open and ask for nothing, and I knew I'd had it wrong. You hold your hand back because the thing it would take belongs to someone else. You ask, and then you wait. You asked me about the cuts. You asked Muthu for nothing."

"Neela—"

"The kindi is full every night with the spout turned left, because I reach left-handed. The knife under my mattress, you've known since the second week, and you never moved it. I read both as a hunter's patience. They were you keeping your hand off what was mine." She shook her head. "Nobody did that for me. They had a number, and they had a leash." She made herself lift her eyes. "I'd like you to hear it said. That's all."

He didn't answer.

He looked at his hands. He turned the scarred right one over on his knee and looked at that too. A long time went by. The langurs in the east cliff started up again, one by one.

"I haven't anything ready for that," he said at last.

"I know."

"I thought I'd have something."

"I know," she said, and found that she was smiling. "That's why I said it."

He gave a sound, short and rough, halfway to the laugh he had given the goat. He did not reach for her. He moved his knee until it touched hers, and left it there, and let her decide.

She leaned.

It was not enough. It would do for tonight. She would be gone by first light, up a hill road, down a coast, into a palace where a surgeon with a thin blade was waiting for wrists like hers.

How, she thought, and held her hand up in the dusk, and looked at it for a long time.
