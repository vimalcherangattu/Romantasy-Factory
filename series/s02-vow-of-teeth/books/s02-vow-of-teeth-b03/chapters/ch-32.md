# Chapter 32: The Night Company

The lamp at the hall door had no shutter.

Neela checked it every dawn, the way she had once checked exits. It was a plain clay lamp on an iron hook, and it burned sesame oil day and night. Devan had taken the shutter off the first evening, prying the little tin door from its hinge with his left hand and a borrowed knife. He had set it on the sill without a word.

Once, at the foot of a stair, she had opened a lamp a thumb's width because a man told her to. She had counted to three hundred and ten in the dark afterward. Now the lamp stood open to the whole pass, and anyone climbing the shoulder could see it from a mile off. It said: *someone is here, and the door is not shut.*

She trimmed the wick and went in.

Weeks had gone by since the charter. The dry season had come early and hard, red dust where there had been mud, and the pass road had opened behind the last of the rains. The Thurai fleet had sailed. Sixty hulls had crept out of the bay on one tide, like a stain going out of cloth. The queen had read the truce aloud on the Kodiyur harbour steps with her own voice and her own name. Usha had stood at her shoulder, and Usha had told it to Neela twice. Fishwives had come down to hear it. Rukmini had sold mackerel through the whole reading and shouted at the scale-boy for staring.

Open truce, the queen called it. Ships might return, under a cloth, to talk. Nobody in the hall believed it was peace. They believed it was a door left on the latch, and they had all learned to sleep beside one of those.

The hall was the old watch-house with its roof mended and its floor swept, and a long table that Thilak had built from the lower court's doors. Sixty-odd names hung on a board by the entrance in lamp-black. Sunlight lay across the table in a slab. Out on the packed earth behind, someone was swearing in a bright, cracked voice.

* * *

"Left," said Devan. "Not the right. Your right is the clever one. That's the problem."

He stood in the yard with his dead arm in its sash and his good hand held out flat. In front of him stood six of the youngest: Kuttu's cousin from the ferry, a pair of Kandan's recruits with the cuffs still too long for them, and Meenu, who still stood with her back to a wall whenever she could. Each held a wooden practice staff in the wrong hand.

"Why?" asked Meenu.

"Because you're going to lose the other one someday." He said it flatly, as a fact about weather. "Everyone does, a little. A hook. A fever. A door shut on it. I had eight years of a drill that needed both hands, and it took a spear to teach me I'd only learned half of it." He turned the staff in the boy's fist, gently. "Again. Slowly. Slow is how you find where it hurts."

They went again. The staves clacked. The drill was a slow open-handed pattern he had made himself at twenty-five, the winter after the ford. She had watched him learn to run it backward with the wrong arm, a hundred times a day, swearing quietly. He did not look at her. He knew she was in the doorway. The welt on her left forearm had risen when the first boy cracked his own knuckles, and Devan's ears had gone a little pink.

Muthu rode up at midmorning, which meant it was the seventh day.

He tied his horse to the rusted gate basket and walked into the yard with his old grin and the iron disc swinging at his belt. He had grown thinner in a month. Holding a pass-seal did that, Neela thought. It was what happened to a man who chose a loyalty.

"Seventh day," Muthu announced. "I've been owed a round for eight years and one month."

"You were owed one a month ago," said Devan. "I paid it."

"You paid half. You'd your good arm up the whole time." Muthu pulled off his sword belt and dropped it on the dirt. Then, with ceremony, he tucked his right hand into his waistband and picked up a staff in his left. "There. Fair, now. I'll not have it said I beat a man with one hand and used two."

The youngsters had stopped. Even Meenu had come off her wall.

"This is a terrible idea," Devan said.

"Yes."

"You're going to hurt yourself."

"Warden," said Muthu, "I've been losing to you since we were boys. Let me lose my own way."

They squared up in the dust. It was not graceful. Muthu led with a stroke so wide that Devan simply stepped under it, and for a breath Neela thought it would end there. But Muthu spun on his heel, wrong-footed and furious, and cracked the end of his staff against Devan's ankle. Devan hopped. Then he stepped on his own sash, and went down in the dust like a felled tree.

Muthu stood over him, panting.

"That's one," he said, delighted. "That's one in eight years. Admit it, you're beaten."

For a moment Devan lay looking at the sky. Then his shoulders began to shake, and the sound that came out of him was the slow dry one she knew, except that this time it came all the way up. He laughed at the sky. He laughed until he had to put his good hand over his eyes. The six young ones watched him in awe.

"Beaten," Devan managed. "By a man holding his own wrist."

"Say it again," said Muthu. He was laughing too, with his head back, the big army laugh that had once drowned out a lie. "Say it for the roll."

Neela leaned on the doorpost and let her ribs ache. Somewhere far off, a welt of her own rose on his ankle and she felt it, a small bright joke in her own bones.

She remembered the day he said he had laughed once before she made him do it. She had stopped counting how many times since.

* * *

The boat came in the afternoon, and it came as a basket.

It arrived on a cart with a fisherman's boy who had walked up from the coast, and it was the sort of woven basket that carried mackerel. The boy had a letter. The letter was from a boatwright at Pazhayangadi, who wrote carefully that the vessel was sound and the keel true, and that he had painted the name as requested. *Kirri.* Twelve feet of coast fishing boat, bought with the Company's first earnings, the pay the crown had sent up in a sealed bag for a month of holding the pass.

The vote had been quick and strange. Kuttu had proposed it as a joke. Madhavi had stopped laughing before he finished. Thulasi had raised her hand first. Then it was hands all round the long table, even the four who never raised them for anything.

Neela turned the letter over and read it a second time.

Kirri had promised to send her anklet back in a fish-basket, a sign, once she had a boat of her own. She had been the guild's best liar, and nobody had believed her. Now there was a boat and a basket, and Kirri herself lay in a cage in the Resin House kennels with her mind going down a year at a time. The anklet was still on her.

Neela had a clean cloth folded. She had washed it three times in the well, with Janu's lye soap, and laid it in the sun. She put it in the basket with a leaf of rice and a note, which she had written four times and torn three.

*Kirri. Your boat is built. She floats. I'll send the anklet down to her when you're ready to go out. Or you come out. I don't know which. Neela.*

She had not written *I'm sorry.* The word had no place in a cage. But she had signed with both names, in the bottom corner, small. *Indu.*

Pachan would carry it to the cage. He carried everything now, with Chathu behind him under a gag cloth at dusk and nothing but a mop and a bucket in his hands. Muthu had seen him on the cook-house step. Gaunt, grey, mopping.

Neela sent the basket down with the fisherman's boy and watched him go.

Ponni was waiting when she turned.

* * *

The old woman had come up the shoulder alone, which at her age was a kind of rebuke. She was small and brown as a nut. She wore a faded cotton wrap and held a bone needle in one fist, as if it were a weapon she had not yet decided to use. She did not look at Neela's face first. She looked at her hands.

"Still tying it," said Ponni.

Neela looked down. Her fingers had been working a sash end all the while, pulling a double hitch tight. She had done it without thought. She did it in her sleep.

"Your mother tied it so," Ponni said. "Same hand. Same little flick of the thumb. She'd not have known she did it." She wiped her eyes briskly with the heel of her palm. "Don't fuss. I've seen the whole thing and I'm too old for you to fuss at."

"Come and sit."

"I'll see this door first."

So Neela took her to the board by the entrance. The roll hung there in lamp-black, sixty names on a plank, with Thilak's careful letters at the top. *Neela,* the first line read, and beside it, in a smaller hand she had added herself, *Indu.* The two names stood together with a short bar between them, like two rivets in one plate.

Ponni could not read. She put a finger on each name and listened while Neela read them aloud. She went over the second one twice.

"That's her," the old woman said. "That's the girl I carried round the bend. She was heavy as a sack of rice and she never made a sound." Her mouth worked. "I thought I'd put her in the ground, you know. I thought it every day for twenty years."

"I know."

"You'll keep both?"

"I'll keep both," said Neela. "One of them is a number I answered to. The other I didn't know I had. I'd like to have them in the same place."

Ponni nodded, once, as if closing a ledger. Then she squinted into the hall and saw Janu's tiffin on the table. It had been sent up that morning by Kannan, in a covered brass tin, with a note in Janu's large furious hand. The note said the rice was overboiled because the cairn-fire wood had been wet, that the greens were the last the garden would give, that Neela was thinner than she had any right to be, and that she should send the tin back clean. A second line added, almost as an afterthought, *Eat the curd first.*

"Who sent that?" said Ponni.

"Janu. She keeps the house at Kettamala." Neela lifted the lid. "She's bound to one of ours, now. She says it's a crime."

"I like her already," said Ponni, and sat down to eat.

* * *

At dusk they voted.

It was the Company's first contract, and the Company took its time. Sixty-odd of them sat at Thilak's table or stood along the walls, with the lamp in the door burning behind them. Kuttu stood at the head with the great ram's horn on a thong round his neck. He had insisted. He had also insisted on speaking first, and had to be asked to stop.

The offer came from the pepper factors of the lower village, in a plain letter with no wax. Forty carts of pepper were stacked at the pass foot, waiting since the rains. The raiders had never touched them. But the road beyond the shelf was long and lonely, and the factors had lost two drovers in the spring. They wanted the carts escorted over the pass and down to the coast road, in daylight, by the Company. They offered pay by the cart.

"Paired members only," Neela said. "That's my one clause."

Heads turned. She had said it quietly, as she said most things, but the room heard it.

"A pair can bleed for each other and know it," she said. "A lone member out on a road with a wound she didn't take is a woman in trouble nobody can trace. Pairs go together. If one is cut, the other knows where, and how bad, and which way to run." She looked down the table. "And nobody goes who isn't ready to bleed for the one beside them. If you've no partner yet, you stay."

There was a silence. Then Kamalakshi, from the bottom of the table, said, "Janu will want a place on the first cart."

"Janu," said Pavi, "is sixty-one."

"She says she isn't."

The laughter broke the room open. It was not the laughter of people at ease. It was the laughter of people who had bled for strangers a few weeks ago and were relearning how to do it for money. Neela let it run. Then Kuttu raised the horn and sounded one short note, the hall's sign for hands.

They voted. Forty-one for, nine against, the rest holding. Nobody had asked Devan to lead it. He sat at the table's end with his dead arm along the wood, and when the count was done he said only, "Pairs, then. We start at first light."

* * *

Mist still lay on the pass the next morning when the horn went.

It was not Kuttu's long bellow this time. It was Bhaskaran's code, blown from the gate far below and carried up the cut on the cold air. One long note, two short. Unarmed strangers at the gate, and not many.

Neela went to the door.

The lamp still burned in its hook. Beside her, Devan came and stood in the way he had learned, a step behind and a little to the side. They watched the road together.

Three came up the shoulder in the grey. The first was a tall man in a pale robe, grey at the temple, with a white cloth tied at his left wrist and another at the head of the staff he carried. A truce cloth. Behind him walked a servant with a flat box. And at the tail, a pace apart from the others, came a lean man with a long jaw and a coat too heavy for the weather.

The envoy stopped ten paces from the lamp. He looked at it for a time. It was the look of a man who had expected a guarded door.

"I am told," he said, in careful, accented Varanad, "that this is the house of the Night Company."

"It is," said Neela.

"I am sent by the lords of the sea. I come under cloth, as your queen has allowed." He bowed from the waist, exactly as deep as law demanded and no deeper. "They wish to hire it."

Nobody spoke. Down the shoulder, someone dropped a bucket.

"For what?" said Devan.

"That," said the envoy, "I am to say to the Company, and not to its door."

Neela hardly heard him. She was looking at the man behind.

He stood with his hands loosely folded, the cuffs of his coat pushed back a little, as if the wind had done it. The skin of both wrists showed bare and pale, and she knew those ridges. She had seen them every day of her life on her own arms. Thick, layered, a decade of burning. Under them, the wind brought her something else. Salt. Sweet, dry clove-bark, and under that the bitter scorch of resin, fresh as a week ago.

She had smelled it once before, on a sleeper by a half-built tower, among sixty-three tents on a slope where her mother's house had been.

The man's eyes came up and met hers. He did not look away. His long jaw moved as if he would speak, then closed. He wore a ring on the thumb of his left hand.

She had read every page the Resin House ever wrote. Eighty-two pages in a single day, and the ones in the ledger's back half, and the stubs. She knew every number and every shape the House had ever made. She had counted them twice.

He was in none of it.

"He's yours?" she said to the envoy, and kept her voice level.

"He is ours," said the envoy. "He asked to come. He says you will want to see him." A small, thin smile. "He says also that he is in no book you hold. I confess I did not understand what he meant."

Neela understood it perfectly. It rose in her like cold water. A rogue, with no master and no ledger, who smelled of a House that had been finished. Someone had been making odiyans in a place she had not found, and had been doing it with the sea lords' salt on them.

Who made you? she wanted to ask. And what do they want with free knives?

She did not ask. She had learned that much. One does not ask a stranger at a door the question that matters. One asks the people behind one.

She turned her head.

Devan was looking at the rogue too. His good hand had closed, once, at his side. Then he looked at her, and only at her, and his face had changed in a way she knew now. It was the face of a man who had spent eight years deciding everything alone, and was waiting to be told what to do.

"I'm asking aloud," Neela said, quietly, so the stranger would hear it and so Devan would. "Before the Company, and before them. How do you vote?"

Devan held her eyes. A breath. Two. In the mist, the lamp burned in its open frame.

"You first," he said.

She had not known how much she wanted it until it was given. She turned back to the envoy, and the stranger, and the box, and the long road down.

"We'll hear you," she said. "All of us, at the table. And you'll leave your staff at the door." She lifted her chin. "We vote at noon."
