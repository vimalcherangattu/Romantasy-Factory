# Chapter 6: Lantana

The resin was thinner than lentil water, and Neela spread it as if it were gold.

Thilak had coaxed it out of the three noon trees before the Company left the shelter. One finger-width for each pair of wrists, and not a drop to waste. She tipped the pinch the Company had voted her onto two fingers and set them against the scars at her throat. The old burn woke at once. She breathed through it, and smeared the inside of each wrist, and slid the plain gold bangle up her forearm out of the way.

Dusk was coming down the gorge like water poured into a bowl. Below the lightning boulder, Kandan's nine stood on the cart road with their backs to her and their spears grounded. Nobody had told them to face away tonight. They did it anyway.

Devan sat on the foot of the boulder, with Kannan's white pebble in his cheek.

"Say it back," he said. It came out round and slow, as if he were talking through a plum. He had never once complained of it.

"You sound like Muthu with a cold."

"Say it back."

"Count what I can. Tents, horse lines, anything with wheels. Don't fight. Don't stop to be clever." She tied off the thought with a count of her own, three breaths. "Be at the cache by grey."

"And?"

"And if I smell your mirror man's friends, I run."

"You count first."

"I'll count while running."

His mouth moved around the pebble. It was not quite a smile, but it was kin to one.

She looked at the stone in his cheek. Once it had been the one thing standing between her and a cage. A sleeping man says things, and Devan had seen her turn. Now there was no cage left to open. Since the night at the Resin House, nobody could close one on her without her own voice beside theirs. The pebble was only a habit now, the way you keep a door propped that no longer sticks.

"You don't need it," she said.

"I know." He shifted it with his tongue. "I like having something in my mouth that isn't an opinion."

Muthu had asked for a thing he could write down. A count of ships was no use from a leopard's mouth, but a count of tents was. Pavi and the others would go south at the next light. Tonight there were numbers to fetch.

She pulled her sash off over her head and laid it with the day cloth in the crack under the boulder, where the stone made a dry shelf. Then she stood in the last of the light and let the burn climb.

It always began in the wrists. The skin went tight and hot, and then her spine remembered it was a rope. She dropped to her hands. Her ribs bent. The ground came up, and with it the smell of the whole hill, a hundred things at once, one of them Devan. He had not looked away. He never had.

A warm hand rested between her ears for the space of one breath.

Then she was gone down the road, low and quick, with the dusk in her mouth.

* * *

The coast road ran downhill all the way, and the dark ran with it.

She kept to the verge. A leopard on a cart road is a rumour, and a rumour is better than a sighting. She passed a burned byre, then a well with its rope cut, then a village that was only a smell of wet ash and a dog gone feral in the ruins. It watched her go by with its ribs showing and did not bark.

The sea wind blew inland. It carried the camp up to her long before she saw it: tar, fish, wet wool, horse dung, cook-smoke and old sweat. It carried her away, up the hill. She had come at the one angle that mattered. She had not planned it. Her body knew.

Then a second smell came across the first.

It was green and rank and slightly sweet. It was crushed leaf, with a sharpness under it like a bruise. She stopped in the middle of the road with one paw lifted.

Lantana.

She had hated that smell all her life. She had hated it in the Resin House yard where it grew along the drain, and again on the east path to the kalari. She had never known why. She had put it down to being a person who hated things.

It rose from a long slope of orange-and-pink flowers on the left of the road, and it was not a smell. It was a house with the door open. She did not remember a house. She stood very still in the road and understood that her body had been remembering one for twenty-one years.

*Nellimala.*

She had the name from a page. She had never had the place.

She put her paw down and went on.

* * *

The footings lay where the slope eased toward the shore. Low stone rectangles, thigh-high, dozens of them, laid out along what had once been lanes. Lantana had grown through every doorway. A cart's width of beaten earth ran down the middle, and the Thurai had made it theirs.

She went in on her belly through the flowers. She counted.

Sixty-three tents. They stood in rows, grey canvas pitched across the old hearths, some of them with a corner pegged to a threshold stone. Three horse lines ran down the far side of the lanes: twenty-two beasts, twenty-six, twenty-four. Two sentries stood at the track end with their backs to a fire-basket, talking about a woman in some other port. A third sat asleep against a barley sack. There were forty or fifty sacks stacked under a hide roof. Seed, then. The sergeant had told the truth.

At the south end of the camp a frame rose out of the dark.

It stood three men high. Its ribs were ship timber, curved and still smelling of tar, and it had been bolted to a cart bed with iron straps. Two shaped planks were nailed along one side. Adzes had worked on it by lamplight, and the chips lay pale and fresh on the ground. It was not finished. It was a siege tower, and it was being built to stand against a gate.

*Kettamala's gate.* The padippura. She counted the rungs of its ladder: nine so far, with room for ten more. She made herself note the wheel hubs, and the greased axles, and the pegs where the hide screens would go. Muthu would want the pegs.

She was working her way along the lantana toward the horse lines when the wind brought her something else.

It came from a sleeper.

He lay on his back on a blanket by the tower's foot, outside any tent, with a cloak for a pillow and one arm flung wide. He was a lean man, no older than Devan. His sleeve had ridden up to the elbow. She was close enough to hear his breath whistle.

Salt. That came first. Then clove-bark, dry and sharp, cutting through the tar like a blade through cloth. It was a resin, she knew it as she knew her own hands, and it was fresh on his skin. It was not nightbark. Nightbark smelled of scorched tamarind and pine sap. This was something else, from some other tree, on some other coast.

She came forward a hand's breadth at a time until she could see his wrists.

The skin on the inside of each was ridged. Pale lines, laid over one another, old and thick. She knew the pattern as she knew her own face. Hers looked like that. Ammu's had. Every girl in the Resin House had worn it.

*Who are you?*

She could not ask. She had no mouth for it. She could only lie in the flowers with her heart going like a fist on a door and know that if she woke him, there would be a shout, and the camp, and no more numbers. Dawn was hours off, and she could not turn back before it.

That was the rule. It had never bitten harder.

She memorised him. The long jaw, the clean-shaved chin, a ring on his left thumb. Then she backed out of the lantana, a hand's breadth at a time, and did not breathe until the horse lines had put forty paces between them.

* * *

The bank above the footings was her only shelter.

She went up it in a long loop and lay down in the lantana where it grew thickest, with her chin on her paws. The camp lay below her, a smear of low fires. The tower stood over it like a rib cage. She could still smell salt and clove, thin as a thread, though the wind had turned.

Hours stood between her and the grey.

She could do nothing with them. She could not go back and she could not speak, and she could not tell the hill what she had seen. So she lay there with it, and the lantana filled her nose, and she let herself go on smelling it.

*I was here.*

She tried to find a door, and there was no door. She tried a wall, a hearth, the face of anyone. She had a hand's worth of sounds: someone knocking a net-weight against a post, and a woman's voice, low and cross. They might have been real. They might have been the tide.

Everything else had been taken at six years old. A cart. A rope. A blindfold. The Resin House had cleaned her out so thoroughly that she had never known what was missing.

*Indu.*

It was her name, and it meant nothing to her, and it was hers. Somewhere in this ruin, a woman had stood over a net in the dark and said it. It had been a good name. It had been said in a good voice.

She pressed her face into the earth.

A leopard cannot weep. She found that out lying in the dark above her village, with her whole body shaking and nothing coming out of her at all. She was mourning a place she could not picture. She was mourning people who had no faces. She thought she was a fool, and the thought did not help.

She stayed there until the sky began to grey.

* * *

The cache was where she had left it. She came down the last slope with her legs shaking under her, and she barely made the crack in the boulder before the dawn took her.

It always tore. She had come to expect that. It left her on all fours on the cold earth, with every muscle cramped and her stomach folded in on itself like a fist. The hunger came behind it, hollow and sick, and her hands would not stop trembling.

"Here."

Devan stood three paces off with his back to her, one arm held out behind him. Over it lay her day cloth. He had warmed it. She could feel the heat of it on her skin before she touched it. He must have carried it inside his coat all night.

She took it and wrapped herself. He turned.

He had a leaf packet in his other hand. Rice and curd, still faintly warm, folded tight. He put it in her lap without a word, sat down on the stone across from her and spat the white pebble into his palm.

"Eat," he said, in his own voice.

She ate. It was the best meal she had ever had, which she was beginning to think was true of all of them. When the packet was empty he took it from her fingers.

"Numbers first," she said. Her voice came out cracked. "Sixty-three tents. Horse lines of twenty-two, twenty-six, twenty-four. Three sentries on the track end, one asleep. Barley sacks, forty or fifty. A tower." She described it, rung by rung. He did not write. He said the figures back to her under his breath, once, and she saw them go into him like coins into a purse. "Muthu can have those."

"He can."

"There's more." She held the cloth closed at her throat. "A man by the tower. Sleeping. He had a resin on his wrists. Not nightbark." She found the words as she said them. "Salt and clove-bark. Sharp. And his wrists were scarred. Ridged, like mine."

Devan's face went still. It was not the stillness of a man hiding something. It was the stillness of one holding a very full cup.

"The same scars."

"The same kind. I don't know." She shook her head. "I couldn't ask him. I couldn't turn back. I lay there six hours with it in my head and no mouth to put it in."

"You have a mouth now."

"I know." It came out as a laugh and broke in the middle. "That's not all of it. That's not even the main thing."

She stopped. He waited. He did not fill the quiet with a question. After a moment, he moved a little closer, which was what she needed. She had not known it until he did it.

"It smelled like lantana," she said. "The whole slope. It's the smell I've hated since I was small, and I never knew why. It's my village, Devan. I walked down the road and my body knew it before I did." She pressed the heel of her hand against her eye. "I lay there all night and tried to remember one thing. One door. I couldn't. It's gone, and I'm grieving it, and I can't even say what I'm grieving. It's stupid."

"It isn't."

"It's a smell."

"It's a place." His voice was flat and level, and it was the kindest thing she'd heard in weeks. "I've had six years of a stair. It doesn't need a picture to be a loss."

She did not answer. She could not.

The lantana grew right up to the boulder's foot, a thick orange bank of it, bruised pink where the dew had settled. Devan looked at it. He did not say a single word about the Thurai, or the tower, or the scarred man. He leaned across, took a handful of leaves and crushed them in his fist. The sharp green reek rolled out over both of them.

He held his hand open between them, and breathed it in.

Then he slid down off the stone and sat beside her in the lantana, shoulder against shoulder, and did not try to make it better. The sun came up over the hills and found the two of them there. It did not seem to mind.

* * *

They walked up the cart road when the light was full.

Her legs held, mostly. His arm stayed close enough that she could have leaned, and she did not need to. She noticed that, and put it away to think about later.

The nearest pass village lay where the road bent. It was one of the Monkey Pass villages, Kesu's among them, a handful of mud-and-thatch houses with a well in the middle. Refugees from the coast had filled every doorway. They sat on cloths in the early sun with their bundles and their children, saying very little, as the women at Kettamala's well had done.

Neela slowed without meaning to. She was saying names under her breath, as she did, and gathering faces like a woman gathering fallen fruit.

At the end of the row sat an old woman with a net across her knees.

She was mending it with a bone needle, her fingers dark and quick and knotted at the joints. A tear ran along the lower edge. The thread went through the mesh, looped, and came back under itself. Looped twice. Drew tight.

Neela stopped walking.

She knew that knot. It was a fisherman's double hitch. It was the one Ammu had tied round every pepper packet, the one a net-mender behind the sheds was said to have taught them both. It was the one that had always been in her own fingers.

She looked down. Her hands were working at the end of her sash. She had not told them to. A loop, a second loop, and a pull. The same knot.

The old woman's needle stopped.

She looked up, not at Neela's face, but at her hands. A long look. The net slid down off her lap and lay forgotten in the dust, and her mouth came slowly open on a word she did not say.
