# Chapter 10: Before the Road

Rain came onto the south range after dark, thin at first and then steady, drumming the tiles over the guest room like a hundred fingers counting.

Neela sat on the edge of the cot with the leaf of resin open on her knee. Six pinches, black and glossy, folded in a square of banana leaf. Six for the road. The Company had voted them, hand by hand, and she had been the last to raise hers.

She did not fold the leaf shut. She said it aloud, because said things were harder to cheat.

"One pinch saved if I stay human tonight. One bare night on the count." She touched the first fold. "The ache comes a moon after the last turning, and Nellimala was the last. So tonight costs nothing yet."

"Yet," Devan said from the door.

"Yet." She tipped her head at the leaf. "Kodiyur is roofs. Palace roofs, sea wind, a cliff. Every roof is a night, and every night is a pinch. If I turn tonight, I'm one roof short in the palace." She folded the leaf. "So I stay a woman. It's a cheap night. The expensive part comes later, and I'm writing it down now so I can't call it a surprise."

He came in and shut the door. The lamp on the sill threw his shadow long across the floor. He had washed. His hair was wet, and the grey in it showed at the temple.

"You've never spent a pinch for me before," he said.

"I haven't spent one. I've saved one." She laid the leaf under the pillow. "It's an old guild habit. Name the debt before it names you."

* * *

He sat on the stool. He did not sit beside her. She noticed that and filed it, and then laughed at herself, because she had stopped filing his distances weeks ago. Twenty-seven years, and she had never once chosen a night slowly. She meant to learn it before the road.

"Say it plainly," she said. "What it does. Once, before we go."

"The share." He rubbed his thumb along the old white line at the base of his other hand. "You'll be in the palace, climbing. Walls, drains, the cliff stair. Anything that cuts you opens on me as a welt. At the shelf I'll be fighting, and you'll carry my cuts. You'll feel it at the worst moment. You'll be hanging from a ledge with a guard below and feel a spear go in my thigh."

"A welt," she said. "Not the spear."

"A welt, if it's a cut. If it's worse—" He stopped. "You know the rest. You felt the poison."

She did. She remembered the tearing along her own side in the Resin House yard, when his ribs had gone. She remembered not understanding it and understanding it all at once.

"Then we say what we do when it comes," she said. "Before it comes. So neither of us has to decide in the dark."

"You first."

"If I bleed, you don't come." She said it flat, like a count. "You've a pass to hold and thirty-six who'll die if you leave it. A welt on your arm means I scraped a wall. It does not mean I'm dying. If it's dying, you'll know, because it'll be your own breath failing, and you still don't come, because by then it's four days and you'd arrive for the burying."

His jaw worked. "And you?"

"You bleed, I don't come. I stay in the palace roof until the work's done." Her voice stayed level. She was proud of it. "Whatever opens on my arm, I finish the job."

"Unless the vote sends me."

She looked up.

"The Company's hands put me at the gorge," Devan said. "If the hands send me somewhere else, I go. That's the only door I leave open." He met her eyes. "I won't promise you more than I can keep. I told you that once."

"You did." She nodded slowly. It stung, and it was right, and she would not have trusted him if he had said anything else. "Then say it again, so I know it's one bargain and not two."

"If I bleed, don't come," he said. "If you bleed, I won't. Unless the vote sends me."

"Unless the vote sends you."

They each said it twice, as they had with the ledger. It was beginning to feel like a ritual, which frightened her more than a knife would have. A ritual meant you expected to need it.

* * *

"We'll want a signal," she said.

He had already thought of it. She could tell, because he looked at her and then at the sill, where the lamp stood, and then did not speak. He was waiting to see if she would get there by herself.

"Noon," she said. "Every noon. A prick, so small it's nothing, so nobody could mistake it for a fight." She dug in the pouch at her hip. "I've thorns. Lime thorns, from the bush by the kitchen well. Three each."

She laid them out on the blanket, dark green, as long as her little finger and sharp as stitching needles.

"Three pricks on my thumb at noon," she said. "Three welts on yours."

"And I've the tooth." He reached inside his shirt and drew out the leopard canine on its cord. It was yellowed, no longer than a thumb joint. She had moved it from her waistband to his neck herself, the last night at Kettamala, and he had not taken it off since. The guild had meant it for his wound. He wore it against his chest.

"Three small welts means alive," Neela said.

"Three means alive."

"Fewer means—"

"Fewer means I'm busy. Not that you're dead." He frowned. "None is the thing I won't think about."

"Then think about three." She picked up a thorn and held it out. "Practice. Now. So that neither of us panics at the first one."

He took the thorn. He did not prick himself. He held it between finger and thumb and looked at her, with the rain loud on the roof.

"Do it," she said.

He pressed it to the pad of his thumb, once, twice, three times. Three beads of red. She did not look at his hand. She watched her own, laid open on her knee. Three small stinging dots rose on the ball of her thumb, bright and hot.

She breathed out.

"That's what alive feels like," she said, and was surprised how thin her voice came.

He put the thorn down. He crossed the floor, knelt on one knee in front of the cot, and took her hand in both of his. He put his mouth to the three red dots, one after the other. She felt it on his thumb too, a warmth on a wound he did not own.

"Still goes both ways," she said.

"Still."

* * *

She pushed him back onto the blanket and followed him down. He let her. His hands stayed open on the cloth, as in the yard, until she put them where she wanted them, and then they were not careful at all. She kissed the corner of his mouth, the hinge of his jaw, the grey at his temple. She went slowly. She had decided to be slow, in this one room, before the road. She was good at fast. She was done with fast.

His shirt came off over his head and she sat back to look.

Eight years of drilling alone had made him lean and hard, and he wore it the way another man wore a coat. But what she looked at was the rest. The white seam at his ribs from the ford, puckered and shiny. The scar through his brow. Seven flat pale marks across the knuckles of his right hand. The ropeburn on his palms from the well shaft, healing in stripes.

"Let me," she said.

He didn't ask what. She laid her fingers on the seam and followed it from the ribs to the hip. Then the brow, the knuckles, each mark, one at a time, the way she had once learned every servant in a strange house. She named them in her head. Ford. Stick. Wood. Stone. A man who had stopped hitting anything that hit back.

"You're counting," he said, very low.

"I'm learning." She bent and put her mouth on the seam. His breath caught in the hitch she had listened for all those nights, a third of the way through the long count. "Ford," she said into his skin.

"Neela."

"I know where they are now. All of them. If anyone opens one, I'll know it's yours."

He made a sound she had not heard from him. It broke off in the middle. She felt it in her own chest, as if she had made it herself. When his hand slid up her ribs and found her, warmth ran from his palm to hers and doubled back. She gasped. She could not tell whose skin was whose.

"It still does that," she said, unsteadily.

"It does." His voice had gone rough. "I don't think I'll get used to it."

"Good."

She laughed against his shoulder, helplessly, and then she stopped laughing.

She led for a long time. She took him in and held still and let it be slow, and watched his face, which he did not hide. He looked at her the way he looked at the whole fort from the wall-walk, as if it were a thing he had decided to keep. When his hands tightened on her hips, she bent and whispered against his ear, "Now you."

He turned them over with great care. He did not rush. He asked with his eyes first, and she answered with hers, and then he moved, and the room narrowed to the creak of the cot and the rain and the heat of him. Whatever he felt, she felt as well, an echo of it rolling down her spine and out into the dark, and she told him so, in broken pieces, and heard him say her name like a word he was learning.

At some point she set her teeth in his shoulder. She did not break the skin this time. She pressed hard enough to leave a pink crescent, and a matching crescent bloomed on her own shoulder, hot and sweet.

They both stopped. They looked at each other.

"Last time that was play," she said.

"Last time you drew blood."

"Tonight it's practice." She grinned up at him, wild and breathless. "Bite me. Then don't move."

He bent his head and bit the curve of her neck, gently. Pain flared on his own, and he lifted his head, startled, and she saw him wait for the old reflex to rise. The one that said hurt meant help, that his body should already be gone, up the stair, out the door. His hands braced either side of her. His whole back shook with holding still. He stayed.

"Not a summons," she said.

"Not a summons." It came out like a vow.

"Again."

He laughed, shaken, and did.

* * *

Afterward the rain was still going. She lay with her head on his arm, the blanket kicked to the foot of the cot, a pinch of cool air from the window across her legs. His heart beat under her ear, slower now. The lamp had burned down a thumb's width.

He spoke into the dark. He did not usually begin things. She waited.

"I'm not afraid of dying," Devan said.

"I know."

"I thought you might think so. It isn't that." His chest rose and fell. "I'm afraid I'll stand at the shelf with the line bending, and a welt will open on my arm that is yours, and it'll be the kind that means you're on a ledge with nobody below you. I'll know it. I'll feel it as a fact. And I'll have to pick." He did not say between what. "Your welt, or the line."

She lay quite still.

She had expected a lot of things from him. A comfort, an apology, a vow. Not this. It had cost him to say it, and she could tell that he had said it in the plainest words he owned.

"Then choose the line," she said.

His arm tightened.

"That's what I married," Neela said. She lifted her head so that she could see his face in the lamp. "A man who holds. Not a man who runs after every bleeding thing. I know exactly what it costs. I've counted." She laid her palm flat on his chest, over the tooth on its cord. "I'll do the same. If your welt opens, I'll stay on my ledge and hate you, and finish, and come home. And you'll be at the door."

He said nothing for a time.

"You'll hate me," he said.

"For about a day."

"I can bear a day."

"I know you can." She put her head down again. "That's why I picked you."

* * *

She woke at the middle watch with the rain gone. The air in the room had that scoured quiet it gets after a downpour, when the tiles tick as they cool. Devan was asleep, on his back, the hitch in his breath coming steady as a clock.

She slid free of him and went to the door for no reason she could name. She wanted to count it. She always counted the door.

She opened it a hand's width.

The corridor lamp outside was lit.

She stood and looked at it. It was a plain clay lamp, the kind that hung from a hook by the stair, and it burned with a low, even flame. Someone had trimmed the wick. Someone had filled the cup to the brim, so that it would last until dawn without a hand near it. A smell of sesame oil hung in the draught. At the bottom of the stair, under the door of the north range where Muthu slept now, a line of yellow showed.

In the first weeks of the marriage the lamp outside the warden's door had been kept dark, and a boy posted at the stair. The boy was Raiu. Nobody had said why. A dark corridor had been Devan's kindness, once, so that nothing could see.

Now Raiu was ash, and a lamp burned in a corridor, and nobody had said a word.

She knew whose hand. She had watched that hand set down a second bowl at supper and look at the air instead of her. There would be no word about it in the morning, and there would be no thanks possible. It was a door left on the latch.

Neela touched the cool clay of the lamp with one fingertip.

"Thank you," she said, to the corridor.

She went back in and shut the door gently, and left the flame burning.

* * *

They rode out at first grey.

Five waited in the lower court with the horses, and the pack pony stood among them, looking put upon. Pavi had a spear across her saddle and her curled forepaw of a hand tucked in her sleeve. Thilak sat his brown cob like a rock set on a pillar. Madhavi, Kuttu and Meenu were pale and quiet, with the look of people who had said goodbyes the night before and did not mean to say more.

Janu stood at the kitchen door, her hands wrapped in her apron. She did not come across the court. She nodded once, short, and went back inside.

Devan stood by the mare. He had not slept much. He held out a leaf packet of cold rice and curd, and a folded cloth, still warm from the brazier.

"For the first night," he said. "In case."

"I've a cache plan for the road."

"In case." He put the bundle in her hands, and kept his own on it a breath longer than he needed to. Three small dots showed on the pad of his thumb, healed to pink. Hers had faded the same.

"Noon," she said.

"Noon."

She did not kiss him in the yard. She would have fallen apart. She mounted instead, and gathered the reins, and rode out under the padippura with her back straight, and counted the horses behind her until she had five.

The hill road dropped away in loops, wet and shining. Langurs barked on the cliff and then, as they passed, fell silent, and she took it for the rain-sodden morning. A mile down the cut she heard the dull far boom of the gorge below, water running over the broken rock. The sun came up red behind the ridge.

An hour down the hill road, her left forearm began to burn.

It came slowly. First a prickle at the inside of the elbow, then a line of heat, thin as a thread, running from wrist toward the crook. She pushed back her sleeve with one hand and looked.

A welt was rising. Narrow, red, shallow, about the length of her hand. As she watched, it beaded at one end.

She knew where it came from. She knew it the way she knew her own number.

Far behind and above her, in the cut he had spent a week making into a bad day of stone, he was already fighting.

Her horse walked on. The mare's ears flicked back. Pavi, three lengths behind, said sharply, "Neela?"

"It's a graze," Neela said. Her voice came out level. "His. Shallow."

"Do we—"

"No."

She did not turn her head. She did not slow. Her fingers closed on the rein, and the welt pulled with the movement, and she let it. If I bleed, don't come. If you bleed, I won't. She said it to herself in the rhythm of the hoofbeats, once, twice. She counted the bends in the road instead of the welts. She counted until the cut was gone behind her and the sea showed, a grey seam at the foot of the hills.

Another line rose, lower on the arm. Then nothing.

She rode down toward the coast with his blood hot on her wrist, and she kept the bargain.
