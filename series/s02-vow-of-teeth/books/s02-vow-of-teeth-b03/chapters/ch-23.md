# Chapter 23: What Vellan Carries

The queen's captain would not take an order from a man with no office, and he said so before Devan had finished unrolling the map.

"You gave back the disc in front of this whole hall," Captain Haran said. He was a broad, grey-moustached man who sat his chair as if it were a saddle. "I carry the queen's word to three hundred spears. I don't carry yours."

They sat at the long table in the hall, under the eight pillars: Haran, Muthu with the iron disc at his belt, Usha with her sleeves pulled down, Thilak with his big tapper's hands flat on the board, Neela at Devan's left. The queen sat at the head of the table in the warden's chair, with a cup of water she had not touched. She had said she would listen.

Devan had drawn the cut in chalk on a plank. Forty paces wide at the gorge. Narrower at the shelf, where Raman had died. Fourteen hundred on the far side of the river.

For five years, men had done what he said because the disc was on his belt. Nobody had ever answered him, and he had never had to hear whether he was right.

"Then don't take it from me," he said. "Put it to a vote."

Haran's moustache lifted. "A vote."

"Five of you hold something on that wall. You hold the crown spears. Muthu holds the garrison and the seal. Usha has the queen's riders, Thilak the Company's hands, and Neela the Company's voice. I hold nothing." He laid the chalk down. "I'll say what I'd do, and each of you can say yes or no. If it's no, I sit down."

"Sutlers vote," Haran said. "Wardens command."

"Let him, Haran," Nirmala said, from the chair. She turned the cup a quarter turn. "I'd like to see what happens."

Devan told it plain. Crown spears in three ranks on the shelf wall, behind the stakes Kandan's men had cut, because they were the only ones with the numbers to take the first ladders. Garrison archers behind them, with Muthu's eleven sheaves. The Company in the thorn below the lip and on the wall by turns, rotated by the hour so no one was spent before the tower came. Kandan's ten to the cart road, to cut the horse lines if the line bent.

"And where does my household stand when the line bends?" Haran said.

"Where it can retreat to the wall. I'd rather your men lived and ran than died and held." Devan heard how it sounded. "That's the part you'll like least."

Neela spoke for the first time. "Captain. Who is your second?"

Haran looked at her as if she had offered him a sweet. "Sethu."

"Then I'll want Sethu at the stakes with Kandan before dusk," she said. "They should know each other's faces before they have to know each other's backs."

Nobody laughed. Haran's mouth worked.

"Hands," Muthu said, in a voice that was almost his old loud one.

Muthu's went up. Usha's. Thilak's. Neela's. Devan raised his own last, and it felt strange, a hand held up like a boy's among people who could have sent him from the room.

Haran kept his on the table. "No."

"Five to one," said Muthu.

The captain looked at the chalk plank for some time. "I'll hold the centre," he said. "Not under that man. Under the table." He glanced at the queen. She gave him nothing, only the dry watching look she had worn the day before.

"That will do," Devan said. To his surprise, it would.

* * *

At dusk he went down to the shelf.

The stakes stood in a double row, new-cut ash, the points still pale. Below the lip the river ran brown and loud. Beyond it, the Thurai fires made a low orange smear along the far bank. He counted a hundred and ten of them and stopped.

Vellan was at the lip, where the cut narrowed toward the top. The bear's limp showed in the man today, a drag and a step. He stood with his arms crossed, looking at the place where the walls drew in to eleven paces.

"There," he said. "I'll hold that."

Devan came and stood beside him. "The cut is the last narrow before the gate road. If they take it, nothing stands between them and the court."

"That's why."

"Alone?"

"Unbound." Vellan did not turn. "No one has named herself to me. If I'm cut there, no one else bleeds for it. I've handed enough people my wounds." He gave the cut a nod, as he might have to a bad neighbour. "Let the others bleed for the people they chose. I'll bleed for nobody."

"The Company voted the line. You'd be off it."

"The Company's voted me a hand. Mine's up." A small rough sound came out of him that might have been a laugh. "You'd have done the same."

Devan would have, once. He did not say it. The wind came down the cut, and with it, faintly, the smell of the far bank's smoke.

"She said—" Vellan began.

Devan went still.

The big man's jaw moved. His eyes were on the narrow place, not on Devan. "Asha. At the end. She said—" He stopped. "No."

"Vellan."

"After." Vellan uncrossed his arms. "If there is an after. Not on the night before a fight. You'd carry it up the cut instead of your spear."

Devan had waited six years. One more night he could do. What he felt was not forgiveness; he did not have that to give yet. It was nearer to the thing a man feels when a hard animal finally lets him within reach. He did not step closer.

"After, then," he said.

Footsteps scuffed the path behind them. Kamalakshi came up from the gate arch with a rag bundle held against her ribs, the way someone carries something she's afraid will be seen. She was perhaps nineteen, thin, with her hair cropped short, and the shelf stones rang under her sandals as if she were trying to make them.

She stopped three paces from Vellan.

"Here," she said, and held the bundle out at arm's length.

He unwrapped it. A bird, the length of his thumb, carved from pale wood. One wing was thicker than the other. The head sat too large for the body, and the beak, which should have been whole, was a blunt wedge that looked ready to bite.

"It's bad," Kamalakshi said.

"Yes."

"I haven't forgiven you."

"I know."

"Don't know it so loudly." She scrubbed her palm on her hip. "I did it with your knife. I wanted to see if it was hard."

"It's hard."

"Good." She turned on her heel and left, and did not run, which Devan thought must have cost her.

Vellan stood with the bird in his hand a long time. Then he put it inside his shirt, over the heart, and turned back to the cut.

Devan looked at the river. He gave the man the privacy of a view, as Neela had once given it to him, and the Thurai fires crawled on the far bank, thick and slow.

* * *

The pit below the east wall smelled of sesame oil and red earth. Lamps burned in their niches round the rim. Muthu was already down in it, barefoot, his shirt on the step.

"The seventh day," he said. "You owe me a round. I counted."

"I know I do."

"I've owed it eight years and a bit. I'd like the interest in blood." Muthu grinned. It was his old grin, wide and a little foolish. "Don't worry. I'll be gentle with the stitches."

"Don't be."

He went down the north steps barefoot. The ribs pulled under the silk. He let them. Muthu knew where the old seam was and where the new stitches were, and Devan said so, and Muthu said he'd take it as a map.

They did not use marma. They went to touches. Muthu came in low and fast, as he always had, and Devan gave ground and took it back, and the packed earth slapped under their feet. It had been eight years, and then a few mornings, and the body remembered what it had been taught as a boy. His open hand stopped a hair from Muthu's ribs. He had practised that stop alone for years, in front of a post, and now there was a man at the end of it, laughing, who lifted his own hand to answer.

On the fifth pass, Muthu's palm landed on Devan's left side below the ribs. It was light. It would have been a rib broken, and a lung after, if either of them had meant it.

"There," Muthu said, breathing hard. "It drops. When you're tired. It always did."

"I know."

"You've always known. You've never done anything about it." He stepped back, rubbing his chest. "Who found it, then?"

Devan looked up. Neela sat on the north steps with her bound hands around her knees. She had not made a sound. Her lips were moving in a small count, steps or breaths, he could not tell.

"My wife," he said.

"She told you."

"She came down those steps on the eleventh day, put her fingers at the back of my neck, counted three, and told me my left dropped. I didn't turn. I held my breath." He wiped his face with his forearm. "I never did anything about it."

"Then do something," Muthu said, and went up the steps for his shirt, and passed Neela as he did, and put two fingers to his brow. It was a captain's salute, given to no one in the garrison, and she returned it with her chin.

Devan climbed out. The lamps burned steady. He stood in front of her with the sweat cooling on him, and for a breath he did not know how to ask.

"On the wall," he said. "In the line. Wherever I'm standing when the ladders come. Will you take my left side?"

She did not answer at once. She looked at the pit, at the packed earth, the marks of their feet.

"I'll be a cat by then," she said.

"Then the cat." He held her eyes. "My left drops. You know it, you found it. I'd rather it were yours to cover than anyone's. It's the weak side. I'm putting it in your hands."

"Whether it's hurt or not, I'll feel it."

"I know. That's why you."

Her mouth moved, and she did not smile. It was not that kind of thing. She reached out and put two fingers, light as a clerk's, on the cloth over his left ribs, where Muthu's palm had landed.

"Yes," she said.

He let out a breath that had nothing hidden in it.

He realised on the way up the steps that Muthu had watched the whole of it, and so had the lamps and the men on the wall, who had come to look at a warden fighting. He had used his strength in the open. Men would vote on what he said and count how badly he had stood. He had thought it would feel like having his skin off. It felt like being seen. He found he could bear it.

* * *

A hand on his shoulder in the middle watch.

He was awake before the voice. The lamp in the passage showed him Koman, the old pass-village watcher, with Unni behind him holding a torch. Koman's breath came in short pulls, and his eye had the stillness of a man who had run up a hill.

"Warden," he said, and Devan did not correct him. "I was on the lip. The far bank is moving."

Neela had sat up beside him without a sound. Her hand had found his wrist, as if it knew.

"Moving how?"

"The spies said after dark, the second night. They'll come at dusk, while the light's still in our eyes." Koman wet his lips. "They've brought the tower up in the dark to the shingle. They're not waiting for full night. I counted ladders until I couldn't." He spread his hands. "Dusk tomorrow."

Devan swung his legs to the floor. The stitches pulled. He felt Neela's other hand close on the sheet.

"The queen's spies were wrong," he said.

"The queen's spies," Koman said, with a flat old man's patience, "were not on the lip."

Unni's torch spat. Down in the court the cistern chain clanked the hour, three, and was answered by nothing.

Neela was already out of the bed and reaching for her cloth. Her face in the torchlight had the shut calm of someone doing sums she did not like.

"I set my answer for dusk tomorrow," she said. "At the gate. With the Company and the queen."

"Yes."

"That's the hour they come." She tied the cloth at her waist with a hard small pull. "My answer and the first ladder, in the same dusk. She'll want it sooner. She'll have to. They all will."

"You don't have to give it by first light."

"No." She looked at him. "But I won't be given the time to be afraid of it either. I'll go and wake the queen."
