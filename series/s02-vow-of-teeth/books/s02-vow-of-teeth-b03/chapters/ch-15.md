# Chapter 15: The Sea Stair

By day there was no shape to hide in.

Neela had never minded that before. At night the other one hid her. At noon she was a tall woman in borrowed boots, hanging off a cliff by her hands.

The rope was stiff with salt and it burned through the rags she had wound round her palms. Forty feet of rock went up in front of her nose, black and weeded at the foot, dry and brown higher. Below, a mackerel skiff rode the swell under the overhang. Rukmini had lent it for a day and four panam and asked nothing. Pavi held it against the cliff with one oar. Her left hand, the old-broken one, lay curled on the gunwale like a sleeping crab.

"The slot's a body wide," Pavi had said. "Usha swears it."

"Usha put the odds at three in four."

"And you?"

"I think Usha is loyal to a building."

Pavi had laughed once, short, and then not again.

Neela had pricked her thumb before she started. Three lime thorns from the bush by the Kettamala kitchen well, a full half hour before the gun. She had sat in the skiff with her sleeve pushed back and watched three beads come up. It was not noon. It was not nothing. He would read it for what it was, a word said early, because she would have no hand free at the hour. She had told Chathu the cost was Devan's to hear from her. This was the first instalment.

She climbed. Fifty-one breaths. The slot was a stone-framed gap in the stair roof where spray had eaten a beam, and she went through it on her stomach and came down on a wet step with her heart going.

The rope slithered up after her. Pavi hauled it off the face before the quay could see a washing line on a cliff. Then the skiff was gone from the edge of the slot, sculling for the point.

The sea stair took her.

It was roofed in dark stone and loud with water. She stood still and counted. Below her, down a long bend, two men talked at the landing. Usha's pair. They faced the water gate and had never once looked up. She went up on the outsides of her boots, step by step, and did not hear them stop.

* * *

At the top was a door of black wood, and it was not barred.

She didn't like that either, but she had a list of things she didn't like and a stair behind her. The passage beyond was screened in carved teak. Light fell through the fretwork in lozenges. She slid into the niche behind the last screen and held her breath with the count.

Voices. Women's, and a clink of keys. Then another, warm and unhurried, with a trace of laughter in it.

"No, leave the shutters. I like to hear it."

A courtesy to an attendant. No raised voice. Neela had spent a month learning to fear a woman she had never heard, and the voice was kind. That was the worst thing it could have been.

Skirts went along the passage, away from her. A door closed far off. Forty breaths. Fifty. She counted every one and the passage stayed empty.

*Noon sits audience from the gun,* Chathu had said. *Which means she leaves before it.*

She went along the screens to the third door and into the queen's room.

It was plain. She stood a moment and let the plainness settle. Whitewashed walls. A narrow bed with a grey cover. A table, a stool, a brass lamp, a shelf with eleven books and a gap for a twelfth. A window of shutters left open on the sea, and the sea, huge and flat and bright, filling it. She had expected silk. She had expected a room that told her what the woman was.

On the table lay a stack of petitions as high as her hand. She moved close, because she looked at everything, and read the top one.

*Cheru's widow, of Kalladi. Seed grain for the second sowing, and her husband's last pay.* Under it, in a quick slanting hand, wedged between the lines: *Seed goes tomorrow. Pay is slow; I have paid it from my own purse, and you are not to thank the roll. Hold the well. Nirmala.* The whole name, as if a widow deserved all of it.

She turned over the next. And the next. Every one was answered. Every answer was in that same hand, no clerk's copy, no stamp. Forty petitions, perhaps. Forty people who now believed someone had heard them.

*She signs death writs in the same ink,* Neela thought. The thought did not fit anywhere, so she set the paper back square on the stack.

Under the window, on the floor, stood a chest of red lacquer with a bird picked out in gold.

She knelt. She would wait for the gun. The lid would sing on its hinge and the gun would drown it. She laid her palm flat on the lacquer, which was warm from the sun, and heard the harbour tower take its breath.

* * *

The gun went off, and the room jumped.

Something took her under the right arm and dragged.

It came from nowhere. A line of fire from ribs to spine, ragged where a blade would be clean, catching and pulling as it went. She knew the shape of it the way she knew a rotten stair. Not a cut. A hook. Something with prongs had gone in and kept going.

She bit down. Her tongue split against her teeth and the copper taste filled her mouth. She did not make a sound. The gun was still rolling off the rock and would have covered a scream, and she kept it in anyway, because the guild had spent twenty years teaching her what to do with a scream.

*Dragging,* she thought. *If it's the start of the drag, I'll know in a minute.*

The welt burned. It did not widen. It did not pull her down. It was hot and weeping along her ribs, soaking the guard's coat, and it held.

*He's alive. He's alive, or he was.* The thought had no floor. She put it aside by force and lifted the lid.

The chest was not locked.

The farm lock hung from the hasp with its shackle up, as if someone had meant to come back and turn it. Her skin went cold. *Opened worse at fourteen,* Chathu had said. She had come with the picks in her cuff and the plan in her head, and the lock had not waited for either.

Inside, on a bed of folded blue cloth, lay a stack of pages.

She counted them with the tip of one finger. Thirty. Each was a stiff pale leaf with an entry in resin-ink black as a beetle's back and still tacky after years. Each had a thin strip of another paper looped to one corner by a twist of green thread. An intake roll. A name and a village, for every one of them. A person.

She did not read the top entry. Her eye caught a number in the corner, a date, and the third line, which was the one that named the shape, and she dropped her eyes before it could settle. A name read in a hurry was a name you carried.

But in the margin, beside the entry, was a small brown bird.

Wings folded. Head turned. Drawn in a fine thin line, like a tick in a ledger.

She had seen it before. She knew where, and the knowing came in pieces. A tally book in her wrap, with the same little bird inked beside the lines marked *by leave*. Thurai boats at Nellimala crossing a bar at slack water. And the back half of a ledger, thirty cut stubs, a folded bird by each.

*By leave. Whose leave?*

The itch was enormous. She had no hand to scratch it with. Voices in the passage. Not near. A door, a laugh, somebody calling for water. She turned the leaves one by one. Every margin, the same bird. Thirty out of thirty.

She slid the stack into a sheet of oilcloth, folded it flat, and bound it under the guard's coat against her ribs with the long strip she had brought. It sat against the welt like a hand pressing on a bruise. She shut the lid. She left the lock exactly as she found it, shackle up.

At the door she looked back once. The sea filled the window. The petitions lay in their square stack.

She went down.

* * *

The stair was empty all the way to the landing, and at the landing the sixth bell began.

It was a thin bright sound off the harbour tower, and it was the sound Usha had promised. The pair at the post straightened, said something to each other, and walked off down the lower flight to be relieved, each with one shoulder a little higher than the other from a long standing. Neela pressed into the shadow of the bend and counted.

Sixty breaths for the change. The last twelve with the landing bare.

She reached ten in the dark and the post was empty. She reached twelve with her feet on the stones of the landing and the sea flat as a plate beyond the arch. No wind. If it had been blowing it would have been nine, Usha said.

She crossed in eleven. At the twelfth she was below the post, and the relief came up the stair behind her in a clatter of spears, and no one looked down.

It had fallen to the breath.

A palace guard of eleven years who had measured that landing till she dreamed it could do that. Neela told herself so down the last flight and out through the fish wicket, where porters in wet wraps hauled in the palace's afternoon catch and did not look at her. Nothing in a palace fell to the breath. She had been in houses for a living. She knew which locks were left open and by whom.

Pavi was at the sea steps with the skiff.

"Go round to the salt store," Neela said. "I'll walk."

"You're white."

"I climbed a cliff."

Pavi looked at the front of her coat and said nothing. Then she shoved off.

The cliff path went up from the steps between gull-white rocks and wind-bent thorn. Neela made twenty paces and sat on a stone. Her hands were shaking. She held them out in front of her and watched them do it. They had done everything she asked for an hour and they had saved the shaking for now.

The sun had gone an hour past noon when her left thumb stung.

One.

Two.

Three.

Three small welts, hot and neat, one beside another on the pad of her thumb. An hour late. Late, and not a word, and the oldest word they had.

She put the thumb against her mouth, where the split tongue was, and for a while she did nothing at all.

*Alive.* He had been on the ground, or something had been in him, and now he had a hand free and a tooth to prick with. The bargain had held. He had waited out the hour, and she had waited out the hour, and the not-knowing had cost them each the same. She had told Chathu it was Devan's to carry too. She would say so to his face.

The pages pressed her ribs. She sat on the cliff path in the white afternoon with thirty people against her skin and a thumb that said *I live,* and the success of it was as hollow as a dropped shell.

She stood. She went up to the lane.

* * *

By dusk the drying shed smelled of salt and old fish and the lamp-oil Usha had brought. Racks of split mackerel hung in rows like the washing of the dead. The side door was bolted. The front door was bolted. The door behind the nets was open a hand's width, and Thilak sat on a crate beside it with his boar's patience and a broken cup of tea.

They came in ones and twos, in plain cloth, with their sashes left in lockers. Neela had learned the names by the second lamp. Sajan, bitter about the queen. Lata, whose brother's wife had had a reply to a petition. Sanu, the youngest, who had carried notes for a year and never read one. Twenty-seven others whose hands she took one at a time, whose mothers' villages she would not know till morning.

Thirty. She counted twice.

She laid the oilcloth on a barrel and did not unfold it.

"I have your pages," she said. "All thirty, and the rolls that go with them. I haven't read them. I won't tonight."

No one moved. Somewhere in the racks a drop of brine fell and was loud.

"Tomorrow at dawn, in this shed, in daylight, I read each one aloud to the one it belongs to, and put it in her hand. After that she holds her own leash, and no one else can read it against her. That's the rule, and it works. I've done it eighty-two times."

"And the room hears our shapes," said Sajan.

"Everyone in it. You'd have new witnesses. I'll ask the room to be only people you chose." She made herself say the rest. "That's the cost. I won't dress it."

"What happens to us after?" said Lata. "When she finds the chest empty."

"She'll know it was me."

"She'll know it was one of us."

"She'll know it was me," Neela said again. "I'll be the one she hunts."

Lata looked at her a long time. Then she looked at her own hands and said, "I'll take my page from the queen's hand, or I'll not take it."

"That's yours to say."

"I'm saying it." She stood. She did not seem angry. She seemed a woman who had finished a sum. "I'm sorry. She's never hurt me."

"I know," Neela said. "That's the trouble."

Hands, then. Kandan wasn't there to count, so Neela asked and counted herself, slow. Usha's went up first and her face did not change at all. Sanu's followed, shaking. Sajan's last, hard, as if it hurt him. Twenty-two. She counted them twice.

Eight stayed down. Lata and seven who gave no reason. They went out one at a time by the net door, and Thilak held it for each, and did not look at their cuffs.

* * *

It was full dark when the shed emptied. Neela stood in the net door with the cool coming off the harbour and the oilcloth against her ribs, and she did not feel like a woman who had won.

*Too easy.* She had said it on the stair and she said it again. The chest left open. The landing bare to the breath. A voice that kept the shutters open to hear the sea.

"Neela," said Thilak, very quietly. "Look up."

The palace stood on its cliff above the roofs, a black shape cut out of a darker one, studded with a few late lamps. She had stood below it all her childhood and seen nothing but a wall. She knew its windows now by the count: the third door beyond the black one.

One lamp was moving along the outer gallery.

It came out of the long hall's end and went along the cliff wall at a walking pace, steady, held at the height of a shoulder. It passed one window and the next. It turned the corner where the passage began.

It was going toward the queen's room. Toward the red chest, and the lock hanging open with its shackle up.

It did not hurry.
