# Chapter 18: Twenty-Two Riders

"Safety isn't a small thing," Lata said. "Say what you like about it."

She said it to the road ahead, not to anyone. The road was red dust, a day north of the last post, and twenty-eight horses were raising it in a long tail behind them. Neela rode at the front and heard every word.

"Nobody's saying small," said Sanu.

"You are. Every time you open your mouth." Lata rode with her back straight and her cuffs buttoned to the knuckle. She was Third Guard, the one who had refused her page in the fish shed and would take it only from the queen's hand. She had come anyway. She had been at the Salt Gate at dusk with a horse and no explanation. "A vouched name. A roof with a lamp in it. No hunter on the road who may lay a hand on you. Thirty years she hasn't caged one of us. Tell me what the hill has given you that matches that."

"It gave me a brother," Sanu said.

Nobody answered. The hooves went on. Neela knew the rest of it, because she had read it aloud at dawn four days back, in a shed that smelled of brine. Sajan's leaf had come ninth. *Pallikara, a tailor's son, sold at nine, one brother younger, sold at seven.* Sanu had been standing by the door. Both men had gone the colour of ash, and neither had spoken until the shed emptied.

"He hasn't said yes," Sanu said now, lower. "I know he hasn't. I only want to be asked what I'd do with the yes."

"Then ask him," said Lata.

"I've asked him. He says he'd bleed for me and he'd rather not." Sanu laughed, a short wrecked sound. "That's the nicest thing he's said to me in twenty years. I think he meant it kindly."

Sajan, riding twenty paces back, kept his eyes on his horse's ears. Neela did not turn her head.

Usha rode at the rear with her sleeves pulled down. At the Hill Gate the queen had put a strip of paper in her hand, folded small and sealed in green wax with the sunbird. She had not let Usha break it. She had said the line aloud, so that Neela heard it too, in that warm unhurried voice: *Ride with her. Press nothing.* Usha had carried it unbroken ever since. At the third post, when the stable-master saw twenty-eight riders and began to ask questions, she held the strip up whole, sunbird to the lamp. He fetched fresh horses and asked nothing more. She tucked it back in her sash, and it rode there all day behind Neela's shoulder like a hand not quite laid on her.

* * *

The offer was honest. That was what made it hard.

She turned it over for the hundredth time while the road dropped into a dry riverbed and climbed out again. Thirty knives under her hand. No page on any of them, vouched under the crown, no hunter allowed within a day's ride. No one would need to name themselves to anyone, and so no one would need to bleed. A spear in a night-guard's thigh would be a spear in a night-guard's thigh. Nobody's husband. Nobody's sister.

She would be their protector. She'd be no one's, and everyone's master. And she would never have to stand in front of a living person and ask them to bleed for her.

That was the part she kept coming back to. Not the fort, not the lever. She had asked it of Ammu without knowing she was asking, and Ammu had paid. She would not ask it of thirty more.

*Safe, unseen, and mine.* Warm in the mouth. A coin laid on a table.

Against her saddle, under the flap, lay Chathu's tin. She had taken it from the cell because her hand did it before she thought. She had eaten one, in the dark, standing, and the sweetness had gone to ash on her tongue at once. She had not touched another. It rode against her thigh all day, warm still from some fire she had not seen, and she did not open it.

*A gift is the start of a bill.* The queen had given her no sweets. She had given her a fly stuck on a page and a hand held out flat.

"Neela," said Thilak, riding at her stirrup. "You've stopped breathing again."

"I haven't."

"You hold it on the climbs." He didn't look at her. "It's all right. I only count."

* * *

They made camp in a dry grove off the road on the third night, under teak trees with the leaves gone leathery and brown. Nobody smeared. It had been Devan's last order before she left, and the Company had carried it out of the shelter on its back. The air went blue and the stars came on over the hill road. Twenty-eight odiyans and guards sat round a small fire in their own skins.

Neela walked to the stream with her cloth and her kit and a bar of the brown soap Pavi had bought from a tinker.

For five days she had worn a green starched cuff and a throat paste of lime and ochre. It had dried chalky. It cracked when she swallowed. She knelt by the water and began to wash it off. The paste came away in grey ropes, and the water took it, and under it, the scars.

She heard them come up behind her. She had known they would.

She did not turn. She scrubbed until the skin stung. She undid the cuffs and rolled them back, the green cotton, the wedding bangle under the left one, and laid her wrists on her knees with the palms up. Throat. Wrists. The pale thick ridges, layered over each other like a tree's rings. Eleven years' worth.

Then she stood and walked back to the fire and sat down in the light with every inch of it showing.

Nobody spoke. The queen's guards were pared smooth at the wrist and painted at the neck. Their scars had been taken from them by a surgeon, once a season, and they'd learned to think of it as care. Sanu looked at his own cuff, and then at hers.

"Does it hurt?" he said. "The real kind. Still."

"The burn's the first night. After that it's the morning." She turned her wrist to the fire. "I don't hide mine. I'm not telling you not to. I just want you to see what it looks like when you leave it."

"A question for a question?" Kuttu said.

He had come up on the other side with a pot of rice. He was Kumaran, a ferryman's son, not yet thirty, and it had been his idea in the first place to call the forty a Company. He sat down hard and began to ladle.

"That's how they did it in the Resin House," he said. "Nobody told us. You get a question if you give one."

"We did it at the long table," Neela said. "I'd forgotten."

"I haven't," said Madhavi, and sat.

So it went, as the rice went round. Meenu asked Lata what the queen had given her that nobody could take back, and Lata said, after a long moment, "A name that was always mine. She called me by it before anyone else." Thilak asked Sajan why he'd served the queen so long, and Sajan said, "Because I thought if I was useful enough, she'd tell me where my brother was." That was a harder answer. The fire cracked. Sanu put his head down.

Lata turned to Neela. She did it the way she did everything, straight on. "What does it cost, then? The name. I want the cost, not the poem."

Neela told her. She gave the cut that opened on someone else, the fever that dragged another body toward the ground, the three welts on a thumb an hour late. She said she'd had the ache, and the forced resin, and a man's wound opening across her side in the Resin House yard. Then she said the rest, because it was the question.

"I've never felt so tied in my life. And I've never been less owned."

"Both?" said Lata.

"Both." She held her wrist to the light. "It took me a month to learn those are different."

Lata looked at her a long time. "I don't know that I'd pay it," she said.

"No. You don't have to. That's why I won't take the offer by myself."

It was out before she had looked at it. She sat there while it went round the fire, face by face. Usha, beyond the light, had lifted her head.

* * *

Later, when the others slept, Kuttu sat with her on the stream bank. He didn't ask. He turned a stick in the coals and it threw sparks.

She did not know she was humming until he went quiet to listen.

It was the old tune. Ammu's, the fishing song she'd hummed at nine and twelve and twenty-four, when her hands were busy and her head was not. It had no tidy words. Neela had made up the half she didn't know.

"Pull and pull," she sang, under her breath. "The tide is coming in."

"I know that one," Kuttu said. "My father's ferry crew sang it. Different tune." He hummed his version, flat, slower. "Where'd you get it?"

"From someone who heard it at a net-mender's knee." She stopped. *A net-mender behind the sheds,* Ammu had always said, *taught us both the hitch.* There had been no net-mender behind the sheds. There had been Ammini, a net-mender of Nellimala, and her hands, which Neela's fingers had carried up the goat path without her. Maybe a girl of seven had tied it for a smaller girl, and Ammu had never had the heart to say whose knot it was.

She sat with that, and then with the other thing it knocked loose.

Three short yips. A pause. One long rising note. *Tomorrow, I will find you.* She had heard it a hundred times as a child, from a girl with a gap in her teeth. She had heard it a last time in the Resin House yard, from a jackal's throat, the dusk Ammu drew Vellan and the hunters off. Neela had stood there in shape and could not answer.

She had always taken it for a goodbye. A promise Ammu had broken because she died.

But Ammu had given it on the nights Neela went off alone to settle something that was not hers alone. The stone empty, the cup bare, and that thin cry from the spur. It had never meant *I'll arrive.* It had meant *don't decide without me.*

"What?" said Kuttu.

"Nothing." Her throat hurt. "I only just heard what she was saying."

He didn't push. He threw the stick on the fire.

She lay awake after, on her back, with the stars. She tried the queen's offer one more way. If she took it, nobody would bleed for her. She would give the Company safety, and she would also hold the door shut on the choice she had made at a dusk turning, with a man looking at her. The only freedom she had ever had was the one she had been given, and had given back.

She could not decide that alone. Ammu had said so across a season and a grave. It wasn't hers to decide. It belonged to the forty-two with a page in a cuff, and a plank of names, and a man on a shelf who held the same offer in his own hands and would call it a lever, if he had any sense.

The Company voted on resin, on a sally, on a hill. It would vote on this.

And Devan would have a voice among theirs. He had said so himself, in the shelter, with a pebble in his cheek and no office: *I go where the hands send me.*

She was afraid of it.

She had watched him do it the night the hands sent her south. He had put a river pebble in his cheek before the count, so that whatever he felt would stay behind his teeth until the last hand was up. Then he had said *yes* to the result. One flat word, level and careful, for the pass, for Kesu's people, for a boy in a red headcloth. He had said it the way a man shuts a gate on his own fingers and keeps his face still. She had not been able to tell what it cost him. She had only seen that he could pay it.

That voice would say yes to the queen. Or a no said for her sake, so that she'd never know whether she had wanted him to say yes. Either way he would say it gently, and she would have to stand there and hear it.

She fell asleep counting the stars she could name, and she knew only four.

* * *

They climbed the last day without stopping. The road bent west, steepened, and went to stones. By afternoon the langurs had gone silent on the cliff faces, and she did not need Thilak to say what that meant. Strangers on the road. The Thurai, or the hunger of them.

Nobody sang. Even Lata rode without a word. They went up through thorn and scree, up past the lightning-split boulder where the goat path left the cart road, and Neela knew the place without a map, and for once did not wonder why. She did not slow.

The sun was a hand off the west rim when they crested the shoulder above the pass.

Below them, past a long fall of rock, the cut lay in its own blue shadow. A thread of river. A shelf of stone beneath the cliff, small as a hearth-ledge at this height. There was smoke over it. Not the cooking smoke of a camp. A thick black column leaning with the wind.

She gathered the reins.

The first sting came in her left palm. It was like a bee, small and bright. She ignored it. Then the right went, and the ones that followed were not stings. Heat rose under her skin, across the pad of each hand, along the base of each finger, slow and then fast. She looked down. The leather of the reins had not changed. The skin under it was lifting, white, in two bubbles the size of a thumbprint, and then in four.

She did not cry out. She made a sound in her throat and her horse tossed its head.

"Neela?" That was Thilak.

She held both hands up. The blisters shone in the low light. She turned them over, back and front, the way a woman looks for a splinter. The skin wasn't broken. It was only burned.

*Fire.* Not hers. She had touched nothing hot. Somewhere below, under that column of smoke, a man had taken hold of something that was alight, with his bare hands, and had not let go.

"Ride," she said. "All of you. Now."

And she put her heels in, blistered palms on the reins, and went down toward the smoke.
