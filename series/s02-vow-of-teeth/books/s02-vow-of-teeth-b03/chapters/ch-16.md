# Chapter 16: Safe, Unseen, and Mine

The first name Neela read aloud belonged to a girl who had stopped answering to it at six.

"Intake," she said. "Thulasi's friend, no, that's a wrong lead. Give me the line again." She held the leaf nearer the shed's one grey window. Dawn came through the slats in stripes and lay across the mackerel racks. "Chandrika. A tailor's daughter. Of Pallikara, by the river. Sold at seven."

Sanu stood very still in front of her. He was the youngest of the twenty-two, and he had carried notes for a year without reading one.

"That's me," he said, as if someone else had said it.

"Yes."

"I thought it was a girl's name."

"It was your mother's mother's. It says so, two lines down."

She read the rest. The number. The date, six years back, in a witness's hand. Then the line that named the shape, which she said aloud in the shed's cold light because the rule said she must, in full, before the one it belonged to. Twenty-one people heard it. Sanu closed his eyes. When she folded the leaf into his palm his fingers shut on it like a trap.

"It's yours," she said. "Nobody else can read it against you. Burn it, keep it, eat it."

"Keep it," he said, thickly. "Can I keep it?"

"Anything but give it away."

That was the rule of the work. A page read and handed over, and the room left holding what it had heard. She had done it eighty-two times at the Resin House with the sun on her back. She had not known it would be this hard with a roof on.

She did them in ledger order. Each came forward. Each heard a village that had not been said aloud in fifteen years, a trade, a mother's name. A woman called Bindu's-sister's-daughter learned she had a sister. A man of forty with a soldier's walk was a fisherman's son from a place that had since gone under the tide, and he laughed once and sat down on a crate.

Usha was seventeenth.

She came up with her hands open and her sleeves pulled to the knuckle, as she had come up the hill to the Company. Neela read the leaf. Birth name, a hill village, a potter's daughter. Usha's real name was Ushakumari and she had been called Usha the whole time. It was the only one that had survived being sold.

"That's it," Usha said, when Neela finished. Her voice gave nothing.

She took the page. She folded it twice, small, and put it inside her cuff against the pared-flat seam on her wrist. She said not one word of thanks, and went back to the wall.

Neela noted it the way she noted everything. A woman who had not asked a single question. A woman who had stood nearest the door since first light.

Twenty-two. When the last had gone out into the lane, blinking, the shed was empty but for Thilak at the net door and the smell of brine.

* * *

Noon came up the harbour in a long white glare.

Neela sat on a crate with her back to the racks and ate cold rice, because Thilak had put it in her hands and not moved until she did. Pavi had gone to the salt store to sit on Madhavi. Kuttu and Meenu had the front. The pages were gone, every one, into twenty-two cuffs and sashes and hems. Against her ribs there was only the fading ache where a hook had not been.

*Alive.* She had said it to herself for a day. He would be at the cistern shed now, or on a wall. Noon was in minutes. She would prick three times with the thorns in her sash, the right hand this time, so he would feel it clean.

She had the first thorn in her fingers when Thilak lifted his head.

He did not make a sound. He only turned the broken cup in his hands, once, the way he did when he scented a thing before it came into view. The net door moved. Not a push. A hand, laid flat and patient, and the door swung back a handspan, and then wide.

A woman stood in it with a writing case.

She was not tall. She wore plain grey cotton, a widow's wrap, and her hair was greying under a cloth. She was fifty-four. Neela knew the number as she knew the sum of a lock's pins. She knew the voice before the woman opened her mouth.

"May I come in? I would knock, but there is nothing to knock on."

Behind her, a step back, with her sleeves pulled down, stood Usha.

There were no soldiers. Neela looked past both of them, down the alley, to the roofs and the blank wall opposite. No green sash. No spear. Thilak's eyes found hers and asked a question with his whole boar's body. She gave him the smallest shake of her head, and he moved his bulk aside from the door.

The queen came in alone, and every door in the shed was open behind her.

* * *

"No," she said, when Neela stood. "Sit. I've been walking since the gun and I would like to do the same."

She took the second crate. It was low, and she settled on it without fuss, her case across her knees. She looked at the racks of split fish. She looked at the stripes of light. Her face was lined and kind, and tired in a way that had been earned a petition at a time.

"You climbed in by the spray slot," Nirmala said. "My grandfather's masons left it. I've been meaning to ask them to close it for thirty years. I'm glad I never did."

"You left the lock open."

"I did." She said it simply. "I wished to see what you'd take, and what you'd leave. You took the thirty and left the petitions square. That told me more than a good many reports." She unlatched the case. "Usha, would you shut the net door? Thank you."

Usha shut it. She did not look at Neela.

"You told her," Neela said.

"Last night," Usha said. "After the lamp."

"Why?"

"Because you'd run thirty of us into rogue lives by Friday." Her flat voice cracked at one corner. "Half of us hunted by crown and guild. I wanted you to see the rest before you read the first. And she asked me. She asked, Neela. Nobody has asked me a thing in eleven years."

"That's what a leash does," Neela said. "It asks."

"Yes," said the queen. "And I would like to ask you something, too."

From the case she drew a leaf and laid it on the barrel between them.

It was a fresh page. The paper was still the pale, stiff kind. The ink was black and tacky, and a fly had landed on one corner and could not get free. The hand was a quick slanting hand she had read twenty times over in a petition stack.

*Ushakumari, called Usha. Ledger number Four, crown leaf. Night-shape —*

The word was there. Neela did not read past it.

"I wrote it last night," Nirmala said. "In my own hand, after I had fed her tea and heard what she had done. It will take a day to dry. It has taken, as you see." She laid a finger beside the line, not touching. "You carried away paper. You can't carry away what I saw."

Neela looked at it. The shed stopped being a shed.

*A master who saw you turn can always write you again.* She had been twelve or thirteen. She had stood at the door of Chathu's room and watched him bend over a gnawed page, one that rats had been into, resin-ink blooming wet under his reed. *Kirri's,* he had said, not looking up. He had written it whole inside a quarter hour. Neela had walked away believing she had seen a kindness.

And at the shelter, in the autumn of this year, Kamalakshi had asked it straight, with her fresh page in her fist: *What stops a master writing it again?* Neela had said Chathu never needed to. She had not liked how fast she said it.

Burn a page and the witness remained. A page was only the memory of a witness, set down in a form that could be read. Anyone who had stood and seen could write it out afresh.

"The pages in the chest were only leaves," she said. Her mouth had gone dry. "You are the page."

"I am the witness." Nirmala said it gently. "I saw every one of the thirty turn, the first time, in a room of mine. I held each hand. That cannot be stolen." She looked at the white grub of the fly. "I did not tell you in order to frighten you. I told you so that you would not walk out of here believing something that wasn't true."

From the case, once more, she took a sheaf of leaves and set it on the barrel beside Usha's.

They were forty. Neela knew them by their thickness. Black tacky ink, a master's hand. Not the thin untidy brown of the Resin House hand. *Chathu.*

"I had him write at my table, four evenings," the queen said. "Your forty, from memory. He has a very good memory. He wept for the ones who died. He said he'd never had cause to forget a girl." She did not smile. "These are true pages, and true pages hold. Your forty are mine too, whenever I choose."

* * *

Neela did not move.

*Whenever I choose.* The words went into her like the hook, and caught, and she heard herself breathe through her nose.

"You'll say it at night," she said. "To cage them."

"I will not." The queen said it with what looked like pain. "I have not caged a single one of my own in thirty years, and I will tell you what it cost me not to. But I would be lying if I said I could not. You'd know I was lying, and I don't wish to start that way."

"Why does it matter, what I think?"

"Because I want something from you." Nirmala sat back. "I'll say it plainly, and then you will want to know why I have sat in a fish shed to say it."

Neela waited. Her hands were cold.

"The folded bird," the queen said. "You've seen it. You have a tally book in your wrap. You've been trying for a month to learn whose leave."

"Yours."

"Mine." She said it with no shame, and no pride either. "Spread for what the realm sees. Folded for what it needn't." She raised one hand, wings flat. Folded it. "I keep a private hand. Everyone who rules does. You'd have found it sooner or later, and I would rather you heard it from me than from a clerk with a reason."

*Whose leave. By leave.* A boat crossing a bar at slack water without a pilot. A man in grey counting children. She waited for the rest to come, and the queen watched it not come, and did not offer it.

"Not all of it today," Nirmala said. "I'll not lie and not tell. I'll tell you what you need to answer me. Then you'll go and think."

She folded her hands on the case.

"I want the night-guard. All thirty, as one company, under your command. Not under mine. I will write no pages. Not one. They'll be vouched under the crown, so no hunter in Varanad can lay a finger on them, and you will answer to nobody but the throne, and not often to it." She paused. "And I give Devan back Kettamala. The seal, the warden's office, the garrison's grey. No one would call him harbourer again. Safe, unseen, and mine."

It was the way she said the last of it. *Mine.* It was not the word she had come to hear. It came out of a warm, tired mouth like a coin laid on a table.

"It's a good offer," Neela said.

"It's the best I have."

"Why would I take a leash from a hand that's kind?"

"You wouldn't have one. That's what I am offering." Nirmala did not look away. "I'd not even hold the pages. The thirty could burn theirs tonight and I'd stand over the fire. You'd have the knives, and I would have a night-guard that obeyed the woman who freed it, and Varanad would not go dark when the Thurai land."

"They've landed."

"They have." The queen's eyes did not change. "And I have kept a promise to my own people, which I may not break. You will want to ask about it. Not today."

Neela turned the words over, and one stuck.

"There's a better way," she said. "I know it. I've done it. An odiyan says her own shape, aloud, in her own voice, to one person she chooses, at the dusk turning. It voids every other witness. Every one. You. Chathu. The room. After that nobody can cage her without her. It's the only way to be free of all of you at once."

"I know." The queen's voice was very quiet. "I have known it for nine days. Usha told me what a ledger page does when it burns by itself."

"Then you know what it costs."

"Do you?"

It was not a taunt. It was a question put, patiently, to a woman who had been paying the cost since a night at the Resin House with a bleeding wrist.

"You'll have thirty pairs," Nirmala said. "Thirty odiyans each bound to a chosen person, and each pair feeling every wound the other takes. A spear in a night-guard's thigh is a spear in her husband's thigh, her sister's. They bleed for each other for life. And every one of them marked rogue, because the guild cannot vouch for a page that has burned. Your Company will be the freest knives in Varanad, and also the most precious, because every blade has a heart in the shape of a wound. You'll have made them precious, and precious knives don't win wars."

Neela didn't answer.

"I'm not saying it to wound you," Nirmala said. "I'm saying it because I've watched what a wall does to a man whose wife stands on the other side of it. I would not like to watch it thirty times."

*A thumb. Three welts, an hour late.* She had sat on a cliff path with her split tongue against the pad of it, and for some time she had not been a woman who needed anything.

She had told herself it was freedom. Freedom was belonging to no one. She had said it to Ammu at fourteen, and to Chathu in a locked room, and to a man on a rampart in the dusk with resin on her wrists. She had meant it as armour.

And the only time in twenty-seven years she had not been owned, she had been tied to a man by a wound that opened when he cut his chin shaving.

*The only freedom I have ever had,* she thought, *is the one that bleeds when he does.*

She'd not know how to say it. She did not say it.

"You've not yet asked about him," Nirmala said, "and you have asked about nothing else with your eyes. So I will say it. A rider leaves the Salt Gate for Kettamala at dusk with the same offer in my hand. Three hundred crown spears for the pass, and the fort given back. If his wife takes command. If she does not, the rider turns round."

Neela's hands, in her lap, closed.

"He will have it before you do," the queen said. "I thought you would rather know."

"You've priced him."

"I've priced his old office. Which is not the same as pricing him, and you know it. But I'll own it's a lever, and I won't pretend otherwise." The queen's tired face was gentle. "It is a hard thing, to hold a lever on a person one respects. I would not have done it to a stranger."

*He'll get a letter that says his house for my answer. And I can't answer before the rider does.* She had no right to decide his life, and no right to let someone else decide it either. His fort. His father's seal on a nail. Raiu's pyre. A garrison who had saluted him fist by fist at the gate.

If she said yes, she bought it for him. If she said no, she kept it from him, and he would never have asked her to.

"I want time," she said.

"You'll have it. Until the pass." Nirmala stood, and the crate scraped, and she was a plain woman in a widow's wrap with a case in her hand. "You'll be going north to him, and I will be sending what I send. Answer me there. Not before, if you'd rather. I won't ask twice."

She took up the leaf with Usha's name on it, and blew once across the tacky ink. Then she laid it on top of the forty on the barrel and left them both there.

"Keep them," she said. "A witness who tells you what she holds is one you can bargain with. The other kind does not tell."

At the net door she stopped, one hand on the post.

"Thirty is a great many people to have against your ribs," she said. "I know the weight. Don't carry them alone."

She went out. Usha went after her without looking back.

* * *

Thilak shut the door.

Neela sat very still. The light on the barrel was white now, high, falling straight down through the slats. She looked at two stacks of pages, and at the fly in the corner of one leaf, which had stopped moving.

*Noon.*

She had been counting. She was always counting. She went back over the last quarter hour and found where the count had broken: the gun had gone, a dull blow through the rock and the racks, and she had not felt it. Somewhere between *May I come in* and *a lever, and I won't pretend otherwise*, the harbour tower had fired and she had been sitting with a thorn in her fingers.

She opened her hand. The thorn was there. The pad of her thumb was smooth.

She had missed it.

A day behind, she had pricked early. Now an hour late, and no word. And he would sit on a wall or a ladder rung with a stitched side and listen for a pain that was not his own, and hear nothing, and know what nothing could mean.

She took the thorns out of her sash. Three, in a row. She put the point to her thumb and pressed.

One. Two. Three.

Blood came up in three small beads. She watched them, and knew, as she knew a rotten stair, that she would carry the offer north to a man who would already have answered.

"Thilak," she said. "How far to the Hill Gate, on foot?"

"Eleven hundred paces." He studied her. "Neela. What did she want?"

"Everything," Neela said. "She offered it all."

She wiped the thumb on her coat. Then she took up the pages, Usha's on top, the fly still stuck to its corner, and began to count how many hours there were between this barrel and a pass she could not yet answer at.
