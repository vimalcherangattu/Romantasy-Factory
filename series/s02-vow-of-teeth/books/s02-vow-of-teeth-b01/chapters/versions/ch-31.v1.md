# Chapter 31: Daylight Vows

At noon the sky hung low and dark as a wet plum, and Neela walked out into the courtyard with nothing in her hands.

No knife in her cloth. No flask in a fold. She wore the ivory kasavu again, its gold border dulled by two days of damp, and the plain bangle on her wrist. She counted the first ten steps from the west stair and then stopped counting.

They had all come. The garrison stood in lines with spears grounded, sixty men and the rain not yet falling. Janu waited at the kitchen door, arms folded. Chinna leaned beside her on her bad hip. Kannan held Pepper on a rope, and the goat chewed something it should not have. Muthu stood at the front of the soldiers, big and quiet, with his hands behind him.

Devan waited under the padippura edge, where the rain could reach them if it chose. He wore a plain grey tunic. He said nothing as she came. He looked at her the way he looked at weather, closely and with respect.

She stopped beside him and turned to face the fort.

"I am Neela," she said.

Her voice carried too well in the wet air. She let it.

"I came to this house sold, and I didn't mean to stay. I say that first because I've stood in your kitchens and your yard and not told you the truth, and I'd like to start clean." She found Muthu's face. He did not look away. "I'm going to say my own vow. Nobody wrote it for me."

Nobody coughed. Far off, thunder walked along the ridge.

"Two people should be here and aren't. Raiu, who was nineteen, with a cowlick that wouldn't lie down. He held the torch outside my door and never once asked what I was doing awake." She heard Unni breathe in sharply. "And Ammu, who was my sister. She hummed when she was frightened and she was frightened most of her life. She is dead, and I'll say her name in the open as long as I have a mouth. Raiu. Ammu."

The yard took the names and kept them.

"Now the living. Janu, who scolds me and feeds me anyway. Chinna, who has a wager she won't show anyone. Kannan. Pepper, who bites. Gopan, who has kept every hour for nineteen years bar one. Bhaskaran. Unni, whose mother sells salt fish, and I'll buy it. Eravi. Muthu, who rides when he's asked." She went on. She had learned them all, the way she learned every house, and she said every one. Grooms. The woman who turned the wash. The two brothers on the south wall who argued about dice. It took a long time. Nobody shifted their feet.

"That's my house," she said. "I've been given a number most of my life. Here I have names, and I mean to keep every one."

She turned to Devan.

"I take you as my husband, in daylight, with my own mouth. I won't leave you in the dark, and I won't be left there. My promises have teeth in them." Her mouth twitched. "I'll keep them with those."

He took a breath. He had never wasted words and he did not now.

"I'll stand where there's room for two. I won't lie to you. What you give me, I won't hold over you." His eyes didn't leave her. "Ever."

That was all. It was more than she had ever been promised.

* * *

Janu wept first. She wept loudly, with her apron up, and complained in the same breath.

"Rain on a wedding. *Rain!* I said it would turn, I said it to Chinna at the well, and who listens? Nobody. And you in silk in this damp, you'll take a chill, and he'll take it after, and I'll be nursing the pair."

"Janu," Neela said.

"Don't *Janu* me." She seized Neela's face in both wet hands and kissed her hard on the forehead. Then she was gone, and the kitchen door banged.

Food arrived within the quarter hour. Rice cakes, a fish curry with the pepper turned up, plantain fried in ghee, a jar of sweet payasam that two boys carried between them. Devan was handed a third helping before he had finished the second.

"I'm full," he said.

"You're a wall," Janu said. "Walls eat."

Chinna cornered Neela at the trestle with her hip jutting. "I'll tell you now, nobody won the wager. Damp-madness, privacy, Janu's old tale, every one was wrong. I'm keeping the stakes." She looked at Neela sidelong. "I'll not ask what was right."

"Good."

"I'm not stupid, lady."

"I know," Neela said. "I learned your name first."

Unni was crying quietly into a rice cake. Bhaskaran put a heavy hand on the boy's neck and left it there. Someone started a coastal song, badly, and halfway through the second line it cracked in the middle, because it was Ammu's, or near enough, and Neela had hummed it in the yard that morning without noticing. She stood with her throat shut. Then Kannan fed Pepper a whole plantain, and the goat took his sleeve with it, and the court roared. She laughed until it hurt. The grief didn't leave. It sat on the same bench as the laughing, and neither moved over.

Muthu found her last, with a bowl he wasn't eating from.

"Lady." His big voice was low. "I'll have to write this down."

"I know."

"I'll write the weather."

She studied him. He was very quiet, and she knew now what that meant on him. It also meant something else.

"Write what you like," she said. "It's all true."

* * *

By dusk the rain had settled to a steady drum, and the chamber smelled of wet laterite and lamp oil.

Devan had opened the shutters before she came up. He had put a folded cloth warm from the brazier at the foot of the bed, for the dawn. Beside it stood a covered dish and a cup. She looked at those a moment. She had never told him what the morning after cost her. He had simply watched her shake, three weeks running, and noticed.

She stood at the window with her back to the room.

"Door?" he said.

"Six steps." She smiled at the dark. "I don't need it."

The thumb of Ammu's resin lay in her palm, hard and black, smelling of scorched tamarind. She pared a little with her nail and warmed it between her fingers. Then she turned around and looked straight at him, because the rule wanted a face and she wanted him to have hers.

"I'm going to do it where you can see," she said. "I'm not hiding it. I don't want you looking through your lashes."

"You knew about the lashes."

"I count your breathing. I'm not blind."

His mouth moved. "Then look at me."

She smeared it on her throat, over the pale ridged scars, and on the inner wrist of each arm, left and right. The burn came up bright and clean. She kept her eyes on his. Ammu had put this in her hands. It tasted of her.

The pull started at the base of her spine. She went down on the broad sill on her palms. Bone slid. The room swung sideways into smell, rain, wet stone, lamp oil, the wound-salt of his ribs, and his steady heart, which didn't speed. She heard her own breath turn to something deeper. The last thing she saw as a woman was his face, wide open, with no fear in it.

Then she was low and spotted on the sill, and he was breathing slowly across the room.

He crossed to her. He took the river pebble from his cheek, set it on the sill beside her paw, and the small wet click of it was loud.

"I won't need it," he said. "It takes both our voices now, and one of them's yours. But I'll keep it." His fingers closed over it again, and he put it in his sleeve. "It got us through a hard month."

She looked at him, and he looked back.

"Not the cat," he said. "Neela."

She pushed her head into his hand. He let it be as heavy as it wanted.

* * *

The bed took her weight with a groan. She lay along his left side, away from the bound ribs, with her chin on his thigh and her tail over his feet. He lay back in the lamplight with the curtain tied wide and the rain coming in on the sill, and he talked a while to the ceiling, low and without hurry, about nothing. Pepper. The fourteen hounds. A cousin's boat.

Then his breath slowed. A third of the way through each long count it hitched, small and true, no stillness in it. She listened to it all night and did not once count the door.

* * *

First light came grey and wet.

She felt the pull in her spine before she saw it. He was awake, though she hadn't heard him wake. His arm was already round her, and he drew her up against his chest as the fur went out of her and the bones came apart. It hurt as it always hurt. She did not climb the ledge and she did not go to the cleft under the spur. She turned back in his arms, with her face in his throat, and let him see every part of it.

It was the first time anyone had.

Then she was a woman again, shaking, hollow to the root, and cold as a stone in a stream. He had the warm cloth round her before she knew he'd moved. The dish was in his hands. She ate two mouthfuls of rice and curd with her teeth chattering on the spoon, and he held the cup, and said nothing at all. The rain drummed.

"That's it?" she said, when she could. Her voice was scraped. "That's the thing I've hidden since I was sixteen?"

"That's it."

"It's ugly."

"It's you," he said, and he did not say it as a compliment. He said it as a count.

She put the spoon down.

She was twenty-seven and he was thirty-two. They had both been grown a long while, and neither had ever done this in daylight with the shutters open and nothing owed. Her hand found the knot of the cloth at her shoulder and undid it.

"Lie back," she said.

His brows went up. "Neela, the ribs—"

"I know exactly where the ribs are." She pushed him gently down by the breastbone and swung a knee over him, careful of the left side. The cloth slid off her. The cold air touched her and she barely felt it. "I'm leading. Tell me if it pulls."

"It'll pull."

"Then say so."

"I'll say so." His hands came up and rested on her hips and stayed there, waiting. She had never been waited for. She bent and kissed him, slowly, and his hands tightened.

He kissed her throat next. He found the ridged scar there with his mouth, the pale rope of it, and went along it as if reading. Then he took her wrist and turned it and kissed the inside, where the resin had burned her for eleven years. The torn red line was still healing, and a matching one on his own wrist pulled in answer. She felt a tremor go through him. "Does it hurt?"

"No." His mouth moved against the skin. "It's yours. I'm only holding it."

She had to close her eyes.

Her fingers found his ribs after that, the puckered white seam from the ford and the newer cut over it, healing. Beneath her own left breast lay its twin, a thin pink welt like a drawn thread. She laid her palm over his and her thumb across hers, and they matched. "Look at that," she said unevenly.

"I see it."

She moved on him then, slow, with her hands flat on his chest, and he gasped her name against her collarbone. She learned the pace. When she went too fast he said, "Left," once, low, and she shifted, and the pull in his side eased. She rode him through the grey light with the rain loud on the roof and the shutters wide, and he watched her face the whole time with an attention that undid her more than his hands. When she came it was long and shaking and she said his name into the damp air, her real name for him. He followed with his forehead against her breastbone and her fingers in his hair.

She lay on him afterward, listening to his heart come down.

"I'm not hurting you."

"You are." His chest moved under her cheek. "I wouldn't change it."

* * *

The rain was still coming down when they spoke again. Neither had pulled the blanket up. It had stopped mattering.

"The hounds," Devan said. "I'll let them run the yard again. Loose, the way it was."

"They'll bark at the cat."

"They'll learn her."

She laughed into his shoulder. "You'll need to tell Gopan the chain wants oil. The jam in the wheel's still there."

"I'll tell him. In the spring, the road opens." His fingers moved idly on her back. "Muthu takes the cart to the Resin House in three days. He'll carry fodder. I thought you might want to send something."

Her breath caught. "Water for the end cages. A cloth for Kirri's anklet. The anklet itself, if she'll let him. Don't open any of them."

"I won't."

"I don't know what I'm asking."

"A cloth." He said it like a plain fact. "I can do a cloth."

She was quiet. Outside, the cistern chain clanked the hour, thin through the water. Four months, she thought. No rider, no writ, no hand sent from Kodiyur. The fort stood shut and dry on its hill with a prisoner in the guardroom and a grey-sealed letter waiting for a road.

"What do we do with four months?" she asked.

"Eat what Janu gives us." He turned his head on the pillow to look at her. "Mend the bows. Teach Kannan to spar. Open the shutters at dusk."

"And at the end?"

"Ask me then." He touched the bangle at her wrist. "I'll say it aloud. I'll be here."

She believed him. She lay with her head on his shoulder and watched the grey go slowly gold at the edge of the sill, and for once she asked nothing at all.
