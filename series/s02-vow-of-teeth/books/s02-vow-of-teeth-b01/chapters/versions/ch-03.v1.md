# Chapter 3: Closed Shutters

Her hands would not hold still.

Neela woke on her side with the sheet at her chin and a hollow where her stomach should be, as if someone had scooped her out with a spoon. Weak tea light leaked through the shutters. The left side of the bed was empty. She held her fingers up in front of her face and watched them tremble.

The morning after. Eleven years of it, and her body still treated each one like news.

She sat up too fast. The room tilted, righted itself, and showed her the low table by the wall. A banana leaf lay on it, heaped with rice, a stack of plantains, a dark knob of jaggery, and a spoon of ghee going gold at the edges. A brass pot of water stood beside it, beaded with cold.

Janu, she thought. The old woman had stared at her plate all through the feast.

She meant to cross the room like a person with all day. She got there in four steps and went down on her knees. The rice was hot. She ate with her right hand, fast, ghee running to her wrist, and she did not taste a grain of it. At the Resin House the orphans had eaten in rows, and the child who slowed was the child who got less. Nobody had told her that eating was a weak thing to be caught at. Nobody had needed to.

The door opened.

Janu stood there with a basket on her hip. Neela's hand stopped halfway to her mouth, and she felt her face go flat and polite. It was a useful trick. It did nothing for the rice on her chin.

"Thank you for this," she said, when she could. "It was kind."

Janu looked at her for a long moment. Her mouth opened, then shut. "Well," she said. "Somebody had to feed you." She set down a second leaf, and a third plantain, and did not say another word about it. "Wash. Then I'll show you the fort. A warden's wife who can't find the privy is no use to anyone."

Neela washed. Her hands steadied somewhere around the second mouthful of plantain. She counted it as a small debt owed, and filed it where she kept the others.

* * *

The tour began at the pepper terraces, which Janu introduced as if they were relatives.

They climbed in steps up the inner slope, each terrace a ledge of red earth walled in laterite, and every ledge was green. Vines wound up their stakes with the berries hanging in tight strings, unripe and hard. The smell of crushed leaf was sharp enough to sting. A small boy sat on the third wall with a stick across his knees and six goats arranged around him like a council.

"Kannan," Neela said.

He nearly fell off the wall. "You know my name?"

"You carried the garland." She held out her hand. "Is the white one yours?"

"She's Pepper. She bites."

"Then I'll stand back."

Janu made a noise like a kettle. "Don't encourage him. He's learned two new words since the wedding and both of them are about you."

Neela looked at the boy and the goats and the walls, and kept walking. She kept count as she went. Gaps in the laterite where a person could drop one terrace to the next. A drain big enough for a child. Two guards on the upper wall who passed each other at the far end every hundred and ten steps, and turned on the same foot.

The cistern stood at the heart of the fort, a great iron-banded tank under a tiled roof, with a wheel and a chain thick as her forearm. A bent man in a faded mundu heaved a lever as they came up. The chain went over the drum and the sound hit the courtyard like a dropped anvil, and every pigeon in the fort went up at once.

"That's the hour," Janu shouted over it. "Gopan keeps it. Nineteen years, and he hasn't missed one."

"Not once?" Neela said.

"Once," said Gopan, with dignity. "In the fever year."

She learned the day watch changed on the sixth clank after dawn. She learned Gopan's knees hurt in the damp. She learned the sergeant on the gate was called Bhaskaran, and the thin guard at his side was Unni, and that Unni's mother sold salt fish in the lower village and had opinions about wardens.

The kalari lay below the east wall, a sunken pit with its lip worn smooth by sitting. Sesame oil lamps stood in niches around the rim, unlit now, their wicks black. The red earth inside had been swept in long clean arcs. In the middle was one set of bare footprints, sharp as a seal in wax.

Only one.

"Every day before light," Janu said. She was looking at the pit, not at Neela. "Eight years. He used to have a partner. Now he has the earth." She sniffed. "I stopped asking him."

Neela stood at the lip and looked at that single trail of steps. It went round and round. It never crossed itself. A man who drilled like that, alone, with no one at his back, was a man who had decided something about his hands.

It was useful. She told herself it was only useful.

The padippura came last, the roofed gatehouse with its great double door and its four guards in a row. She said all four names. Captain Muthu came ambling out from the guard room with a laugh already in his mouth.

"The lady knows my men better than I do," he boomed. "I'll have to ask her their mothers' names."

"Unni's sells fish," Neela said.

Muthu's laugh broke in the middle. Then it came back, bigger than before. "Janu, she's going to run this fort."

"God help us," said Janu, and meant it kindly.

* * *

She found him in the afternoon on the highest terrace, with his back to her and a handful of green berries in his palm.

Janu had been summoned by a scream from the kitchen. Neela had said she'd find her own way down, which was a lie of the plainest sort. She'd come up here because from the top terrace you could see the whole road to the pass.

"You walk quietly," Devan said, without turning.

"You listen for it."

"I do." He turned the berries over. "These are early. The vines think the rain is close."

"Is it?"

"Forty days, give or take."

She came up beside him. The wind off the pass pushed at her kasavu and she let it. Below them the road fell away in loops, red between the green, and far down a cart the size of a seed was creeping toward the gate.

"May I ask you things?" she said.

"You may ask me anything."

"That's not what I said."

"No." He glanced at her sidelong. "It's what I meant."

All right, she thought. Let's see.

"Did you sleep well?"

"No. Not much."

"Why not?"

"I sleep badly. I told you."

"Did I wake you?"

"You didn't wake me."

It was clean and flat, and she turned it over and found no seam. Her pulse settled. "What do you eat for breakfast?"

"Rice. Whatever Janu sends." A pause. "More, lately."

"Do you like me?"

That got her a longer look. "I don't know you yet."

"Do you trust me?"

"No."

She laughed. It came out of her before she could check it. "You could have said yes."

"It wouldn't have been true."

"People say untrue things to wives all the time."

"Do they?"

"It's most of what a wife hears."

"I wouldn't know." He shook the berries off his palm into the earth. "I don't say them. It saves effort."

She decided to push. "Say the sky is green."

"The sky is blue."

"It's a game. Say it."

"No."

"Say you're glad of me."

He considered the question as if it were a stone he'd been handed. "I'm not sorry," he said. "I can say that."

"Say it was the council's idea and you hated it."

"It was the council's idea."

"And the rest?"

"I won't answer that."

"Then I'll ask the one that matters." She kept her voice light. "Why did your council choose me?"

The wind moved the vines. He stood very still, and for the first time since she'd met him there was something in his face that looked like effort.

"I won't answer that," he said.

"You could lie."

"I could."

"Nobody would know."

"I would." He said it without weight, the way another man might say he'd had breakfast. "Ask me something else."

She looked at the cart on the road. It had not moved at all, as far as she could tell.

She had known men with power over her. Chathu, soft and patient, who lied the way he breathed and gave every lie a column of figures to hide in. The masters before him. The buyers she'd never met. Every one of them told her what was convenient, and she had learned to read them by the hole it left. This man left no hole. He told her the sky was blue and that he didn't trust her, and when he couldn't tell her, he put up a plain wall and stood in front of it.

It should have made him easier to kill. A man like that had no cunning to hide behind.

Instead it made her careful. A man who would not lie was a man who would notice hers.

"Tell Janu thank you," she said, "for the rice."

"I will," said Devan.

And he did not say anything else, and she did not think to wonder why.

* * *

He was closing the shutters when she came back at dusk.

The chamber had gone amber. The sun sat low and swollen over the pass, the sky round it clean as a washed bowl. Devan stood at the west window with one leaf drawn in, and as she stopped in the doorway he pulled the second to, then the third. The third slat gave its dry crack. He lifted the long teak bar from its hook and dropped it into the iron brackets, and the whole window shut like a mouth.

"Rain," he said.

Neela looked past him, at the thin gold line still showing between the slats. "The sky's clear."

"Yes."

"You said rain."

"Rain comes off the pass fast." He rested his hand on the bar a moment, as if checking it was seated. "I like it shut."

That was all. He did not turn the lamp up. He did not explain. He crossed to the table where the supper leaf lay waiting and sat, and began on his rice with the same slow thoroughness he brought to everything.

She stood in the doorway a moment longer.

A fussy habit. Some men kept their boots in pairs. Some counted their coins every night. This one barred a window against rain that wasn't there. She would put it with the sword on the floor and the creaking slat: the small private rules of a man who lived alone and slept badly.

The trouble was the bar itself. It was a good one. A hand's width of seasoned teak, seated in iron, and the only way to lift it was from the inside.

She would have to lift it herself. And put it back.

* * *

She counted his breath again.

One to ten, steady. Forty, slowing. A hundred. At two hundred and twelve he caught, that same hitch like a bucket knocking a well wall, and she let it pass the way she had the night before. A man with a habit. She was starting to collect those.

At three hundred she gave him thirty more. Then she slid out from the sheet and stepped over the sword.

The bar was heavier than it looked. She took it in both hands and eased it up out of the brackets an inch at a time, and set it down against the wall on the side where it would not fall. The shutters swung under her fingers. The third slat cracked.

She froze with her hand on the wood and counted twenty. His breathing went on, long and even.

Out on the ledge she knelt in the cold dark and thought it through, the way she thought through any job. Last night she had come in at dawn, pulled the shutters to, and left the bar propped against the wall where she'd put it. He had not said a word. This morning it had been in its brackets again, because he had put it there at dusk himself, so he'd looked at it. A man who barred a window looked at its bar.

If she left it lifted, he would see. And if he saw it, he would ask. A man like this would ask, and he would listen to what she said, and he would know exactly what the answer weighed.

So it had to go back before he woke. Not from the ledge. From inside, with her hands, as a woman, in the grey, with the shaking still in her arms.

She drew out the flask, and put the bangle on the sill, and did what she did.

The burn took her. The wind rushed in, loaded with a thousand smells. She did not think in words after that. The other one ran along the ledge, dropped to the terrace wall, and went along the stone as if it were a road. She learned the pairs on the wall and how they walked. She learned the third clank after the middle watch, and what a dog smelled like through a kennel door, and that the yard below the padippura lay open and pale. Somewhere in the hours she learned that the chamber door had a guard in front of it and the window had a bar behind it, and that between the two a clean kill had no room to stand.

She did not know which of them was the problem. She would work that out in daylight.

* * *

The chain clanked the last hour, and the sky went grey behind the pass.

She came back along the ledge with her legs like wet rope, and the turning went out of her in a long ragged shudder that left her on her knees on the stone. The hollow was back. It was worse than yesterday, because tonight she had a task waiting at the end of it.

She put the bangle on. She wrapped the kasavu round herself with fingers that kept missing. Then she stood, which took two tries, and drew the shutters in behind her one at a time.

The room was dim and smelled of sesame oil and sleep. Devan lay on his left side with his back to her and one hand open on the sheet, breathing slow. Rice sat on the low table, a fresh leaf, covered by a cloth. She did not look at it. She would be sick if she looked at it.

She lifted the bar. It weighed what it had weighed before, and her arms had less in them. She set the end in the left bracket first, then walked it round to the right, slowly, a finger's breadth at a time, because the iron was cold and the wood would knock if it went wrong.

The bar slid home.

The third slat answered it. A dry crack, sharp as a knuckle, loud in the quiet.

Neela stayed where she was, her palm flat on the teak and her breath held behind her teeth. She did not turn. She counted.

At nineteen she gave up, and looked.

He lay exactly as he had lain. The sheet rose and fell, rose and fell. His hand was open on the cloth. Nothing in his back had changed.

But she had not been watching his back when the slat cracked, and she could not tell whether the breathing she heard now was the breathing from before.
