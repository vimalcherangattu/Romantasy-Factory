# Chapter 26: The Kennels

He did not finish the word. His hand opened on her wrist, and Neela caught his weight before the straw could.

"Muthu!"

The captain came at a run from the stable door, where he had been standing guard with his arms folded and his big face gone quiet. He took Devan under the shoulders without being told. Between them they got him onto a hay bale, then down flat, his cheek against the sack.

"He's only gone under," she said. "The poison does that. Keep him cool. Vinegar and wet cloths, and the shutters open for air, whatever Janu says."

"Open?"

"A sick room breathes." She had it from Devan's own father, by way of Janu. "Don't let Eravi bleed him again. He's got no blood to spare."

Muthu nodded. He was not laughing. In eleven years of soldiers' jokes she had never seen his mouth so still, and she knew what the stillness meant, because Devan had told her. She looked at him anyway, straight, and said the rest.

"If I'm not back by the second dusk, put him in a cart and bring him after me. Follow the wood road to the Resin House. Don't ask me what it is when you get there."

"Neela—"

"Tomorrow's dusk. Not the day after." She pulled the girth tight, and the mare blew. "I'm trusting you with something I can't spare. Don't make me sorry."

"I won't." He said it low, with his eyes on Devan's face, and she could not tell what it was worth. She had no one else to ask.

She swung up. The gatehouse lantern showed Bhaskaran at the padippura, who opened the wicket without a word. Then there was only the road and the dark, and the cistern chain clanking the hour behind her like a door being shut.

* * *

It began to rain before the first light.

She rode the Monkey road downhill with the mare's breath smoking and her nails throbbing at the quick. By dawn the cart ruts ran like streams. By noon the water had got inside her shawl and found her spine. She counted milestones, then breaths, then the mare's strides between breaths. It kept her from counting how many hours Devan had left.

Three days, Eravi had said. Perhaps.

She changed horses at no inn. She did not trust one. She walked the mare through the fords, rubbed her down with a fistful of wet grass at the one dry shelf she found, and ate the heel of bread Janu had pushed into her saddlebag without a word. She was sick of her own hands. The ache sat behind her eyes like a coal. A fever she had not given in to for weeks began to climb, and she let it climb, and rode.

By late afternoon the road forked at a leaning stone, and she took the narrower way. The trees changed.

They went black first at the foot, as though soaked in ink, then all the way up. Their bark hung in plates, and the plates wept. The smell reached her before the shadows did, and her stomach turned over: bitter, scorched, like tamarind left on a fire, with pine sap running underneath. She had breathed it every day from six years old to twenty-seven. It was the smell of her bed and her bowl and the first time a man held her wrist still.

*Blackbark Wood,* she thought. *Home.*

She had never hated a word so much.

She left the mare tied in a thicket a mile out, with the girth loosed and a handful of oats from her pouch. Then she went in on foot, along the old drain path she and Ammu had used as children, when they were out after dark and the cook was asleep.

* * *

The Resin House had not changed. It rose out of the wet wood in tiers of dark roof, with tapping sheds along the east side, each with a trough under its eaves, and the long training yard lying open between the buildings like a swept palm. No lamps burned in the yard. They never had. Chathu said light was for those with something to show.

She went over the wall by the old fig, dropped into the wash of the drain, and crossed the dark without a sound. She knew every board. The third shed along had a door with a loose hasp and a lock that she had learned to open at fourteen, on a dare, with a pin and a held breath.

It still opened for her.

Inside, the smell was thick enough to chew. Troughs. Tapped bark in baskets. At the back stood the cupboard, plain deal, with a brass lock the size of her thumb. She worked her knife tip into it and felt the little wards give one by one. The door swung.

Shelves of small clay vials, stoppered with wax. Dozens. She struck a flint spark and held it, and saw that every vial wore a tag in the same fine hand she had once copied letters from. *Fever, young. Fever, old. Black gall, counter.* Chathu labelled everything. She had never found it funny until now.

She took the last one. It was no longer than her finger, and the wax had a pinch in it where his thumb had pressed. She drew it out, pocketed it in the fold of her shawl, closed the cupboard, and turned the lock back with the knife.

*Eleven for eleven,* Vellan had said. *You've never missed.*

She had not. She had walked in, found it and taken it. The whole of it had been quiet and easy, and her skin crawled to the roots of her hair.

The door of the shed stood open, and nothing stood in it. She walked into the yard.

The rain had stopped. A smell came across the wet earth, and it was not resin. It was wet fur and old musk, and something that sat much lower in her mind than a smell had a right to, a long way down, where she kept being six.

The bear stood in the middle of the yard.

It was upright, which they seldom were. Its great black shoulders were roped with wet. The pale Y of its chest showed, and its head swung slowly to find her, and she could see that the claws on its forepaws were bright where the rain had washed something off them.

*It was in the wall that night,* she thought. *He ran to be here first.*

The bear did not charge. It stood and let her see it.

Behind it, a lamp opened. And another. Men came out of the long shed one after another into the yard, a dozen, with hooked poles and nets and iron rings on chains. Chathu's hunters. She knew three of them by name. She had eaten with Pachan at the long table when she was ten, and he had given her his sweet.

She had the knife out before she had decided anything. She took the first man at the wrist and the second at the knee. The third got the heel of her hand under the nose, then a marma point in the thick of the arm, and he dropped his pole. She was fast. She was always fast. But she had ridden a night and a day on bread and fever, and her hands were shaking, and a net came out of the dark behind her and dropped across her head like a sheet.

They pulled her down.

A knee found her back. Someone wrenched the knife out of her grip. Someone else tore at the fold of her shawl, and she heard herself make a sound as the vial came away, held up and turned in the lamplight.

"There it is," said Pachan. He did not sound unkind. "Master said she'd come for it."

*Master said.*

The bear dropped to all fours and went into the shadow of the long shed without a sound. It did not look back.

* * *

The kennels lay under the oldest roof, behind the cook-house and a shuttered well.

She had seen them from the outside all her childhood. She had been told, once, what they were for. She had not been inside.

They carried her in by the arms, with the net still tangled in her hair. A lamp burned at the far end, and the light went down a long aisle between cages. They were iron, floor to roof. Low, one beside another, with straw on the boards and a bucket in each. Hundreds of breaths moved in that dark. A deer lay curled in the nearest, with a hide gone patchy and its ribs showing. Beyond it was a hare, a mongoose, something striped. There was a fox, a very thin fox, that did not even lift its head.

Their eyes shone as she passed. They did not follow her. That was the worst of it. They looked at the lamp, and at the hunters' feet, and at the wall, as if they had once been looking for something and had not found it, and had forgotten what.

The hunters unlocked a cage near the end, put her inside, and dropped a leather cord over her wrists. They knotted it to a ring in the wall, high enough that she had to stand. Pachan tested the knot.

"I'm sorry, Seventeen," he said. He meant it, and it made no difference. They took the lamp, and its light went out of the aisle.

She waited until her eyes found the dark. Then she looked at the cage next to hers.

It was lower, and smaller. The straw had been changed that day. In it, curled with its nose against its own tail, lay a civet: grey-brown, long-bodied, with a dark mask across the face and a ringed tail. It breathed very slowly. It watched the corner of the cage, where nothing was.

Around its hind leg, worn smooth and thin, was a silver anklet with no bells.

She stopped breathing.

"Kirri," she said.

Nothing moved. The eyes did not shift. She said it again, a little louder, and then again in the voice from the yard behind the sheds, the one that had made a twelve-year-old girl who was far too clever for her own good sit up and say, *What?* The civet's ears turned a hair toward the sound. That was all. The ears turned back.

*Released.* She had lived twenty years on that word. Eleven contracts, and one more, and the page burned and a name given back. She had killed for that word. She had believed in it the way other people believed in rain.

Kirri had been the best liar in the House. Kirri had wanted a boat.

She hung there by her wrists in the dark, in the smell of straw and old fear and bitter resin, and said quietly to nobody, "Well. That's the price, then."

She counted the cages. Forty-one. She counted them again.

* * *

Chathu came at dawn.

She had not slept. Dawn reached the aisle through a high grille, grey and thin, and then came a step she would have known blind: soft, unhurried, a little uneven on the left foot where he had once broken it. The lamp that hung from his hand swung a slow circle on the straw.

He was an old man, round-shouldered, with a clean white mundu and a plain wooden comb in his hair. A big ledger was tucked under his arm, bound in black cloth, and in his free hand was a tin.

He stopped before her cage and looked at her a good while. His eyes were wet.

"Child," he said. "You look half dead."

"I rode a long way."

"I know. I'm sorry for it." He set the tin on the cage bar. "Jaggery and sesame. I had the cook make them warm. Eat."

She did not look at it. She had learned, around the fifth contract, what a gift was.

"You're not hungry? No. Never, when you're frightened." He sighed and sat on an upturned bucket, and drew the ledger across his knees, and laid his palm on the cloth the way another man would stroke a sleeping dog. "I want to tell you how it comes out, Seventeen. I find people are better for knowing. It is only a matter of accounts."

"Chathu—"

"You came for the antidote. It was well done. The lock, the shed, the quiet of it. You always had a hand." He nodded as at a good sum. "I made that vial, and I know how long it takes. Your husband will be dead by the end of tomorrow, and not before. At dusk, I will put the resin on you myself, as I did the first time. I will open this book to your leaf and read it aloud. Then you will be in the cage next to Kirri, and the next morning you will not remember very much of what went before."

He said it kindly. He said it as a physician might say that the bone had to be set.

"And Vellan will finish your warden after," he said. "Tonight. A short ride."

She had to stop before she could answer. Her voice, when it came, was hoarse. "You sold her. Kirri. You told her it was release."

"I told her what she needed to hear to do her work." He did not flinch. "And when she was tired, and the work had cost her more than she could pay, I gave her a place. Do you know what happens to an odiyan with no ledger? The crown hunts her. A rogue dies in a ditch with a rope round her neck, or is sold to a man who will break her. Here she is fed. She is kept dry. She is not afraid."

"She doesn't know her own name."

"No. But she is not afraid." He turned the ledger. "Twenty-one years I have kept you, Seventeen. Rice, oil, salve, teachers. A tutor for your letters. Do you know what you cost me, in all?"

"Chathu."

"A great deal. I have written it down. I weep when one of you is lost, and I mean it, and the book balances all the same." He touched the black cloth. "Without a leash you would all be hunted dogs. The ledger is mercy."

The old voice was gentle as ever. And she saw, for the first time, that it had never once been lying. He believed it. It was the worst thing about him. He had believed it at the table, when he gave her sweets. He believed it now, at the bars.

"The twelfth contract," she said. "Release."

"Is a lovely word." His smile was sad. "Eleven you did. The twelfth you failed. So you see how it stands. Release has always been a word for what comes after."

He rose, tucking the book under his arm. He lingered, looking down at her, and she thought for a moment that he would reach through the bars. He did not.

"You are thinking you were owned," he said. "You were. Of course you were. You are a clever girl, you have always known it." His head tilted. "But it comes to me you think this book is the whole of it."

She said nothing. She did not trust her voice.

"The ledger isn't the only book, child," said Chathu, softly. "It never has been."

He took up the lamp and went away down the aisle with his uneven step. The tin of sweets stayed on the cage bar, with the steam still lifting from the lid.

* * *

They came for her an hour later and bound her better.

Three hunters this time, and Pachan not among them. They took the soft cord from the ring and put on hard cord, wet leather that would shrink in the heat. They pinned her arms behind her at the elbows, then tied her wrists together and hauled the knot to a staple in the floor. They did it without hurry. They did not speak. One of them ran his thumb down the inside of her wrist, over the pale ridged scar there, and looked at it a moment as though it were a mark on a cheese.

Then they left her alone with the light climbing the grille.

*Dusk,* she thought. *Maybe ten hours.*

*Second dusk is tonight. Muthu will put him in a cart.*

That was the arithmetic, and it was no good. It brought a sick man to a yard full of hunters, a day too late. She worked at the knot at her wrists, quietly, until the leather bit. It held.

She had sat at the head of her life as a thing that did not need to be told. She had gone into houses and learned the name of every goat-boy, because a number was what the House had given her, and she would not do it to anyone else. She had let herself be sent. She had let herself be paid. And she had been proud, God help her, of how quiet she was.

Boots came across the yard outside, and a voice she knew. Quick and low and out of breath, with a hum in it. Not words. Only a tune.

*Pull and pull, the tide is coming in.*

The door at the end of the aisle opened. Light fell down the straw, and Ammu stood in it.

She was mud to the knees, in the plain brown cloth of Valli the pepper trader's girl. Her braid had half come down. One hunter had her by the elbow, though she was not struggling, and Chathu's voice somewhere behind her said, *Let her see.* She had been recalled. She had ridden all night, and her face was grey with it.

She looked down the aisle at the cages. At the fox. At the deer. At the small grey shape in its straw with the silver on its leg.

Then she looked at Neela.

Twenty years. Neela had seen that face laugh and lie, tease and steal and cry itself sick over a dead sparrow. She had seen it frightened. She had never seen it like this: still, and white, with its mouth shut hard over something it was not going to say.

Across all the bars, Ammu's eyes held hers, and did not look away.
