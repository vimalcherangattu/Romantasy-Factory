# Chapter 32: The Queen's Seal

The records room at Kettamala had no window, and the rain made it darker anyway. Devan lit a second lamp and shut the door on the clerk.

Ittan's effects lay on the long table in the order Bhaskaran's men had unpacked them. Two saddlebags. A writing case of tooled leather. Seven good shirts, which Janu had counted with disgust. A silver comb. A small brass box of clove oil, half used.

*Look through my things, my friend,* Ittan had said an hour ago, through the guardroom grille. He had been shaved. Someone had given him tea. *I am only a hand. Read what I carried, and then ask me again.* After that he had asked if his wife's cough medicine could be sent on, and said no more.

Devan took the tally book first. He had read it once already and read it again. Forty leaves of dated sums, pass place-names, tokens sold by the dozen to men with no names. Beside the sums, in the same tidy hand, were the prices of fire. A village on the coast side. A ford. The sums were small, and they were everywhere.

He set it down and opened the writing case.

The letter lay on top, unsealed, half finished. *To the physicians attending my wife in Kodiyur.* He read it standing.

*Boil the willow bark, do not steep it. She does not say when the cough is bad, so judge by the cup in the morning. If she asks for me, tell her the road is long and I am well. I send forty measures more with this rider. If it is not enough, write and say so, and do not tell her it was ever a question.*

Below that, in a shakier hand, a line scratched out and written again: *She likes the window open when it rains.*

Devan read it a second time. He had spent six years wanting this man's throat, and this was a husband's letter, specific and tender. It made no difference to what Ittan had done. Asha lay under the same stones regardless. But Devan noted it, as Neela would have noted a goat-boy's name, and set it aside with care. A man could do both. That was the worst of it.

Under the letters lay a flat bundle wrapped in oilcloth and tied with red thread.

He knew the thread. Asha's notes had been tied in it.

That was coincidence; red thread was cheap. He told himself so as he worked the knot. Inside were two papers.

The first was a strip of pale hide, the width of two fingers, stamped with a mark he had seen once on a seized courier's letter in a classroom twelve years back. A resin-ink bar and a circle. Beneath it, a brief line in a fine, careful hand: *Contract: the warden of Kettamala. In his chamber. By beast. Before the closing of the pass.* The signing mark was a stroke he did not need to be told. It was Chathu's.

The guild's chit. His own death, written down like a delivery of salt.

He laid it flat. His hand was quite steady. He had known for forty days that someone had bought this. Knowing it, and holding the receipt, were different things.

The second paper was a writ.

It was good cream paper, heavy as cloth, folded in thirds. *To the Resin House of the Blackbark Wood, in payment of the account of the warden of Kettamala, the sum of* and then a number in pepper weights that made him sit down on the edge of the table. It was dated seven weeks before the wedding. Underneath, a closed hand had signed with a flourish he did not recognise.

Beneath the signature was the seal.

Green wax. Not clove-sweet like the council's brick red, not the dull sprig of a tally. A small bird pressed into it, wings spread, the long curved beak of a sunbird.

He raised the writ to his face and breathed. Crushed leaf.

There was no need to compare. He did it anyway, because he was a man who measured. From his sleeve, wrapped in a scrap of linen, he drew the cut seal off the palace wedding gift. It had been in his sleeve since the sandalwood box came up the road with its silver betel set and its courteous note, and Janu had wanted the thing hung in the hall for the honour.

He laid the two side by side under the lamp.

Same wings. Same chip at the left tip, where the matrix had flawed. Same smell of leaf. The one on the writ was fuller and brighter, because nobody had cut that one in half to open a gift.

Devan did not move for some time. Rain ran in the gutters above. Somewhere a door shut.

He thought of the sandalwood box. The queen had sent it herself, with her blessing on the match. He had locked it in the granary because he distrusted the council. He had never once thought to distrust the gift.

*I proposed the bride,* Ittan had said, *out of vanity.* He had said it to Neela's face, genial, over wine. A hand. Only a hand.

Devan folded the chit and the writ into the oilcloth. He did not take them to Bhaskaran, or the clerk, or the strongbox. He did not put them in his sleeve to be thought about later. He tied the red thread round them and went out of the records room with the bundle in plain sight, up the wet stair, and the rain ran in under the arch.

He walked straight to Neela, and did not hide where he was going. It was the whole of what he had learned.

* * *

She was in the warden's chamber with a bowl of rice and the window open. Her hair was still damp from the wash-house. She looked up as the door opened and saw his face, and put the bowl down.

"Tell me," she said.

"Ittan said to read what he carried." He crossed the floor and set the bundle on the bed between them. "I did. I'd like you to read it too. Everything. I'll tell you what I think after."

She did not ask why. She untied the red thread and spread the oilcloth, and her fingers went still on the hide strip.

"That's his mark," she said. "That's Chathu's."

"It names the chamber."

"Yes." Her voice had gone very flat. She counted something. He saw her lips move, one, two, three, four, and then stop. "This is how it comes down to the shed. A strip like this with a mark. They give it to me at the stone, and I'm told to burn it." She touched the edge. "The House keeps the other half."

"This is the half the buyer holds."

"Ittan." She lifted the writ. She read it in silence, the number, the date. He watched her reach the foot of the page and he saw her smell it before she saw it. Her head came up. "That's leaf. That's not clove."

"Look at the seal."

She looked. She turned it to the lamp. He unwrapped the linen from the second piece and laid it beside it, and she bent so close her breath stirred the wax.

"The gift," she said.

"The gift."

"You kept it."

"I kept it because I thought it was too fine to throw away." He heard how odd that sounded. "The queen sealed the box with her own hand and the writ with her own hand. It's the same hand, Neela."

She said nothing. She laid both papers flat on the quilt, and he watched the thing arrive in her face. It did not come as a flinch. It came slowly, like a count run to its end.

"So it wasn't Ittan's money," she said. "He didn't buy you. He carried the purse."

"He carried it. He sealed nothing. She paid." He said the next part carefully. "Seven weeks before the wedding. After I wrote to the council that I'd found the first forged tally, and was close to the Keeper's office. Ittan wouldn't have needed to act on his own. He was told."

"Then the queen of Varanad bought a leopard to kill her own border warden." Neela's mouth twisted. "And sent you a betel set for the wedding."

"With her blessing."

"The sweets, Devan. The tin before every contract. Chathu said a gift is the start of a bill." She laughed once, short and horrible. "Look at it. She sent the gift, the bill, and the knife in the same month."

He reached across and took her hand. She was cold. For a moment they sat with the papers between them, and neither of them tried to make the thing smaller than it was.

"I told you at once," he said. "I could have kept it till I'd thought. I could have brought it to you after I had a plan."

"I noticed."

"A month ago I would have sat in that room until I'd decided how to carry it alone." He turned her hand over. "It's the most dangerous thing in the realm. I'd rather you were holding it with me."

Her fingers tightened on his. Then she did what he had learned she did, and sat back, and asked.

"The next question. If the crown paid the guild—" She tapped the writ with one nail. "What else does the queen own?"

He had asked himself the same thing in the records room and had not liked the answer.

"The passes. Ittan sold tokens on her council's wax. The raider scouts carried new Varanad silver, straight from the mint." He kept his voice level. "I thought a councillor had stolen the coin. I may have been wrong about what was stolen."

"And the House." She was staring at the sunbird. "Chathu told me in the kennels that the ledger wasn't the only book. I took it for a boast. I think he was warning me what he'd already sold." Her thumb found the welt on the other thumb, absent. "If she pays the guild, she can read the guild. If she can read the guild, she knows my number. She knows what Ammu was. She might know Kirri."

"She might know yours."

"She has since the writ." She almost smiled. "I was a line in a bill. Now I'm a line in her bill."

"You were never a line," he said. "You were the wife I kept."

"Don't be kind. I can't think when you're kind."

"I'll be exact then. You are the only person I have ever seen who could plan a fight against her."

That caught. Her eyes came up, dark and bright together, the look she wore when she counted exits and found one he hadn't.

"Who else has seen this?"

"No one. Ittan knows what's in the case. He told me where to look."

"Then he wanted you to have it." She frowned. "Why? A man doesn't hand over the crown's name unless he's afraid of it."

"He asked about his wife's cough medicine."

"He's afraid for his wife." She nodded, slowly. "Of course. Whatever he tells you, the queen will know he told. And she writes warm letters and signs the other kind." She bent over the papers. "We keep these where nobody finds them. Not Muthu. Not the strongbox."

"Not the granary."

"Not the granary." She nearly laughed again. "Under the third slat. It creaks, and a creak is a good watchman."

He took the strip and the writ, and she took the seal in its linen, and between them they fitted it all back inside the oilcloth. He tied the red thread. She tied it a second time, harder, as if the knot could close a door.

"A season ago," she said, "I'd have run."

"Would you?"

"I'd have been on the ledge before the ink was dry." She looked at the window, at the grey day. "I'm not running. I'm only making a list."

* * *

Nine days into the rains, the cart came back from the Resin House mud to the axle, and Muthu with it.

Neela was in the hall when he came in. She had been mending a bow string for no reason except to have her hands busy. The brazier hissed. Devan stood at the long table with a map of the wood road under his palm, and neither of them said what they had been thinking for nine days.

Muthu stood dripping. He did not take the stool Janu pushed at him.

"The cattle are fed," he said. "Water in the end cages. Clean straw." He looked at Neela and did not smile. "I laid the cloth in the civet's cage myself. She didn't move. Then she put her nose against it and lay down on it."

Her throat shut. "The anklet?"

"Still on. I didn't touch it."

"Thank you."

"Lady." He seemed about to say a great deal and said, "There was a man in the yard. The old master. He came out of the cook-house when the cart rolled in and stood on the step in the rain." Muthu rubbed his jaw. "He had a book under his arm, held to his chest like you'd hold a baby. Black cover. He never put it down. He watched us feed every cage, and when I looked at him he nodded, polite as a priest, and went back in. Pachan stood behind him with a pole."

"The ledger," Neela said.

"I don't know what it was. I know he didn't let go of it." He shifted. "I'll be at the stable."

He went. The door banged in the wind.

On the end of the table, where the map did not reach, lay a packet in plain grey wax. It was thick, and addressed in Muthu's cheerful hand to no name at all. It had been there since the morning of the vow, and it would lie there until the roads opened in four months and a rider took it south.

Neela looked at it. Then she looked at the strip of black beyond the open door, where the wood road ran down into the rain.

"Chathu's keeping every page where he can touch it," she said. "He knows we know where it is. He'll know the queen's paid his bill." She laid the bow down. "I'm going to take it, Devan."

He did not ask what. He did not tell her it was madness, or that the roads were shut, or that they would be two against a House of hunters. He studied the map as he studied a weather sign, with respect.

"If you take a book that makes you a master," he said. "Every page in it. Whoever holds it."

"I know what it makes me." Her voice was quiet. "I don't know yet how to hold it and not be it. But I'm going to take it."

"Then I'll come."

He said it as he had said *noon* and *yes*, with no weight at all. She felt the room settle. The grey packet sat on the table. Rain went on falling.

She took his hand off the map and held it. Four months, she thought, and began to count.
