# Chapter 22: A Night Without Resin

At dusk Neela opened the shutters herself.

The third slat creaked, as it always did. She let it. The west lay open below the sill, laterite red going to brown, the pepper terraces stepping down into shadow, the sun a hand's width above the ridge. The flask sat stoppered in its niche. She had not touched it all day.

Two nights without. The ache would count them both, the way Chathu counted a tin of sweets, and send the bill at the end of the moon.

Seven nights to the new moon. And on the second spur, in the grove, a split slab of laterite with a cup cut in its top. Ammu had promised to read that stone every night of her week of silence. The other one's scent-mark in the cup meant *still working*. An empty cup meant a woman who had stopped, or been stopped. Tonight the cup would be empty, and Ammu would stand in the dusk and wonder which of the bad answers was true.

Neela knew exactly what it would cost. She leaned on the sill and did it anyway.

The door opened behind her. She knew his step: heavy heel, then the pause where he checked the corridor. The pause came. Then it didn't finish.

"The shutters are open," Devan said.

"Yes."

"You haven't—"

"No resin." She did not turn. "Not tonight."

He came to stand a pace behind her. He did not touch her. The light lay red across the floor between them, and she heard him take the grey pebble from his pouch and set it on the shelf by the door, softly, like a man putting down a tool he would not need.

"Why?" he said. Then, because he was who he was: "I'm not refusing it. I'd like to understand it."

"A question for a question."

"Ask."

She turned round. "Do you want to be here? Not guard, not warden. Here, in this room, with a woman who can't turn into anything tonight."

"Yes." No pause. His ears had gone dark. "That one was easy."

"Then here is mine. I want one night when the only thing in this bed is a woman." Her voice was steadier than her hands. "I want to be looked at by lamplight and not run."

He lit the lamp himself. A little sesame oil, a wick, the flame leaning in the draught from the open window. He set it on the chest so that it lit her and not him, which was thoughtful, and a mistake. She crossed the floor and moved it to the shelf between them.

"Both of us," she said.

He nodded. For a moment neither knew what to do with their hands.

She was twenty-seven. He was thirty-two. She had eleven contracts behind her and a trained way of reading men in rooms. She had never once stood in lamplight and let a man read her back.

She unwound her day cloth. She did it slowly, because if she went fast she would stop.

The scars were the first thing the light found. Pale ridges across the throat, two bands on each inner wrist, raised and shiny where the resin had eaten. He had seen them in the dark, she knew, and in the grey before dawn. He had never been given them to look at.

He looked. He did not flinch and he did not soften his face into pity, and she was so grateful that her eyes stung.

"I'd like to touch them," he said. "I'm asking. You can say no and I'll still be glad of tonight."

"Which?"

"Whichever you'll give."

"Wrists," she said. "First. With your thumb. Nothing tight." She held out her right arm, palm up. "No one holds my wrists. Not once. Not even for play."

"No one will."

He took her hand as if it were something that could spill. His thumb came down on the inner ridge and traced it, the length of the band. His hands knew every pressure point on a body; she had watched him in the pit, and she knew what that thumb could do. She watched him choose, deliberately and visibly, to know none of it.

"Does it hurt still?" he asked.

"Not since the first year. Chathu held my wrist the first time. He said it would hurt less if I didn't pull." Her voice was dry. "It hurt exactly as much."

"A question," he said. "Why tell me?"

"Because you asked what the scars were, in the second week, and I said a fall from a cart." She held his eyes. "That was a lie. I'm tired of that one."

He lifted her wrist and put his mouth to the inside of it, to the worst of the ridges, so lightly that she only knew it by the warmth. Her whole arm tightened to the shoulder.

"Throat?" he said, against her skin.

"Last."

"Last, then."

She took his hand in both of hers and put it where she wanted it, flat against her ribs, and felt him breathe out. Then she unwound his mundu, because she wanted to see how he held still when she was the one deciding. He held still beautifully. He had a soldier's body, scarred along the left forearm, a puckered white seam at the ribs, the long muscle of a man who drilled alone before dawn every day for eight years. She put her palm against his chest and found his heart going hard.

"You're nervous," she said.

"I haven't done this since before the ford."

"Eight years."

"I'm out of practice." He said it gravely, and she laughed, and the laugh loosened something in both of them.

They went to the bed, and it was not graceful. His knee hit the frame. She had to move his sword, which lay on the floor on her side, hilt toward her as every night, and she slid it under the bed with her foot. "You can have it back after," she said, and he said, "I won't want it."

He was careful with her, almost too careful. He asked at every step and waited for each answer, and each time the waiting was its own caress. She had expected a man holding back. She found a man who had spent years teaching himself exactly what restraint was for, and had finally been handed a use for it.

"You can stop asking," she said at last, breathless, her back against the pillows. "Ask me with your hands. I'll tell you if it's wrong."

He took her at her word. His mouth moved down over her collarbone, her breast, the soft place under her ribs, and when his hand slid between her thighs, slowly, she put hers over it and showed him the pressure, the pace, the angle that made her catch her breath. He learned it like a drill. He did not drop a single detail. Every time she arched he said her name against her skin, the cover's name, which was hers now by use, and every time it undid her a little more.

"There," she said. "Don't change anything."

"I won't."

She came with her fist in the sheet and her heels dug into the mattress, quietly, in the end, with a low cry that surprised her. When she could see again he was watching her face with something close to awe, and she pulled him up by the shoulders.

"Now you," she said.

"You don't have to."

"Devan."

He came over her on his forearms and she guided him in, her hand on his hip, the angle hers. He went slowly. She felt the effort in him, the shaking just under the skin. She wrapped a leg round him and said, "More," and he said it was her choice, and she said that was exactly what she was making. He moved, and she moved with him, and the lamp threw their shadows huge on the wall, two bodies with nothing hidden in them.

His rhythm broke before hers, and he put his face into her neck, a hand's width from the scarred place, and stopped there, not touching it. She felt his breath on the ridges.

"Now," she said. "Please."

His mouth closed over the pale band at her throat, gently, and a long shudder went through her from scalp to heel. No one had ever been allowed to put anything there but resin. He kissed the scars one by one, as if reading a line he had to get right. She broke a second time with her hand tangled in his hair, and he followed her with her name in his mouth, and the shutters were open the whole time and nothing in the dark moved.

* * *

Afterwards he lay with his head on her shoulder and she traced the seam at his ribs.

"Ford?" she asked.

"The captain's knife. Before he knelt." His voice was slow. "A question. What's the count tonight?"

She smiled at the ceiling. "Two nights. The ache will want its due."

"Then I owe it."

"It's mine to pay."

"Not alone."

She did not answer that. She said, in a voice she barely knew, "Stay till morning."

He lifted his head. He knew what it asked. A man had never stayed while she was awake and unguarded, and a woman had never asked it. "Neela—"

"Please. I want to wake up here."

"I'll be here," he said.

He pulled the sheet over them both and did not put the stone in his mouth.

She woke once in the dark. The lamp had burned out. The shutters still stood open, and a cool wet wind moved across the sill, smelling of rain and laterite. Devan lay turned toward her, one hand open on the sheet between them, palm up, as if waiting. His breath hitched on its long count, the little catch a third of the way through, and she lay and listened to it.

Her nails ached at the quick. A dull, small warning.

*Two,* she thought. *Two nights.* She added the second one to the tally in her head and found she did not mind the sum. She laid her hand in his open one and slept.

* * *

An hour after first light she went up the second spur alone, in her day kasavu with the shawl over it, and found Ammu sitting by the stone with her back to the slab and her arms round her knees.

She was naked and grey and shaking. The turn-back had taken her at first light, an hour ago, and she had not moved since. Her cache cloth lay half-wrapped round her shoulders, her hands were scraped raw, and burrs and red dust clung in her hair.

In the cup of the stone, thick and fresh, lay a jackal's scent-mark. Not a reed. Only Ammu's own mark, laid in the dark and left to say *I came, and I looked.*

"You came," Ammu said. Her voice cracked on it. "I thought you'd got yourself killed."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be clever." But her grin was thin and bright. Neela sat down beside her, pulled a rice cake from her sleeve, and put it into Ammu's scraped hand. Ammu ate it in four bites. The hollow after a turning was the same for every one of them.

"Tell me," Neela said.

"Well." Ammu licked her thumb. "The watcher in the pass village doesn't count faces, chechi. He logs scent-posts. Every dawn he walks the hill edge and reads which of your marks have gone fresh in the night. From down there that's all he sees: the marks, going fresh along your route. No fresh marks, no one out on your account. A refusal. And a refusal gets written to Chathu."

"The rain night," Neela said.

"Already went down as a gap. A second one, back to back, is the one Chathu would act on." Ammu tipped her head. "So I ran yours."

"Yours is the stone."

"Mine is the stone. And then I ran yours: the east wall, the terraces, the sentry's walk." Her smile was shaky. "Two routes, one jackal. I put my nose to every post you touch and laid mine over it, so they read fresh at dawn. I was right under the rampart when the guard changed, with the wind wrong, and a boy with a lantern nearly trod on my tail."

"Ammu."

"I know." She leaned her head on Neela's shoulder. "It was reckless. I'm good at reckless. You always said so." The hum started, small and absent, the fishing song. She stopped it herself. "The shutters were open. I saw from the grove. A lamp, and two people behind it, and nobody on the ledge." She said it without heat. "I'm not going to say anything about that, today. I'll be too hungry."

Neela said nothing. Warm and wrong, the lie she had told on the last dawn lay between them.

"I got you something," Ammu said. She reached under the cloth, to her waistband, and drew out a folded scrap of paper gone soft with sweat. "I shouldn't. If Chathu knew I'd torn it off the carrier's satchel he'd cage me before he asked why. It came up with the last cart. Accounts. He sends the carrier a balance sheet for every open contract so the man knows who to dun. I tore the strip clean off the foot of it."

Neela unfolded it. Chathu's neat hand, resin-ink, tidy as a ledger:

*Balance due from the Keeper on the widow's seal.*

Nothing else. Not a name. She read it twice, and the cold came up through the soles of her feet.

*The widow's seal.* The charter line. If the warden of Kettamala died married, his wife held the pass-seal in trust until the council named another. Chathu's plans had made her that widow a dozen times over. The balance was due from the man who would come to take the seal from her hand.

Not the council. Not the queen. *The Keeper.*

The Keeper of the Passes. The office that held one of the three dot-and-split seal boxes. The office that sent the kind letter every season for six years and never once climbed the hill.

"Chechi?" Ammu said. "You've gone the colour of ash."

"It's nothing."

"It's not nothing. It's the first time you've ever lied to me badly."

Neela folded the slip along its creases. She did it slowly, and she put it inside her bodice, against her skin, where the heat of her body could not hide how heavy it was.

*Ittan.* The name came up in her like a bell. Genial Ittan, my friend, the man who had proposed the match.

Below the spur Kettamala stood red in the early sun, and in the warden's window, a small dark square, the shutters were still open. Somebody was in the pit under the east wall, drilling alone. She knew the shape of him at that distance, and the way he stopped in the middle of a form and glanced up at the hill.

She had the name of the man who had bought his death. She had it in her hand, in her bodice, and he had said he would not ask.

Ammu was watching her, fierce and starving. Neela put the second rice cake into her fingers and said nothing at all.
