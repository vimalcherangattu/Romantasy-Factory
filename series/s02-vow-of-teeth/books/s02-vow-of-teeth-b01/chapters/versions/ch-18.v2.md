# Chapter 18: Kirri's Anklet

Ammu was already at the meeting stone, sitting on it with her heels drumming the laterite, and she did not look like a girl who had come to chat.

Neela climbed the last of the second spur at the slow pace of a trader's ward gathering wild pepper leaf. She had told Muthu she was going down to Parukutty's, and she had gone, and the child's cough was real. Then she had walked the long way round, up through the pala trees, with a basket on her hip and a count running in her head. Sixteen nights. Her nails had begun to itch at the quick, the way they did before the ache.

"You're late, chechi," said Ammu. "And thin."

"And you're loud."

"Nobody's here but monkeys." But Ammu dropped her voice anyway. Her cover was a coast girl's blue cotton and a tray of cheap bangles, and under it she looked sharp as a split reed. Her eyes went over Neela's wrists, her throat, the grey under her eyes. "Three nights, the cup was bare. Four, if I count the night of the fever. I came to the gate yesterday and the gate said you were abed. I thought, she's dead, or she's in love, and I couldn't decide which one I minded more."

"Neither."

"Chathu minds neither. He minds the days." Ammu pulled a twist of paper from her sleeve, read it with her lips moving, and tucked it away again. "He sent word with the last cart. Sixteen nights and the account is open. Every day counts double now, and he is *patient*, and you should remember what patience costs. Then he sent the sweets."

She held out a small tin. Neela knew its smell before it opened: warm jaggery and sesame. Eleven times it had come before a job. She took it and put it in the basket without looking.

"Not hungry?"

"Not for his."

Ammu laughed, too fast. Then she began to hum, low and tuneless, *pull and pull, the tide is coming in,* and stopped when she heard herself.

"You only hum that when you're frightened," Neela said.

"I hum when I'm happy."

"You haven't been happy since you were nine."

Ammu picked at a chip in the stone. For a moment the quick talk ran out, and what was under it showed, thin and tired.

"Kirri swore," she said. "Do you remember? The night before they released her. She said, *I'll have my boat by the first monsoon, and the first thing off it is my anklet, in a fish-basket, so you'll know it's real.* A little silver anklet with the bells cut off, the one she never took off, not to bathe. She'd send it to us. A sign." She looked up. "Two years, chechi. I've written eleven times to the address she gave. Nothing. Not a note, not a bad one. Nobody could be that busy with a boat."

"Messages get lost."

"Hers don't. She was the best liar in the House. She could send a letter through a fire." Ammu's voice cracked and she bit it back to something light. "Maybe she's fat and happy and forgotten us. Maybe she's sitting on a beach laughing. That's what I tell myself. Then I ask Chathu, and he says, *Kirri is where she wished to be, child,* and pats my hand."

Neela felt the cold run down her arms. She had been given the same pat. Eleven times, with tins.

"Did he say where?"

"That's it. He never says where." Ammu shook herself. "Never mind. Release is a good thing. It's what we're here for. Twelve contracts and your page burns, and then there's you with a boat, and me after, and no one with a word in his mouth. So. Tell me you're close."

This was the moment. Neela had known it was coming since the jackal call, and she had still built nothing. She stood with the basket on her hip and looked at the one face in the world that had ever known all of her.

*Devan has seen me turn. Devan has watched me for twenty days and held his tongue.*

Ammu would have it out of her in a breath, because it was Ammu, and then Ammu would do the sensible thing. She had been raised to it. A second witness in the world was not a gift. It was a hole in the wall, and holes were stopped.

"I'm close," said Neela.

"How close?"

"The pass shuts in sixteen days. I know his guards, his hours, the shutter slats. He has a bad arm and a worse temper."

"That's the fort. I asked about him." Ammu tipped her head. "Chinna at the gate says the bride was seen at the window at dawn in her sleeping cloth. So why three bare nights, chechi? Why human, with him, in the bed?"

"To learn his sleep."

"You can learn a man's sleep from a window ledge."

"Not his waking." Neela made her voice dry and bored. "He wakes at the middle watch to walk the wall, and goes back. I wanted the pattern. If I'd smeared up every night I'd have missed it."

It was a good lie because it was nearly true. She watched it go into Ammu's face and watched Ammu turn it over, the way they'd both been taught.

"And he hasn't noticed anything?"

"What would he notice?"

"A woman who goes out at dusk and comes back at dawn with fur in her hair."

"He sleeps like a log."

"Does he." Ammu leaned in. "Then who told you the hounds were kennelled? Who shut the shutters? Valli hears the whole fort talking. Chinna has wagers."

"A man with a fear of damp." Neela shifted the basket. "Why are you asking me this? Do you think I'm lying to you?"

It was a thing she had learned at eleven, how to turn a question like a blade. She hated how easily it came.

"I think," said Ammu slowly, "that you've never once asked me why I'm asking."

"Why are you asking?"

"Because I'm scared." It came out flat and fast. "Because three bare nights is how you start to be caught. Because if anyone but us has seen you, if one person in that fort has watched you turn, then every night you sleep beside him is a night he can say it." She caught Neela's wrist. Her fingers were cold. "We're safe as long as nobody but us has seen. That's all. That's the whole of it. It has kept us alive twenty years. Tell me nobody's seen."

Neela looked at the hand on her wrist, at the scars on the inside of Ammu's, pale and ridged like her own.

She could not argue with it. That was the cruelty. It was true. It had been true when two orphans hid in the sheds, and when Vellan's bear-shape stood over them in the yard, and when other girls went into the kennels. Nobody but them had seen, and so nobody had said the word. Ammu had never spoken hers in eleven years, and Neela had spent every one of them living on that silence.

"Nobody's seen," she said.

The lie had a taste. It was exactly like the taste of the resin on a bad night. Bitter, and then burning, and then you went on.

Ammu's grip loosened. She let out a long breath and laughed at herself, and the laugh was real, which was the worst of it.

"Good. *Good.* I'm a fool. I've been sat in an inn for three weeks with nothing to do but think." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand. "Leave me a mark in the cup tomorrow night. Anything. A stone. I'll know."

"I'll try."

"Don't try, chechi. Do." She slid down off the stone. Then, light again, a bangle seller once more: "That's a pretty gold bangle. Is it real?"

"It's real."

"Keep it." Ammu was already going, down through the pala trees, a quick blue shape between the trunks. At the bend she hummed three notes of the fishing song and stopped.

Neela stood a long time by the stone with the sweets tin in her basket. Where was Kirri? She turned the question over and found nothing under it, only the small silver sound of bells that had been cut off.

* * *

By evening the rain had gone, and the hall at Kettamala smelled of wet cloth and frying onions and lamp oil.

Janu had commanded it. "Nobody's done anything with their hands but the sword for a month," she told the long room, rapping a ladle on a pot. "Hands want something else. Seven wells, and I'll have the new lady learn it."

Neela had expected to endure it. She had learned long ago to sit out a household's games with a polite face, counting the doors. But the hall was loud, and warm, and the old woman had a way of ordering people around that was so plainly love that it was hard to stand outside it.

The board was a plank scratched with seven small hollows in a ring. Each player took seven grey pebbles from a bowl. You tossed them one by one at the wells from an arm's length, and the first to seat all seven won, but only a pebble that struck a wall of the hollow and *then* dropped counted, and you could knock a rival's stone out with a good flick.

"Skill," Janu said. "And a clean conscience. Cheats get a rap on the knuckle."

"Has anyone ever been rapped?" Neela asked.

"Every one of them. Muthu most."

"Slander," said Muthu, from the bench, with the big laugh already coming.

Across the board sat Devan. He had his bad arm propped on his knee and a face of grave attention, as if the game were a council. He took his seven pebbles from the bowl and set them in a neat row.

"You've played this before," said Neela.

"Once. I was eight." He considered a stone. "Asha beat me."

It was the first time he had said her name in the hall. A silence began, and Janu's ladle stopped. Then he flicked his pebble and it clipped the rim of the first well, spun, and dropped. "She cheated," he added, in his flat voice.

"She did *not*," said Janu, outraged, and the silence broke like a plate.

The game ran. Neela threw badly at first, on purpose, to learn the board. Then she saw it: the plank rocked a finger's width on its trestle if you leaned your elbow on the near edge. A pebble thrown in time with the rock dropped dead into the well. It was a trick for a cheap fairground. She did not even decide to do it. Her hand simply went.

Three wells. Four. Muthu groaned. Unni, who had been drafted from the gate, cheered with his mouth full.

Five. She lined up the sixth and leaned her elbow.

"Your elbow," said Devan.

"I'm resting it."

"On the plank." His eyes had not moved from her face. They were bright, and for a moment she did not recognise what was in them. "You lean when the board rocks. You've done it four times. I have been counting."

"Counting what?"

"Your elbow." He leaned forward, and she saw the corner of his mouth go, and keep going. "It's very good."

The hall had gone still to listen. Janu had a hand over her heart. Neela put the sixth pebble down in its well with great dignity and said, "I don't know what you mean."

"Then you won't mind if I throw."

He took his own seven. He did not lean on the plank. He leaned on the *trestle*, with his knee, at the far leg, and the whole board went up on its side by a hair. His pebble hit the wall of the well and dropped. Then the next. Then he flicked a third at her sixth and knocked it, cleanly, out of its hollow and into the rushes.

Somewhere a pot lid rattled on its pot and settled. Nobody breathed over it.

"That," said Neela, "is a foul."

"It's a rule. Hands want something else."

She stared at him. He was grinning. Not the corner of the mouth, not the slow dry thing she had learned to read. A real grin, wide and boyish and astonished at itself, as though it had been stored in a drawer for eight years and had come out creased.

She laughed. It came up before she could stop it, a rough peal, and she put her hand over her mouth, and then took it away and let it go.

The hall went up like dry thatch. Muthu roared. He banged the bench with both fists and bellowed that he had *seen* it, he had seen the warden *cheat*, and he wanted it written in the chronicle. Unni was crying with laughter. Janu thumped Devan on the shoulder with the ladle, hard, and he took it with his eyes shut, shaking. Somewhere behind, Chinna's shrill voice was calling for the wager to be settled.

"Rapped," Janu declared. "Knuckles, both of you."

Neela held out her hand. Devan held out his. The ladle came down on each in turn with a flat wooden clack, like a shutter slat dropping.

Under the noise, across the board, he turned his hand over and showed her the red stripe across his knuckles. He did not say anything. She turned hers up beside it, the same stripe, a finger's width lower, and he looked from one to the other as if they had matched a lock.

She had never had this. Not once, in eleven contracts and twenty-seven years. A room full of people, and no one counting the exits.

She was still smiling when she looked down and saw her bangle shining in the lamplight, and thought of Ammu's cold hand.

* * *

Later, in the chamber, she sat on the chest with the shutters open on the dark.

He had gone down to see to the wall. She had said good night to a dozen people by name. Janu was Janu. Raiu was Raiu. The goat-boy had given her a pebble to keep, white as a tooth. She turned it in her fingers now.

The laugh was still in her chest like a coal. It was warm, and she did not trust it.

She did what she always did. She wrote it down in her head.

*Today I told Ammu nobody has seen me. I have told her small lies before, to keep us out of trouble. This is the first I have told her to keep someone else safe.*

It sat in the ledger of her mind in a neat, small hand. She looked at the line beneath it, the one she did not want to write.

*Tonight I laughed in a hall with a man who has seen me. That was the first time I have ever laughed for nothing.*

She read the two lines together.

They were not two debts. They were one. The lie to Ammu had bought the laugh, as plainly as coin buys bread. If she had told the truth at the stone, there would have been no hall, only a cold plan and a quiet knife. And she had not been forced. She had chosen it, on a hillside, with Ammu's hand on her wrist, for the sake of a man she was paid to kill.

*What does it cost,* said the quiet voice, *the second time?*

She did not know. She set the pebble on the sill beside the bangle's reflection and sat in the dark until the cistern chain clanked, and the debt was still there, and she could not make herself wish the laugh away.
