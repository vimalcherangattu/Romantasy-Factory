# Chapter 8: The Unlit Lamp

"Leave it dark," Devan said.

Raiu stopped with the taper half raised. The corridor lamp hung in its iron bracket across from the chamber door, a clay lamp with a fresh wick and a full belly of oil, filled every dusk since the wedding. The boy looked at it, then at the warden, then at it again.

"Dark, Warden?"

"It saves oil."

"Janu fills it herself. She'll say—"

"She'll say I never counted oil in my life. She'll be right, and it will still be dark."

Raiu's mouth worked on that. He was nineteen, with a cowlick no water had ever beaten, and Devan had needed four months and two askings to learn his name. He had not lost it since. "Where do I stand, then?"

"Head of the stair. If I want you, you'll hear me."

"And the lady, if she wakes and wants a light?"

"The lady has a five-cup lamp at her window and a husband with a flint." Devan took the taper from his hand, pinched the flame out between finger and thumb, and gave it back. "Go on."

The boy went, wondering. Devan stood in the blue passage and listened to the fort settle. The chain clanked the hour at the cistern. A pot rang in the kitchen.

It did not save oil. A lit lamp laid a gold bar under the door and across the floor, and at first light that bar would reach the window corner, where a woman came over the window ledge shaking, with nothing on her, to find her clothes. At the watch change a guard with a lit lamp would also try the latch, as guards did. Devan wanted no lamp near that door at that hour. Not Raiu's, nor Raiu's shadow stirring in it.

He had lain through every dawn since the wedding with his eyes shut and his breath counted. He had learned the sound of her coming in, which was almost none.

Inside, Neela stood at the window with a taper of her own, trimming the bird lamp's five wicks. She did not turn.

"Raiu's gone from the passage."

"I sent him to the stair."

"Why?"

"So the passage stays dark."

She looked at him over her shoulder, head a little on one side, and he watched her take the answer and set it down with the others. There was a row of them by now. He knew she was keeping them. He did not know what she thought the row added up to.

"Then mine can keep it company," she said, and pinched out the wick.

* * *

Muthu came to the hall with the morning meal still on his chin.

They had the end of the long table to themselves. On a slate Devan had laid two squares of beeswax, each warmed the night before and pressed over a seal. One came from the tally in the dead raider's satchel. One came from a true tally out of Bhaskaran's gate chest. Beside them lay a stack of old council tallies, a year of brick-red wax that smelled of cloves.

"Pepper sprig," said Muthu, squinting. "I've seen a sprig."

"Look at the stem."

Muthu looked. Devan watched him go from cheerful to attentive. It was a quick road, and one reason he had made him captain.

"The council keeps seven tally seals," Devan said. "Each is cut with a private mark at the stem, so a clerk can tell whose box a tally left. Four have a bar. Three have a dot with a split under it." He set a fingertip on the forged impression. "This is a dot with a split."

"So it's one of three boxes."

"Or a copy of one."

"A copy's hard. The dies were cut by the palace engraver, and he's older than the queen." Muthu sat back. A laugh started low in his chest and came out big. "Chief. Come. You know what the treasury clerks are. They'd press a tally for a cousin and take a measure of arrack for the trouble. Some boy lent his master's seal to oblige a friend, and the friend sold it on. It's carelessness. The whole council runs on it. I once saw a minister sign a writ on the back of a fish."

"The wax is a month old and poured with clove oil."

"Then a careful careless clerk."

"Careless doesn't deliver to Thurai."

The laugh ran down. Muthu turned a square of beeswax over with one thick finger. "Three boxes," he said. "That's a lot of councillors to insult on a guess."

"It isn't a guess."

"No." He slid the slate back, gently. "Who else knows?"

"You."

"Then I'm the lucky one." He grinned, a shade late. "I'll keep it behind my teeth. Who would I tell?"

It was the only odd note in the whole talk, and too small to hold. Muthu liked to talk, and no man likes to hear that his masters might be thieves. Devan put it by.

"Four days to the seventh day," Muthu said, rising. "One round in the pit. I'll let you win."

"No."

"Eight years of no. One day I'll catch you on a yes."

"Eat something," Devan said, which was how he said *thank you for asking*. Muthu wiped his chin and went out whistling.

Devan stayed with the wax. Three seals. Three councillors, or three people with keys. Not one name yet, but a bar thinner than the fort wall had been that morning. He began to put the squares away in a cloth and found he was thinking about a bride.

* * *

At noon the sun stood straight over Kettamala and the red stone gave the heat back. Devan sat in the shade of the parapet's turn with his sword across his knees and watched the pass. A cart crawled up it. On the far cliff the langurs argued, loud and unconcerned. When strangers came, they would go quiet. They were not quiet.

"Janu says you haven't eaten."

Neela came along the walk with a covered brass dish held out in front of her like a thing that might go off. She wore the day kasavu from her trousseau, the gold border mended with small neat stitches where she had torn it for Kannan's hand.

"She said it to me, to Chinna, to the goat," she went on. "I came to see if you were alive."

"I am."

"I'll tell her. She'll be disappointed." She set the dish on the stone between them and sat on the parapet, a yard off, with her feet drawn up. Rice, fish, a green mash he didn't know. He ate. She watched him do it as she watched everything, the way a woman watches a lock.

"Can I ask something?"

"You will."

"Why do you never laugh first?" She tipped her head toward the yard below. "Muthu laughs at everything. Janu laughs at her own scolding. At supper someone says something funny and everyone looks at you, and you wait."

"I wait to learn whether the joke is at someone's expense."

"And then?"

"Then I know whether to laugh."

"That isn't a reason. That's a ledger."

"It's both."

She looked at him a moment. Something was moving behind her face, quick and wary, and then it put on a different look, almost sly. "Then here is one at mine," she said. "Safe to laugh at. I have a signed letter from the Chirayil house that says I can haggle a lamp-oil man down to his last thumb. Yesterday I won a thumb. Today Kannan's goat looked me in the eye, took a bite out of my sleeve, and I paid her in full. Nine generations of pepper traders, undone by an animal named after the stock."

For a breath he only looked at her.

Then it came up out of him, short and rough from disuse, and he heard it ring off the laterite. He put the back of his hand to his mouth. It went on a little behind it.

She was staring. Not guarded, not counting. She looked as if she had found a coin in a coat she had thought was empty.

"That," she said, "is the first time."

"It's the second."

"When was the first?"

"A long time before you." He lowered his hand. "I'll tell you the goat bit hard, though. I've seen her take a man's boot."

"You're laughing again."

"I won't answer that."

"Your ears are."

Asha had laughed first at everything. She had laughed at the quartermaster, at a bad bridge, at Devan himself, and then looked round to see who was in on it. Since the west stair he had waited, and waited, and been very sure about whose expense it was at. He had not noticed how long it had been since he laughed at a thing because it was funny.

And he had been given hers once already, by theft. He thought of the ledge in the grey, and a sneezing ginger tom, and a woman shaking with silent laughter while he lay with his teeth in his lip and his breath gone wrong. He had hidden then. Now he could sit in the sun and be answered.

She ate a piece of fish from the dish with her fingers, and he saw the question come up in her, and saw her weigh it. The lower market. A man in a plain brown coat at the foot of a lane, with no escort and no sword. He had stood in the shade of the tamarind wall for the better part of a quarter hour before Muthu's laugh drew every eye and gave him cover to walk in. He had watched a girl with a pepper scale weigh the same measure twice. He had watched his wife listen with her back like a drawn string.

The business with the salt factor had been real. It had also been the second reason.

If she asked, he would say, *I won't answer that.* It was the truth, and she would hear it as a door.

She wiped her fingers on her sleeve. Her mouth opened, and she closed it again.

"Is that all the green stuff?" she said.

"It's all of it."

"Janu will ask."

"Tell her I ate it."

"Did you?"

"I will now." He took the dish, and neither of them said anything about anything else. He thought it was the kindest silence anyone had given him in years, and the most dangerous.

* * *

Unni came up the steps two at a time, red in the face, with a leather case held against his chest in both arms.

"Warden! Rider from Kodiyur. Registry blue. He wouldn't climb, said his orders were the gate, so I said I'd carry it, and he said sealed and into your hand and no one else's, and I said I'm *a* hand—" He ran out of breath.

"Well done," said Devan, and took the case.

The seal on the cord was council wax, dull brick red, the pepper sprig pressed clean. He broke it with his thumb. Inside was a single folded sheet of registry paper, thick and creamy, covered in a clerk's even black hand. The marriage writ, back from the palace record with the registrar's stamp at the foot.

Neela had come to stand at his shoulder, as a wife would. He tilted the page so she could see. He had never hidden a page from her, and he would not begin with one that carried her name.

It was a long writ and a dull one. Her ward-name, Chirayil, and the house's witnesses. His own title. The date of the sunset rite. The settlement of cloth, brass and four hundred panam, and the usual sentence about the wife's duty to the household. He read down it without hurry, as one reads something one has already seen done.

Near the foot, in the same flat clerk's ink, ran a clause he stopped on.

*...and the said wife, being lawfully wed, shall hold the pass-seal of Kettamala in trust from the hour of the warden's death, until the council shall name another in his place.*

He read it a second time.

The custom, as he had always known it, sent the seal to the captain of the fort when a warden died. There was an old line in the charter that sent it to a wife instead, if the warden had one. No warden had married in office in living memory. He had never given the line a thought, as a man gives no thought to a door in a house where he lives alone. Now it stood in black ink with the registrar's stamp under it. He was married, and a stranger would keep the seal.

A stranger. Chosen by the council. A ward from a house nobody at Kettamala had met, who had come up the hill with a bangle and a lamp and a quiet manner. The council whose wax sold passes to Thurai had pressed this match. They had pressed it in a hurry, and were pleased with themselves. The chain ran in his head: the council, the match, the widow, and the coast girl who had weighed twice.

If he died, the seal did not go to Muthu. It did not go to Bhaskaran. It went to whoever slept beside him.

The cold of it ran down his arms. He had thought of the marriage as a door the council had left open for a knife. He had not thought that the marriage might itself be the thing they wanted done. A wife who was an odiyan, sent into his bed. A dead warden with leopard marks and a widow in black, calm, with the pass-seal in her lap. A widow could be steered. A widow could be set aside, afterwards, with no one the wiser.

And the woman at his shoulder either knew it, or she did not. If she did not, she was a blade who had never been told whose hand she was in. If she did, then he had laughed beside her on a hot wall an hour ago, and she had let him.

He could not make himself believe the second. He noticed that he could not, and noted it as he would have noted a weak place in the wall. Either way, this was a person who had to stay alive and near. Dead, she told no one anything. Alive, she knew the name of the hand that held her.

Unni shifted his feet, waiting. A langur barked and was answered. Devan raised his eyes from the page to say something light, so that she would not see him read it a third time.

Neela was looking at the writ. Her face had gone still, as water goes still just before it takes a shape. And for the first time since the wedding, she was not watching him at all, only the page, and he could not tell whether she was reading it or remembering it.
