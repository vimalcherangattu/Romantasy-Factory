# Chapter 4: Kennelled

"They've shut the hounds up again," Kannan said.

He said it to the goat. Pepper went on chewing and watched Neela with one yellow eye, like a small judge.

Neela had climbed to the second terrace with a bowl of Janu's rice. The hollow in her belly would not let her sit still. Besides, a goat-boy on a wall saw everything that moved in a fort. She sat on the laterite beside him and held out the bowl. He took a fistful without looking, like a boy who has been fed by a steward all his life.

"Which hounds?" she said.

"All of them. Fourteen." He wiped his hand on his knee. "They sleep in the long kennel behind the stables. Can you hear them? Every night since your wedding. They howl till the middle watch, and my loft is right over that wall."

"They didn't howl before?"

"They weren't *in* before. They ran the yard from dusk to dawn. Since forever. Nobody crossed it in the dark unless the hounds knew his smell." He scowled. "Then the warden says, kennel them. Chinna asked why. He said, 'I like it quiet.' Quiet! In a fort!"

"Maybe he sleeps badly."

"Janu says he's slept badly eight years, and the hounds ran anyway."

Neela ate the rest of the rice slowly. Her face stayed polite. Behind it she was already counting.

She had crossed that yard last night. Sixty paces of open ground between the padippura and the stables, pale as a threshing floor, and nothing in it. She had noted it the way she noted everything, and had been glad. She had not asked why. Now a nine-year-old with a goat had told her it had never been empty before.

So. A gift, or a trap.

A gift, because a man who barred his window against clear sky was a man of small, odd rules. Perhaps he truly wanted quiet. Perhaps he was a husband with a new wife and a poor night's sleep, and the hounds were one more noise. Careless men did leave doors open. She had walked through a hundred of them.

But this man wasn't careless. She had watched him pick up berries one at a time. A warden with a bride the council had chosen, and tokens leaking through his pass, might very well empty a yard on purpose. Then he would put men on the wall to see who walked into it.

She could argue it either way until the chain clanked noon. She tried. It came out even.

Both readings ended in the same place, which was in the yard, with her eyes open.

"Are you going to eat that?" Kannan asked, looking at the bowl.

"No," she said, and gave it to the goat.

* * *

The hooves came an hour later.

Gopan hauled his lever, the chain went over the drum, and a patrol clattered through the padippura in a spray of red mud. Seven riders. Neela was at the hall door before she'd decided to be, with Janu hissing behind her about wives who ought to be sitting down.

The hall was long and cool, with pillars of black teak and a floor of polished red earth. The riders came in with their boots still dripping. The sergeant at the front was Bhaskaran, who held the gate and had ridden out at dawn when a herdsman swore he'd seen men at the second ford. He had a satchel in his fist, stiff with dried blood, and he slammed it onto the long table hard enough to jump the lamps.

"Raider, sir. At the ford. One of his friends put an arrow in him, and we found what he was carrying."

Devan had come in from the east door. He didn't hurry. He took the satchel by its strap, turned it out, and picked through what fell: a knife, a twist of dried fish, a coil of cord. Then a flat tally the size of a palm, strung on a leather loop and capped with a thumb of dull red wax.

Captain Muthu had come up beside him, big and easy, with his arms folded. "Another one?"

"Another one."

Devan held it up. The hall went quiet in a way she knew from the Resin House, the quiet of people waiting to be told how bad it was. He set his thumbnail under the wax and pressed. It split cleanly into two halves. He turned each to the window.

"The wax is genuine," he said. "Council wax. Council seal, pressed from the true ring."

Somewhere behind her a lamp wick spat.

"It isn't copied," he said, in the same flat voice. "I'd know. This was made in the council house, by someone with the right to make it."

Then the hall broke open.

"A clerk," said Muthu, loudly. "Some wax-house clerk with a bad debt and a wife."

"A Thurai spy in the council," said a young guard near the door. "They've got them everywhere."

"A lord," said Bhaskaran. "It's always a lord. A man who never has to stand at the gate and look into a carter's face." His voice cracked on *face*.

Neela, behind her pillar, made her own wager. She didn't believe in clerks. A clerk made a mark; a clerk could be caught making it. This had a hand behind it that nobody searched. She watched who said nothing, and it was the warden.

Bhaskaran saw it too. "The tally number," he said, and his voice dropped, and that was worse. "Sir. I know this number. It's a cart I passed. New moon, a salt-and-cloth cart from the coast. I looked at the wax. I *looked* at it, and I waved them through. I'll ride to Kodiyur myself and open the whole council with my sword."

"Bhaskaran," Devan said.

"Sir, I will."

"Sit."

Bhaskaran didn't. Two of his men had already taken a half step toward him. He was a big man, red in the face, and his hand had found his hilt. Neela slid her weight onto the balls of her feet without meaning to.

Devan didn't raise his voice. He came around the table and stopped an arm's length from the sergeant. He put one palm flat on the tabletop, where Bhaskaran could see it, empty.

"You looked at the wax," he said. "It was true wax. You were meant to pass it. That's what the wax is for."

"Sir..."

"If you'd turned that cart away, I'd have asked you why. You'd have said, it looked wrong. It didn't look wrong. It was perfect." He waited. "That's the whole trouble. Do you see it?"

The sergeant's chest went in and out. "Yes, sir."

"I need every tally that has passed your gate since the last rains. Numbers, carts, faces, whatever you remember. You're the only man who can give me that. Will you?"

The hand came off the hilt. "Yes."

"Then sit and eat first. You smell of the ford."

A thin laugh went round. Bhaskaran dropped onto the bench like a sack set down at the end of a long road, and Janu was already there with a cup. The whole thing had taken less time than it takes to boil water.

Neela noticed that her pulse had gone quiet, the way it did when a fight ended before it began. She had watched men handle men at the Resin House, but they used the rod, or the ledger, or the threat of both. This one had used the truth and a free hand.

Her own hand lay against the black teak of the pillar, palm flat, fingers open and empty.

She looked at it. It was his hand, the one he'd laid on the table for Bhaskaran, and she had made it without being told. She took it off the wood and tucked it into the fold of her kasavu.

"Neela."

She started. He was looking straight at her across the length of the hall, and so was everyone else.

"Your house trades pepper under these tallies. Come and tell me if this wax is what it looks like."

It was a test, or it was courtesy, and the face he wore didn't say which. She crossed the red floor with the polite smile on and took the two halves from his palm. They were warm from his hand.

Dull brick red. A pressed mark of a pepper sprig, crisp at every leaf. She raised one half to her nose, because a Chirayil ward would, and sweet clove rose off it, faint and green.

"It's new," she said. "A month old, not more. The wax-house pours with clove oil so it doesn't crack in the damp. It's still in the wax." She turned the half over. "And it's good work. Better than good."

"That's what I thought," Devan said.

He took the halves back. For a moment his fingers were against her palm, dry and rough at the pads. Then they weren't. She went back to her pillar with her hand held very still.

* * *

She caught him on the outer stair, where the hall's shadow ended and the court began, and fell into step beside him as if she had been going the same way.

"Your sergeant thinks it was his fault."

"He thinks wrongly."

"You told him so."

"I told him the truth. It's easier than arguing." He didn't slow. "Your nose is good."

"My guardian taught me to judge a pepper by smell before I could read." That was a line she had learned in the Resin House, with the rest of Chirayil. It came out smooth.

"Useful."

They walked a few steps. Below them the court was full of men leading in tired horses. She let a pause go by and then asked it plainly, as a wife might.

"Who sells them?"

He glanced at her. It wasn't suspicion. It was more like a man who has been asked the weather and is surprised anyone cares.

"Someone on the queen's council."

"Which one?"

"If I knew," Devan said, "he'd be in a cell."

She waited for the rest. For the turn: *and that's why you're here*, or *and I've reason to think you can help*. It didn't come. He was looking down at the horses.

"The tokens go to Thurai raiders," he said. "So far they burn grain stores and a mill or two. Soon it will be the villages on the coast side. Your house's carts will want the pass open in harvest. I mean to see it stays that way."

"That's all?"

"It's a great deal." There was dry humour in it, very low. "I would sleep better."

She had come out of the hall braced for her own name. A husband holding a hostile bride might circle the subject of her for weeks; she had planned for that. Instead he had told her his hunt in six sentences, and her name wasn't in any of them. He wasn't hunting her. He was hunting somebody else, and he wanted him badly, and it had never crossed his mind to use her as a lever.

That made him easier to listen to, which was a bad sign.

"I hope you find him," she said, and was startled to find she meant something by it.

"So do I," said Devan, and went down into the court.

* * *

He barred the shutters at dusk. "Rain," he said again, and again the sky was washed clean.

She said nothing. She watched his back and set the two things side by side.

Everything else he told her fit the world. The wax was true, the sky was blue, the cell was waiting for a man he couldn't name. When he couldn't answer, he said so and kept his hands where she could see them. Only here did he give her a word that answered nothing. Rain. It sounded like a forecast, every night, at this window, under a sky with no cloud in it. And only here did he hand her a reason that didn't fit the sky.

Hounds shut up for a bride. Shutters shut for weather. Both were about the dark, and the dark was hers.

She did not know what it meant. A man who held back one thing so plainly had a reason, and she did not like that she wanted to know it. She ate her rice, counted his breath to three hundred, and stepped over the sword.

The bar came up in her hands. The third slat cracked. She counted twenty and he gave her nothing to hear.

The burn. The bright rush of smells. The other one went down the wall on silent claws.

The yard lay open below.

It did not hurry. It took the terrace face in three drops and came to the corner of the stables, where the kennel smell rolled out thick: dog, straw, wet fur, fourteen angry noses turned toward it through the planks. They knew it was there. They could do nothing about it. One began to whine and the others took it up, a low grumble, and a guard at the far wall turned his head, and turned it back again. Dogs in a kennel at night were only dogs.

The yard stretched out silver and flat. The leopard crossed it low and slow.

It learned the padippura from the shadow of the water trough. Four men, and one with his chin on his chest. It went past the open end of the guard room close enough to touch the doorpost. It learned the kitchen door, where Chinna snored on his mat, and the granary, whose lock was a farm lock, and the well. It learned that the pairs on the wall met at the same stone and turned the same foot. Two men stood the west stair, as Devan had said. There was a gap, a count of thirty, under the far wall when the lower pair swung inward.

The cistern chain went. The third clank after the middle watch, and the pairs changed, and the yard took no notice of it at all.

This was the easiest fort it had ever walked. That was what lifted the fur along its spine. Nothing had stopped it. In all its life nothing had left it alone without wanting a price.

It circled back, past the cold stones of the kalari rim, toward the west wall. At the corner the wall pair parted. One man went on along the far walk with his lantern. The other turned back and paced the short stretch of rampart above the warden's window, alone, up and down, up and down. It went flat in the shadow and let him pass.

Then it lifted its head and looked up.

The window was a dark square in the laterite, one shutter leaf drawn back where it had left it. The bar stood against the wall inside. Nothing showed through the gap, but the smell of him came down the stone: sesame oil, old iron, sleep. And on the still air, the hitch in his breath.

It gathered itself. The wall was thirty feet, an easy climb. The ledge was wide. The sleeper was close enough that it could count that breath.

And above the ledge the sentry stopped. He leaned on the parapet and looked out at nothing, and his boots were two spans over its head. When he moved, the lantern light swung over the ledge like a hand. A shape that went in at that window would be seen going. It would be shouted at before the teeth closed, and the whole wall would run. A beast out of the dark was a thing the fort could blame on the hills. A beast that walked into the warden's room under a sentry's eye was a hunt, and a sealed gate, and a skin on a pole.

The sentry coughed and went on. The moment stayed, wide open, and so did the window.

There was a cheaper way. It could wait a night, until the post above was bare, and take the throat in the quiet. The pass left thirty-seven nights, and she would be spending one of them on nothing.

The other one did not weigh the nights. It weighed the man above, whose words had been plain with it all day, and who had never once thrown the weight of a hunt at his wife. *Who is he hunting?* It had no words for the question. There was only the shape of the wanting, and the long cold climb that did not begin.

For the first time since the wedding she was not certain of the answer she'd come to give.

It turned from the window and went down into the shadow of the wall. It lay under the water trough until the sentry's boots found their old beat, and watched the dark square, and spent the night on nothing.
