# Chapter 5: The Man Who Doesn't Spar

She had come back shaking again.

Devan had lain with his eyes shut and listened to it: the bar lifted, the slat cracking, the long breath of someone who had run all night and was trying not to show it. Then the bar set home, and a hand that missed the wood twice before it found it. He had counted her breaths until she slept. Then he had gone out to the kalari before the light, because that was what he did, and a man who did something else on the fourth morning of a marriage would be noticed.

Three nights. She had gone out each one, and each one she had come back. Not once had she come to the bed.

He did not know what that meant. He lit the sesame lamps around the rim one at a time and let the question sit in his chest like a swallowed stone.

The first form was slow. Feet in the red earth, weight low, the long staff turning over his shoulder. The second was faster. By the fifth the earth was spitting under his heels and the staff made a sound like cloth tearing. He drilled the way he had for eight years, against nobody. Every sequence ended in a strike, and every strike stopped a hand's width from the post. He had never once let it land. His arm knew exactly where to stop.

"Warden!"

Muthu came down the pit wall in two jumps with a practice staff in each fist, grinning. Red dust puffed under his boots.

"One round," he said. "One. I'll let you win. I'll let you win so loudly Gopan will ring the chain."

"No."

"I'll fight left-handed."

"No."

"I'll fight asleep." Muthu spun a staff and caught it. "Eight years, sir. I've counted. Every week I ask, and every week you say no, and it's the steadiest thing in Kettamala. The walls leak. The pass closes. But my warden says no at dawn on the seventh day, like a god in a temple."

"Eight years and a week."

"Is that all? I thought it was longer." Muthu laughed, the big laugh that made the pigeons go up. "At this rate I'll ask from my pyre. Burn me, Devan, I'll say, one round first. And you'll say no, and the whole fort will weep."

It was a good joke. It was the same joke, dressed a little differently each week. Devan watched Muthu's hand on the staff. The knuckles had gone pale, and he held the grip a long beat past the laugh.

Muthu had been his partner in this pit once. He had taken every bruise Devan gave him and asked for more. Then one day there had been a captain on his knees with his hands open, and a strike, and a silence. Devan had put down his staff and not lifted it against a living man since.

He couldn't give Muthu the reason. He had never given it to anyone.

"Put the staffs on the rack," he said.

"Sir."

"And thank you for asking."

Muthu's face did something quick and unguarded. Then the grin came back over it like a lid. "Next week," he said, and went up the wall, whistling a little off key.

Devan stood in the empty pit with the second lamp guttering and finished the form. The strike stopped a hand's width from the post.

* * *

After the day watch changed, he took her out to the terraces, because a warden walking his wife through his pepper was the most natural thing in the world. It also kept her where he could see her hands.

She wore a day kasavu from her trousseau and the gold bangle. She took the steps up the slope without looking at them. A woman who counted her feet, he thought, and pushed the thought away, because he had decided to look at her as the Chirayil ward the council had sent.

"Tell me what you see," he said, at the second terrace.

She didn't hesitate. She picked a string of berries and rolled one between finger and thumb. "Early. The vines are confused. They've set fruit for a rain that's still a month off." She pressed the berry with a nail. "Hard. Good. These will dry heavy. Drop a handful in water and the sinkers are the ones worth the weight." She looked along the row. "You've let the east side go too dense. They'll mould if the monsoon comes early."

"Janu said the same."

"Then Janu's right. Is she often?"

"Always. It's tiresome." He walked on. "Your guardian. What was his name?"

"Kunjan Chirayil." She said it smoothly. "He kept the north warehouse in Kodiyur. Do you know it?"

"No."

"You'd smell it from the water. Wet sacks, and cloves from the Thurai side. He'd stand in the door and tell you the grade of a cargo with his eyes shut." A small shrug. "It's what he was good at."

Devan had read the council's papers on the Chirayil house three times, and nothing in them was false. Kunjan Chirayil was real. The warehouse was real. She spoke of both the way a person speaks of a place they have been taught.

"Were you fond of him?"

"Were you fond of yours?"

"My father?" He considered it honestly. "I respected him. Fondness came later, after he was dead and couldn't object."

"Did that make it easier?"

"No. Did it make it easier for you? With Kunjan?"

"Do you miss the hills?" she said. "Janu says you were away once."

"Two years, at the border school. Where did you learn your letters?"

"Where did you learn to be so patient?"

A cart creaked somewhere below. He nodded as though she had given him a fair answer, and filed it with the others. Ask her what a pepper weighs and she would tell him to the grain. Ask her where she slept as a child, and a question came back at him, light and quick and always shaped like a door closing.

It was neat work. It was also the first time all morning she had sounded like herself.

She reached up to pull a runner free of its stake, and the gold bangle slid back along her forearm.

Under it, the skin was pale and ridged, in rows, like a field the plough had gone over too many times. He knew the pattern. He had smelled a sealed jar of the stuff at twenty, in a classroom, while a lean teacher described exactly what it did to the skin of those who used it. The burn had a signature. He had never thought to see it on a living wrist.

He had seen the scars at the window on the wedding night, before the turning. He had not known how much it would cost her.

She noticed his eyes, and the bangle slid down in one smooth motion.

"A childhood burn," she said, lightly. "I was clumsy."

It was no child's burn, and they both knew it. He said, "The stakes want mending on this row," and went to look at them.

She let out a breath behind him. He didn't turn around.

* * *

The yelp came from above them, and then a boy's furious wail.

Kannan sat on the third wall with his right hand clamped in his left, and the white goat stood beside him, looking mildly satisfied. Blood ran between the boy's fingers and dripped off his wrist onto the laterite.

"She bit me!"

"She does that," Devan said. "It's in the name."

Neela was already there. She hadn't run, exactly, but she had crossed the terrace in the time it took him to start. She knelt, took the boy's wrist, and turned the hand to the light.

"It's clean," she said. "A good bite is cleaner than a bad scratch. Hold still." She looked up. "Warden, there's a water pot on the wall."

He brought it. She rinsed the wound with the flat of her hand under it, steady, murmuring to the boy about nothing. When it ran clear she pulled the gold-bordered edge of her kasavu through her fingers and tore a strip off it in one motion. The cloth was trousseau cloth, gold thread and all, worth a month of the boy's keep.

She bound the hand with quick, firm turns and tied it off.

"Tell Bindu to wash it again tonight with boiled water," she said. "And to sing while she does it. She has a good voice and you'll forget the sting."

Kannan stared at her. "How do you know my mother sings?"

"The women at the well were talking about it this morning." She tapped his nose. "Keep Pepper off the vines. She's eating the best of them."

"She isn't," said the boy, who was nine, and incapable of lying about a goat. "Well. Only the low ones."

Devan stood with the water pot in his hand and did not move.

Asha had done this. She had come to the fort at nineteen, and in her first month she had gone through the recruits with a slate and a bit of chalk, writing down names and villages and mothers. Everybody's. He had teased her. *You'll be the only second in the border guard who can address a conscript's grandmother.* She had looked at him, grinning, chalk on her chin. *They'll fight better if someone knows who to write to.*

He set the water pot on the wall. He said, "I have to see Bhaskaran about the gate," and walked down the terrace steps without looking back.

It was not true. The wall by the cistern was near enough that he could stand there, palm flat on the cold laterite, until the chain went and covered the sound of his own breathing.

An instrument did not tear a wedding-gift cloth for a goat-boy.

He had been so sure what she was. The thing that had been sent. The thing like the one that had come down his west stair on a night six years gone, and taken his sister apart with a bear's claws, and left her there for Janu to find in the morning.

He did not know what to do with a person.

* * *

Supper was rice and a dark fish curry, and Janu served it herself and sat down at the end of the table to scold them while they ate. Muthu had come in from the gate and taken the bench opposite, heaping his plate. The hall smelled of tamarind and lamp oil.

"The old woman at the lower ford says there's a cat on the hill," Janu said.

"A cat?" said Muthu, with his mouth full.

"A big one. She heard it cough in the pala grove." Janu pulled her shawl straight, enjoying herself. "It reminded me. When I was a girl, a leopard came down to the village below my mother's house. Every night it took something. First a hen, then a kid. Then the headman's calf, and it left the bell, mind you, neatly, on the doorstep, as if to say it had no use for the noise. The men sat up with spears for a week and never saw it once. They said it knew which house it wanted. It was patient. It waited till the lamps were out, and—"

Devan was not looking at Janu.

Across the table, Neela held her cup with both hands. She had been drinking water, steadily, in small sips. Now her hands were round it as if she were warming them, and the knuckles had gone white as the rim.

Her face hadn't changed. It was the pleasant, attentive face of a bride listening to an old woman's story. But the cup trembled by the width of a hair, and she pressed it to the table to stop it.

He knew that stillness. He had seen it a hundred times on the faces of recruits who were told the thing they feared was coming.

*Janu, enough.* The words were in his mouth. She would have stopped at once, and sniffed, and looked at him with the hurt he had never been able to bear.

He did not say them.

Neela did not know he had seen her turn. It was the one thing he held that she did not, and he had spent four days guarding it. A warden who cut off a leopard story for his bride's sake would be telling her, as plainly as speech, that he had joined the two in his head. She would know it by morning. Every night after, she would run with that knowledge at her back.

So he sat, and let the old woman tell it, and watched the white knuckles, and ate rice he could not taste. He wanted to stop it for her. He didn't. It was the first thing he had paid for her sake, and she would never know the price.

"Is it true?" Neela said, when Janu ended. Her voice was light. "Did you see it?"

"Heavens, no. I was a girl in bed with the shutters closed." Janu patted her hand. "Don't mind me. It's a story."

"I like stories," said Neela, and set down the cup.

She excused herself soon after. Devan watched her cross the hall, back straight, steps even, and counted the times she looked at the doors. Two. The west door and the east.

* * *

He let Muthu finish his meal. Then he walked him out under the arch of the hall, where the torchlight did not reach the corner and the pigeons had gone to roost.

"Captain."

"Sir."

"Take the night man off the west wall. The one who walks the rampart above my window."

Muthu's chewing stopped. "Off it? That's the closest stretch to your chamber, sir."

"I know."

"The raiders have been at the ford. Sir, with respect, if any wall wants a man on it—"

"I know," Devan said again. "Pull him."

Muthu looked at him in the dark. The cheerfulness was gone for a moment, as if something had been set down. "Any reason I should give the sergeant?"

"I won't answer that."

A pause. Then the big shoulders came down, and Muthu clapped him once on the arm, like a man shaking off a thought. "Then I'll tell him you've got a bad stomach and want the quiet," he said. "That'll keep the barracks busy till harvest."

"Tell him whatever you like."

"Sir." Muthu hesitated. "You'd say, wouldn't you. If it was bad."

"If I could."

It was the nearest thing to a lie he allowed himself, and it was not one. He watched Muthu go down the steps toward the barracks, and then he climbed to the chamber.

Neela was not in it yet. The bed was made. The sword lay on the floor on her side, hilt toward the pillow, plain wire grip worn at the thumb. He stood looking at the shutters.

He knew what he was doing. Whoever had sent her would want a report, and she would have to make it to someone, and a woman who ran the hills at night had somewhere to run to. Pull the sentry and she might try for him. Or she might go to find someone to speak to. Either way he would see where the line led.

And if a lantern swung over a leopard on the ledge, a man on the rampart would shout, and the fort would run for its spears. He did not want her shot before he knew who held her leash. That was only sense. It was only sense, and he told himself so twice.

He turned the lamp low. The teak bar lay across the shutters where it lay every dusk, and he did not touch it. Lifting it for her would break the one ritual she had watched him keep for four nights, and a woman who counted everything would count that. She would lift it herself, as she had three nights running. The only thing he had changed was the wall above.

He lay down with his back to the door and shut his eyes. In the dark he counted the gap of thirty under the far wall, and then counted it again.
