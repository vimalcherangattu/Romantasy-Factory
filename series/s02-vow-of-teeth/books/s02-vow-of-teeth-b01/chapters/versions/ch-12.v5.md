# Chapter 12: Parrots

Devan put his hand to the back of his neck. The skin was still warm where hers had been.

She had had him.

He made himself say it plainly, the way he would write a report. She had come down the north steps with her hands empty. She had laid two fingers on the door of his skull, at the exact point, with her weight set behind the wrist. He had felt that weight gather. Three breaths. He had counted them, because counting was the only way not to turn round.

Then she had lifted her hand and told him his left was dropping.

It was. Alone in the pit with the lamps burning out, he had checked four times since, and she was right to the half-palm.

The body had decided to stand still, and his mind had come up behind it and found itself holding a very bad wager. He could not say if he had been testing her. He could not say if he had wanted to be caught out.

For ten days he had watched her as a thing the guild had left in his bed. A blade with a woman's hands. A blade does not pull a strike. A blade has no mind to change at the last. He had looked at the woman on the ledge at dusk and thought only of the cat, and of the one word in his mouth that would close her in it for good.

But a thing that stops has a will. A will can be asked questions. A will can be bargained with.

He had one thing she needed, and it sat behind his teeth. She had one thing he needed, and it sat in her head: who had paid. He weighed it as he would a blade he did not like to carry. A man who held a word over a woman and called it a trade was doing what the guild did. He knew that.

He weighed it anyway.

* * *

Janu was waiting in the kitchen passage, which she never did. She had a key on a cord in her fist, and she held it out the way she held out a cup to a child she suspected of being feverish.

"Asha's room," she said.

He did not take it at once. "Why now?"

"Because there is a wife in this house, and a house with a wife shouldn't keep a shut room like a grudge." Her mouth worked. "And because I can't be the one. I tried. Twice. I got to the door."

He took the key. The cord was warm from her hand.

"Open the shutters first," said Janu. "It's been dark in there since the rains she died. She never liked a dark room."

She walked back to her pots without looking at him, and the pots took a beating.

* * *

The room was at the end of the south passage, the last door before the wall. It smelled of dust and camphor and mice.

Devan opened the shutters. Light fell across a cot, a stool and a peg with a leather cap still hanging from it. He did not look at the cap. At the foot of the cot stood her chest, with a brass hasp gone green.

He sat on the floor in front of it. It seemed the only honest way to do it.

On top lay a slate. He knew it before he picked it up. Her first month as an officer of the fort she had written the name of every recruit on it, with his mother's village beside, because she said a man fights harder for a captain who knows where his mother lives. The chalk had faded to ghosts. She had given even the cook's boy who ran water to the recruits a line. *Unni, salt fish, lower village.* He read the ghost twice. The same boy, grown thin and tall, now held his gate.

Under the slate were notes. Forty or fifty, folded small, tied up with red thread. He slipped the thread and opened the top one, and there at the bottom was the bird.

She had signed every note with it. Five strokes: a round head, a hooked beak, one lazy tail. As a girl she had drawn it on the walls of the cistern house until Janu scrubbed them. The beak was always whole.

His thumb stopped on the beak.

He read three of them. Reminders. A joke about Bhaskaran's boots. A list of what the garrison owed the baker. Then, in the bottom corner of the chest, wrapped in a square of oilcloth tied with the same red thread, he found the other thing.

A tally stick, split ash, notched for a cart. At its end a thumb of old wax.

He did not touch the wax at first. He held the stick by the oilcloth and studied it in the light from the shutters. Dull brick red. A pepper sprig pressed in it. He lifted it to his nose, and under the dust there was still clove.

Council wax. And folded into the oilcloth, a scrap in her quick hand:

*First one. Not yet. Need a second to be sure. Tell nobody at the gate. —* and the bird.

He had never known she had it. He had thought the first token lost, taken by whoever had taken everything else.

From the pouch at his belt he drew the square of beeswax he carried everywhere now, the impression he had lifted from the dead raider's tally. He laid it beside hers in the light. Under the stem of the sprig, a dot with a small split beneath it. Then the other, the same dot, the same split.

One of three seals in the council's keeping, then and now. Whoever held it had been selling the pass while she was alive, and was selling it still.

He sat very still. The cistern chain began to clank the hour below, far off, as if in another house.

* * *

"May I come in?"

She stood in the doorway with her hand flat on the frame, as she had stood at the lip of the pit. She was grey about the mouth. The hollow-eyed look had not left her since dawn.

"Janu said you'd be here," said Neela. "She said not to bring anything, and then she sent a plate. I left it in the passage." A pause. "I'll go if you say."

Devan folded the oilcloth over the tally and put the whole in his sleeve. It was not a lie. She had not asked.

"Come in."

She crossed the room and did not sit on the stool. She lowered herself to the floor opposite him, with the chest between them, and looked at the cap on the peg and the faded slate.

"Is this Asha's?"

"Asha's. My sister." He said it as flatly as a ration count. "Four years younger. My second, after."

"Janu said you'd lost someone."

"Janu says a great deal."

"So I've heard." The corner of her mouth moved and went still again. "Tell me about her. Or don't."

He had not said her name aloud in that room since the rains she died. He found it did not break anything.

"She was fearless with soldiers," he said. "Forty men on a muddy field, and she'd give the order like a woman asking for salt, and they'd walk into a ford for her. She learned every recruit's mother's village in a month." He touched the slate. "She was afraid of the dark. Not of anything in it. Of the dark. She'd stand a night watch on the wall with a hundred spears behind her, then come in and ask Janu to leave a lamp."

"Everyone's afraid of something."

"Yes."

Neela's hand went out to the slate and stopped above it. Then one finger came down on the ghost of Unni's name.

"I do this too," she said, to the chalk. "Raiu has a cowlick. Kannan's goat bites. Chinna's hip is worse when it rains." Her thumb moved along the line, and it was not steady. "I don't like anyone on my wall to be a number. I was one, once. For a while."

She did not look up. Her thumb stayed where it was, as if the chalk might hold her.

He had watched her do it for ten days. The pause at the kitchen door until Chinna looked round. Raiu's name before his rank. Kannan's goat. He had taken it for a bride's manners, or a spy's craft. It was neither. It had cost her something to say, and she had said it to a slate rather than to him, which was the only way she could.

"She'd have liked that," he said. "She'd have said you were cheating, and then she'd have copied you."

Neela made a small sound that was nearly a laugh. Her thumb lifted.

"What happened to her?"

"She was killed. Six years ago. Under contract."

He watched the words reach her. She did not move. It was a stillness he knew, because he had seen it in the pit this morning, the stillness of someone adding up a figure she did not want. He took it for what it looked like. A woman who had been sold into a house and had just learned that a wall in it had once been breached.

"Someone paid for it," she said, slowly.

"Someone did."

"How?"

"I won't answer that."

In his head, where it stayed, were the west stair at first light, and Janu's face when she came up from it. The long parallel wounds, deep and spaced wide. A bear's. Inside walls no bear had ever climbed.

Neela looked at him, and then she did what he had not expected. She left it alone. She did not press and she did not offer comfort she did not mean. She sat with her hands between her knees and let the question lie where he had put it, and the room did not feel emptier for it.

"May I?" She nodded at the notes.

"She wrote them to be read aloud."

She unfolded the nearest and tilted it to the light. Her eyes went down it. Then the thumb of her right hand came to rest on the signature, and stayed.

"She gives it a beak," she said.

"She always did. The one on the west stair has lost its own." He heard his voice go careful. "She said it was an insult to a bird, and drew it mended."

Neela's face emptied. The colour went out of it so fast that he half rose.

"You haven't eaten," he said.

"No. It's nothing. It's the morning." She waved the hand with the paper in it. "It's all right. Let me read."

She read. He sat back down.

Her lips moved on the last line, and then she laughed. It was a short, startled sound, surprised out of her, nothing like the dry noises she made at table. She put her fist against her mouth and said through it, "Oh, she's *good*."

"Which?"

"'Muthu says you're the best fighter in the pass. I told him you're the best at looking like one. I'll take your two best points on the seventh day, brother, and you may have my two worst.'"

Nobody had laughed in that room since she died. Devan put his own hand over his mouth, and under it his face did something he could not have stopped if he had tried. He let it. She had her head down over the paper and did not look.

"Did she win?" asked Neela.

"That seventh day? She took both points."

"Of course she did."

She folded the note on its old creases and set it back with the others, squared, the way a person puts away something that is not theirs. Then she asked, still looking at the thread.

"What would she want now?"

He could have said something pretty. He had a dozen.

"She'd want whoever sold the tokens found," he said. "Not hanged in a hurry. Found, and asked why. And she'd say I was doing it the slow way, alone, in the dark, because I'd decided no one else could carry it." He looked at the faded slate. "She'd be right. She was usually right. It was hard to live beside."

"Hard to live beside," Neela agreed, gravely, and he laughed again, once, through his nose.

They sat there. After some time he noticed that he had moved. He was no longer across the chest from her. His knee was nearly touching hers on the boards, and the chest stood at his shoulder, and he could not remember crossing the floor.

He did not move back. He was less alone in the room than he had been when he opened it.

* * *

At dusk he went down to the padippura to count the watch.

The light had gone orange and low. In the gate yard a rider sat his horse with the palace green on his sleeve, Sudhi, who brought the royal post up every ninth day and took the fort's down. Muthu stood at his stirrup with a letter in his hand.

It was a plain fold, sealed in grey wax with no mark on it.

Muthu saw him coming across the cobbles. For half a breath he said nothing at all. Then the great laugh came out of him, the one that carried to the wall.

"My mother!" He held up the letter. "Don't look at me like that, Warden, it's the usual quarrel. She writes me once a month, I answer once a month, and every time she says her son will die of the pass, unfed and unmarried and unlettered. I'm the first captain in Varanad to be scolded by post."

"Unmarried?" said Devan.

"She hasn't heard about *yours*. I'll let her find out from the neighbours. Better a rumour than a letter." Muthu clapped the rider's leg. "Take it, Sudhi, she'll want it before the rains."

The rider tucked it into his satchel with the palace's own post and clicked his tongue to the horse.

Devan stood a moment. A plain seal, no mark, at the gate. *Tell nobody at the gate*, Asha had written, and her note lay in his sleeve against the sight in front of him. He made himself set the two side by side.

Muthu lied quiet. Devan had known it since they were boys in the pit. When the man had something to hide he went still as a stone in a stream, and this had been the loudest laugh on the wall. The half-breath beforehand was a man embarrassed over his mother's scolding, nothing more.

And the wax was grey. The wax on Asha's tally was brick red. Grey was not council wax, and this letter was going to an old woman in Kodiyur.

"Tell her I'm sorry about the pass," said Devan.

"I'll tell her you're a hard man to feed." Muthu grinned. "Go and eat something."

He believed him. He went up.

* * *

The chamber was empty. Neela was at supper in the hall, with Janu piling a plate in front of her; he could hear Janu's voice through the floor.

The shutters stood open on a last green strip of sky. He took the oilcloth from his sleeve and unfolded it. The tally lay in his palm. Beside it lay the last note from the chest, the one he had kept back and not shown her. He had found it folded under the camphor block at the very bottom, and it was not about boots.

*If you're reading this, I was right, and it's ugly. Say it anyway. The truth protects whoever finds it.* The bird, with its whole beak.

He read the line three times in the going light. It had not protected her. She had found it alone, in a fort that was too small for her, and told nobody at the gate, and it had come up the west stair after her in the dark.

He knew the shape of the offer now. On one side, his silence. On the other, the name of whoever had bought his death. Nothing more, and no lie in it.

The light went. Below, Janu's girls were lighting the evening lamps.

He folded the note into the oilcloth beside the tally and knelt to set both in the carved box under the bed. The boards were cold under his palm. He thought of the floor in Asha's room, and the place beside his own knee where hers had been, a hand's width away, unasked. Once he named the bargain, she would not sit like that again. She would sit across a table from a man who held a word over her, and she would be right to.

He laid the lid down. Then he lifted the bar, set it across the brackets, and let it go. The wood knocked once, softly.

He would offer her the bargain. Once, and plainly, and knowing what it was.
