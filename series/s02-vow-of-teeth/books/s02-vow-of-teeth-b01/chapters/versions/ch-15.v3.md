# Chapter 15: First Storm

The rain took the trail in the time it took to blink.

One breath, the other one had it: pony sweat, lamp oil, wet wool, two men and a pack animal threading the ridge track north of the narrows, a hundred paces ahead and in no hurry. The next breath the sky split from edge to edge and the water came down all at once. The smell went out of the world. Red mud, wet stone, torn leaf. Everything smelled of rain, which meant nothing smelled of anything.

She stood in the track and turned a full circle with her nose to the ground. Nothing.

Eight nights she had worked this ridge. Eight nights of lying in thorn above the cut, watching the beyond for whoever waited there with the other half of a bronze mirror's answer. Tonight, at last, the couriers had come out of the dark, a lamp hooded in a shirt, a pony stepping soft. They carried tokens. They had to. She had meant to follow them to the hand that took delivery.

Thunder cracked directly overhead. The other one flattened to the stones with her ears back and her whole hide twitching, and Neela, behind the animal's eyes, said the only dry thing she could think of.

*Well. That is the monsoon, then.*

She cast along the ridge, wide and then narrow. A hoofprint filled with water as she looked at it. Then another, and both were only holes. Frogs started up in every ditch at once, a screaming chorus. The pass would close with this. Not tonight, not this week, but the sky had said its first word, and she had twenty nights left to hear the rest.

She gave up the trail. It cost her something to do it. She turned for home.

Then came the question of the hour, and she found she had nothing to measure it with. No stars. No moon. The cloud sat on the hills like a lid, and the dark it made was the same dark whether it was the second watch or the fifth. The animal had never kept time. Her own body kept it for her, a slow dull ache in the bones, and tonight the ache was drowned in cold.

Two hours of night left, she decided. Perhaps a little more.

Her cache lay under the second spur, in the cleft where she had hidden a cloth and a gourd. It was a long climb the wrong way up the slope, and by now the cleft would be a gutter running red. The fort wall was nearer. The ledge was nearer. Two hours was plenty for the ledge, she told herself, and she ran for it.

It was a long way. Down the ridge to the cut, across the scree on feet that skidded, along the foot of the cliff where the shelf ran, and the water ran with her, ankle-deep and loud. Once she went down on her side in a gully, and the cold slapped the breath out of her. She heaved up and ran on. The fort came out of the weather as a darker block on dark. Laterite walls. A bar of weak yellow where the padippura lamp burned.

By the lantana she felt it. Not the ache now. A pull, low in the throat. A drag at the bones of her forelegs, as if someone far off had taken hold of a rope tied to her.

*No*, said Neela. *Not yet. Not yet.*

First light did not ask. Behind the cloud, past the east hills, the sun had come up on its ordinary schedule, and under the rain the sky had gone from black to the colour of wet slate. She had misjudged the hour by one, and then by more. The mistake was hers, and the spur was now a world away.

She went up the terraces low, as fast as the slope allowed. The pepper vines slapped her face. A goat shouted from the shed. Pepper, the white one, who bit. On the parapet above, the ginger tom sat out the storm under an overhang with his notched ear flat, and watched her go up the wall with deep personal contempt.

The change came on in earnest at the foot of the wall. She climbed it with her claws going soft on the stone.

* * *

The ledge was a hand's width from her when her front paw became a hand.

She felt it go. The pad flattened and the claws drew in with a tearing ache, the knuckles lengthening, and she was clinging to the lip of the sill by fingers that were not quite fingers. Fur fell off her in wet clumps and stuck to the stone. The spine was the worst. It unbent from the middle, one bone at a time, so that she was half a cat and half a woman on the ledge outside the warden's window. She could not do this part faster. She could only get in out of the rain.

The shutter stood open a hand's width, as she had left it. The third slat gave its little creak when she shouldered it wide.

She got her chest over the sill and her head up, gasping, and saw the room.

A lamp was lit. That was the first wrong thing. It was turned low on the chest at the foot of the bed, throwing a small yellow room inside the larger grey one. The second wrong thing was the bed. The quilt lay smooth, unslept. And on the edge of it, his boots flat on the boards and his forearms on his knees, sat Devan, fully dressed.

His eyes were open.

Not through the lashes. Not the slow breath of a sleeper's face. He looked straight at her, the way he looked at a ledger on a hard day, a thing to be taken in whole.

She could do nothing but finish.

The rest took perhaps a minute, and it was the worst minute she had ever spent in her skin. The fur went. The gold under it dimmed and shrank to skin, goosefleshed, shuddering. The cat's long jaw drew back into a face. Her breath broke from a rasp into something that nearly had words in it. She dragged herself over the sill and down onto the cold boards, on hands and knees, and the cloth she kept folded in the corner was an arm's length away and might as well have been in Kodiyur.

He said nothing. He sat very still, hands loose and open between his knees, and watched the whole of it without looking away. When the last of the fur came out of her wrist in one wet strand and fell, his eyes went to the strand and stayed there a moment. Then they came back up.

Only when she was wholly a woman, naked on the boards with her teeth knocking, did he speak.

"The cloth is where you left it," he said.

She took it. Her hands were not steady and she got it over her head on the second try, and when her face came out of the neck of it she found he was looking at the shutter. At the third slat. Politely, as a man looks away from a stranger changing in a coach.

Her tongue was a strip of dry leather. She asked the only question she had.

"How long?"

"Since the wedding night." His voice was low and even. "You went out as soon as I lay down. I was awake. I have been awake for every one you made here. Twenty days."

Neela sat on her heels and let the number go through her.

*Twenty.* She counted back and found the third slat in every one. Every dusk at this window, the bar lifted, the resin burning. Every dawn she had come home to it, with fur coming off in handfuls and him a body's length away, his breath slowed to a sleeper's. Not all the dawns. Some mornings she had turned back in the cleft and walked up the terraces in daylight, and he had been out at the pit or on the road, and she had thought herself clever. It made no difference. He had been awake for the dusks, and a dusk was enough.

The hitch. A third of the way through the long count, every time. She had filed it under chance, on the day it fell on the exact beat of her laugh, and had congratulated herself on her thoroughness.

And last night's supper, over Janu's fish. *Someone should leave him a bowl of milk. He's earned it.* The corner of his mouth that might have moved or might not.

She had turned that sentence over like a coin, looking for its edge. It had one. She had simply not been willing to cut herself on it.

"Why tonight?" she said. It was a coward's question, a way to stay on the doorstep of the answer. She asked it anyway.

"The rain," said Devan. "You were late. I wanted you to come in to a lit room."

She looked at the lamp. It stood on the chest where the dark should have been, turned low, the way one is left for someone coming in from weather. She did not know what to do with that, so she put it down beside the rest.

"You were asleep," she said. It was a stupid thing to say.

"I was not."

The cold went all the way in.

Witnesses. She had never needed to count them. There were two, and they were hers: Chathu, who wrote her page in resin-ink in a locked room on the day she first turned, and Ammu, who was fourteen and shaking and held her hand through it. Chathu, who read pages aloud at dusk to women who owed him. Ammu, who had never said the word and never would. Two. Eleven years, and a third.

And a man on the edge of a bed, with his own sword lying on the floor on her side of it. Hilt toward her, every night. It had lain there so long she had stopped seeing it. Now she saw the worn grip, and the whole of the last twenty days tipped toward it.

*Three.*

Her body moved before her mind did. She rolled, shoulder and hip, along the boards to the foot of the bed, away from the lamp, and her right arm went down into the gap between mattress and frame where the knife lay. Guild steel, plain, six inches. The grip came into her palm cold as the night it had lain through, and as familiar as her own wrist. She came up on one knee with it in her hand.

Devan let her.

She saw that as she rose. He had watched her cross the floor and had not shifted a boot. Now she was an arm and a half from him, near enough to count the pulse in his throat. She saw the angle of it. The place under the ear. A marma strike could do it with no blade at all, one thumb, and she was close enough. She would be in human shape, with no cage on the other side of it, and a man dead by his own bed.

He did not look at the sword on the floor between them. His hands stayed open between his knees, and his eyes, steady and brown, stayed on her face and not on the knife.

"I thought you might," he said. "Do it, if you're going to. I'll say what I came to say first."

It was a thing he might have said about breakfast. Her pulse went up behind her ears. She did not lower the knife. She did not lift it, either.

"Say it, then."

"Someone paid for my death." He said it without drama, as a fact about weather. "The council pressed this match on me, and a bride came out of Kodiyur with a ward's name and nothing else, and she counts exits in a room she has been in a hundred times. I'm not a fool. I never took you for anything but what you are."

"And what's that?"

"Sent." He let it sit. "Here is what I can offer. What I saw, I keep. To the household, to the fort, to anyone, at any hour. I swear it on Asha's name. And I say nothing in your hearing, ever, after dark. Not in anger. Not in my sleep."

Her mouth was open. She shut it.

"For a price," she said.

"For a name. Whoever bought it." He kept his weight where it was. "I will make the offer once. I won't make it twice, and I won't pretend it's kindness. I need the man, and I'm giving you the only thing I have that you want."

She heard the rain on the shutter, and the cistern chain clanking over it, and her own breath.

*There it is*, said the cold animal sense that had kept her alive through eleven contracts. *Not a blow. A bargain. A thread in the mouth.*

She had known it would come. Every witness she had ever had held the word, and every one of them had put it in a bargain sooner or later. Chathu's tin of jaggery sweets. *A gift is the start of a bill.* This one had only taken longer than most.

"I don't have a name," she said.

His face did not change. "The guild never tells."

"The guild never tells." She wet her lips. "You know that already. You knew it when you asked."

"I hoped."

"Hope is cheap." Her voice was hoarse but it did not shake, and she was grateful for that. "Suppose I did have it. Suppose it was written on my arm. I wouldn't buy my life from a man who can take it with one word."

The room went quiet.

Whatever he gave her, he could take. Whatever he kept silent about, he *kept*. She had been owned for twenty-one years by a man who called it mercy.

He looked at her a long while. Then he nodded once, slowly, like a man setting down something heavy.

"That is fair," he said.

"It isn't fair. It's only what it is."

"Then we both know where we stand."

He got to his feet, carefully, favouring the mended arm. She was up and braced against the bedpost with the knife held low before she knew she had moved. He saw it. He saw all of it, and made no show of seeing.

He passed her at a respectful distance, the way one passes a dog of uncertain temper. At the door he stopped with his hand on the latch and said, without turning, "You should eat. After a night like that. Janu will have rice up soon."

"Do you want me fed so I'll be useful?" The words were out before she could swallow them.

"I want you fed," said Devan, and went out.

The door closed softly behind him. She heard his feet go down the passage at the same unhurried pace, never breaking stride. He had stepped over the sword where it lay and left it there. He would go to the kalari with nothing, as he did every dawn.

* * *

Neela stayed where she was until her legs gave.

Then she went down onto the sill and sat with her knees against her chest and the knife in her fist and the shutters wide behind her. The rain blew in. It came sideways through the gap and over her shoulders and down the cloth, and she did not close them. Perhaps because a closed shutter had been a lie for twenty days, and she wanted, for a little while, to sit in the weather and be told the truth by it.

She was shaking, really shaking, the great ugly shudder she had only ever had after a bad turning. It was not the cold. It was not the hunger either, though that sat in her hollow as always.

She made herself do it the way she did a lock.

*Exits.* The door he had gone through. The window, and a drop of forty feet. The knife.

*Breaths.* She counted eight, lost the number, and began again.

*What do I know?*

That he had seen her turn, at least twenty times, and said nothing. That he had let her cross a floor with a blade and kept his hands open. That he had sat up through the last of the dark with a lamp lit, waiting for her to come home. That he had held his tongue until the last strand of gold was out of her and the sky was grey and the word could do her no harm. That he had *known* when that was.

A hunter knew that. A hunter would know to the minute, and wait, and have a kind of grace about waiting.

She tried the other reading and could not hold it. It slid out of her hand. The man who closed the shutters on her, the man who fed a cat. She could not afford it. Every kind thing that came to mind was also, from another side, a very good trap.

*He offered the thing once and I said no.* She sat with that. *And he said, "That is fair."*

It was the worst thing he could have said. She turned it over and found no seam. She looked at the third slat, at the sword hilt lying on the boards, and could not tell which of them had been keeping watch on her.

Below her, past the parapet and the rain-black terraces, a lamp bloomed in a niche at the rim of the pit. Then another. The sound of bare feet on red earth came up faint through the water, the soft rhythmic scuff she had heard in her sleep on the mornings she slept at all, and taken for the dream-sounds of a restless house.

He was drilling. In weather like this. With one good arm, and no one to strike at but the air.

The cistern chain clanked, and she counted it. She sat on the sill with the rain at her back and the knife in her hand and her whole life lying in another person's mouth, and could not tell whether the man in the pit was practising or waiting.
