# Chapter 2: What the Warden Saw

Devan had been ready for a knife.

He lay on his left side with one hand open on the sheet, because a fist wakes the whole body, and he counted her breaths past three hundred. He had built the night to give her every chance. The sword on her side of the floor, hilt toward her hand. The lamp pinched out by his own fingers. A man asleep, a wife awake, and the council's gift laid out like a plate.

She stepped over the sword.

Her eyes never went to it. She stepped over it the way a woman steps over a sleeping dog, and crossed the floor without a sound, and he understood that whatever she had been sent to do, it would not be done with steel.

Through his lashes he watched her lift the latch on the west shutters. The third slat creaked. He kept his breath long and slow. She stood very still for a count of twenty, and he gave her nothing to hear.

Then she set the wedding bangle on the sill and uncorked a flask.

The smell reached him across the room. Scorched tamarind, pine sap, something bitter underneath. He had smelled it once before, from a sealed jar passed hand to hand in a cold classroom when he was twenty and being taught what to do about people like her. He had not expected to know it at once.

She folded the kasavu on the sill. She drew black resin over her wrists and up her throat, where the old scars lay in pale rows, and for a breath she did nothing at all.

Then she bit her lip, and it began.

He had been told it took about a minute. He had not been told what a minute was. She braced both hands on the stone and her back bowed, and the bones of it moved under the skin like something turning over in sleep. Her fingers spread and shortened. Her shoulders rolled forward. A sound came out of her that was not a cry, only breath forced through a closing throat. Gold came up through her skin in patches, then in spots, dark rosettes on a pelt that rose over her ribs like water filling a footprint.

It was the most private thing he had ever seen. He did not look away. That was the job, and it was also the crime.

At the end of the minute a leopard crouched on his window ledge.

It was long and heavy, too big for the stone and perfectly at home on it. The tail hung once, then drew up. Its head came round slowly toward the bed. Two eyes caught what starlight there was and held it, green and flat, and the nostrils worked.

Devan lay like a man carved on a tomb.

The cat looked at him for three heartbeats. He counted them because counting was all he had. Then it gathered itself, flowed over the edge of the ledge, and vanished into the dark.

The bangle sat on the sill beside the folded cloth, small and bright, with nobody to wear it.

* * *

He did not say it.

Not aloud, not under his breath, not shaped on his lips. He lay with his tongue pressed flat behind his teeth like a man holding a coal, and made himself go through the lesson in order, because the lesson was all that stood between him and the thing he wanted to do.

Anyone who watches an odiyan turn, at dusk or at dawn, with his own eyes, is her witness. Not the one who sees only the beast. The one who sees the change.

A witness who speaks her night-shape aloud, while she is in shape and before dawn and in her hearing, cages her. She stays that animal for life. Her mind goes out over about a year, like a lamp left without oil.

Intent does not matter. The instructor had said it three times, tapping the table. Anger cages her. A joke cages her. A fever or a word spoken in sleep cages her all the same, and nothing on earth undoes it.

Devan was a witness now. He had a live weapon in his mouth, and he was going to carry it to bed beside the woman it was made for.

When the cistern chain clanked the next hour, he rose. He went to the window barefoot and stood in the dark beside the open leaf of the shutter, where he would be hidden until she reached the ledge. The air smelled of wet laterite and pepper. Below, the terraces lay silver and empty. There was no sound at all, which he supposed was the point of her.

Then he sat down on the cold floor with his back to the wall, and waited, and let himself think about the west stair.

* * *

Asha, his sister, his second on this wall, had left notes on his table, under his cup, inside his boots. *Eat something.* She had been twenty-two and fast and funny, and afraid of exactly one thing, which was the dark.

They found her at the foot of the west stair. The wounds were a bear's. Shoulder to hip, deep, the work of claws as long as his fingers.

There were no bears inside the walls of Kettamala. No bear could have come up the cliff, or through the padippura, or past two men on the stair. He had stood in her blood that morning and counted the gates one by one, and when he reached the end of them, he had known what kind of thing had done it. Someone had paid. Something had worn a bear for a night, then put on a face and walked away.

Six years. And now one of them slept, or didn't sleep, in his bed.

His hands had closed on his knees. He opened them.

It would be so easy. She would come back along the ledge in the grey, a beast with a woman's mind caught inside it, and he would be standing there. One word. He could watch her understand it. He could watch her stop being anyone.

The rage was not hot. It was cold and level and quiet, and it felt like clear thinking, which was why he distrusted it.

* * *

He got up, crossed the dark room to where the sword lay by the bed, and carried it back to the wall. He sat with it across his knees and made himself think properly.

A caged odiyan tells nothing. If he caged her, he would own a handsome animal and no answers, and he had married her for answers. Someone on the council had pressed this match, and tokens with genuine council wax were going out through the Monkey Pass to Thurai. A woman who could speak could be made to say. A cat on a chain could only be looked at.

Muthu would want her dead, cheerfully, on the day, by every rule of the border. Devan's thumb found the worn place in the sword's wire grip and rubbed it, slowly, as he weighed that answer. By the rules Muthu would be right.

And the buyer was watching. If Devan caged her, the buyer would learn she had failed and send someone he would not recognise. If he kept silent, the buyer must come asking why the warden still lived, and he wanted to see who came.

He did not follow her onto the hill. A leopard in her own dark would hear him inside fifty paces, and a man who trailed her was a man who had already decided to hunt. He would let her go, and see what she did with the night.

Under all of it, where he did not usually look, lay the real reason, older than the council or the wax.

He had a pair of hands that could end a man in a breath. He kept them closed. Eight years he had kept them closed, and there was a reason, and the reason had a face, and he did not think about it in the dark. He did not spar. He did not strike first. He gave orders to men who did, and he drilled alone at dawn, so the one thing he was good at would never be pointed at a living person. Power was safe in his keeping because he did not use it.

The word was the same. One sentence, and he would be a man who had used it.

Not safe, then. Merely not spent.

* * *

The chain clanked the last hour of the night. Grey crept up behind the pass, thin as milk in water.

Stiff in the knees, he stood a moment at the window with his hand on the sill, near the bangle.

The wind swung the open leaf of the shutter, and the third slat began to complain.

He caught it. He laid his palm flat against the wood, as he would on a horse's neck, and held it until the wind dropped and the sound died in his hand. He left the shutters exactly as she had left them. She would come back to the room she had mapped, with nothing moved.

Across the floor, the bed waited. He set the sword down where it had lain, hilt toward her hand, and lay on his left side with his palm open on the sheet, and lowered his lids to a crack.

She came back with the light.

A shape on the ledge first, low and tired, one paw set after another along the stone as if the stone were hot. The leopard folded down onto its belly and lay there, flanks heaving. Then the pelt began to go.

It went the way it had come, and slower. Spots thinned and ran together and faded to skin. The long spine drew in. A shudder went through the whole animal, and then there was a woman on the ledge on her hands and knees, her hair stuck dark to her neck.

The shaking was not slight. Her arms would not hold her, and she let herself down onto her side on the stone and lay with her knees drawn up, breathing through her teeth. The scars at her throat stood out wet and pale. He had a good view of them. He wished he didn't.

Small. That was the thing he had not planned for. All night he had thought of her as an edge, and the thing on the ledge was a person who had not eaten since the feast and had just been turned inside out. She crawled to the sill. She dragged the folded kasavu around herself with fingers that kept missing the cloth, and her wrist, when she reached for the bangle, tilted toward the light.

She put the gold on. It slid over the scar to the last ridge, the way he had made it do. She held that hand against her chest with the other one, hard, for a count of ten.

Then her head came round toward the room.

Devan shut his eyes.

He heard the cloth whisper. He heard bare feet on the cold floor and the shutter drawn home, and the third slat gave its dry crack as it closed. He kept his breath even and slow and let it drag a little at the end, the way a sleeper's does. She stopped, and he felt her stop. It was a long way to the bed.

Then the mattress dipped on the far side, and a body lowered itself into the dark as carefully as if the bed were made of glass. She lay with her back to him. She was still shaking. He could feel it through the frame.

She did not touch the sword.

* * *

The chain clanked dawn.

He rose without waking her, which was not hard, because she was past waking. She lay on her side with the sheet to her chin and one hand fisted under her cheek, the bangle bright against the pillow. He stood a moment looking at her, taking in what he saw, the way a man takes inventory of a debt.

*Witness,* he thought, and did not say.

The corridor was grey and cold. Raiu had gone off watch, and Janu was on the other side of the door with a brass pot of water and her mouth already set for scolding. She stopped. She looked at his face.

"You didn't sleep," she said. "I knew it. A man doesn't sleep in his own wedding bed. He lies there like a log, thinking of the pass."

"Janu."

"Don't *Janu* me. I raised you." She pushed the pot at him. "She is thin. Thinner than you. She sat at that feast and ate two bites of rice and left the rest, and I watched, I watched the whole time."

"I know," he said. "Leave rice in the chamber."

Janu's mouth stayed open for a moment, which had not happened in his life.

"Rice," she said.

"And plantains. A good many of them. Set it on the low table, where it can be seen from the bed." He took the water pot. His voice was perfectly level. "I'm hungry."

"You," said Janu. "Hungry."

"Is that so strange?"

"It is the first time since Asha." She put a hand flat on her chest as if to hold something in. Her eyes were suddenly wet. "The wedding. I said it would do him good. Didn't I say? Rice and plantains, and a little jaggery, and I'll find the good ghee."

"Thank you."

"Hunger," she said, almost to herself, and went down the corridor with her shawl flapping, already shouting for Chinna.

Devan stood with the water in his hands and the door shut at his back.

He had never lied to Janu. He had not known he could do it so well.

The door opened without a sound. She had not moved. He set the pot down and crossed to the shutters, where the long teak bar hung on its hooks, as it had hung unused all his life. He lifted it free. It was heavier than he remembered.

It would go across the shutters at dusk, before any servant could stand where he had stood.

He leaned it against the wall beside the window, within reach, and looked once at the low table where the rice would go.
