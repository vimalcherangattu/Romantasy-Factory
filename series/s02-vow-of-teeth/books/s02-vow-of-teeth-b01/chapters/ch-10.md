# Chapter 10: The Broken Beak

Forty-four steps.

Neela counted them up the west stair in the wet red morning, and at the landing she kept going. Forty-five. Forty-six. She stood with one hand on the cold rail while the number ticked over in her head like the cistern chain, and she could not make it stop.

It had begun at the bottom.

She had come to map an approach. The stair climbed the west face of the keep from the lower court to the passage outside the warden's door. Two guards on it by night, Devan had told her himself, and Raiu at the head since the lamp went dark. She wanted to know where the stone rang, where the shadow lay, how many steps a body could cover before a man on the landing heard. It was ordinary work. She had done it in a dozen houses and slept well after.

The newel post at the foot was a carved parrot, a forearm long, its wings cut flat against its sides. Someone had loved the thing once. The beak had snapped off at the root long ago, and the stump had been worn to a river stone by years of passing palms. She had laid her hand on it as anyone would, to start the climb, and her fingers had closed round the stump as if it had been shaped for them.

Her stomach dropped. It was the drop of a foot that finds no stair where the stair should be.

She stood ten counts with her hand locked on a wooden bird. Nothing happened. A pot rang in the kitchen. The court steamed in the sun. Then she let go and wiped her palm down her kasavu and told herself it was hunger. The morning after the other one was always a hole in her, and holes made the mind jump.

So she had climbed, and counted, and now she was on the landing counting air.

She made herself look. A pala-wood rail. Steps dished in the middle by a hundred years of feet. A drain slot at the turn, and at the eleventh step a stone that rang hollow under her heel. That was useful. A man at the head would hear that stone. She put *eleventh* away like a coin and went down, and at the foot her mind went on without her. Forty-five. Forty-six.

Names, then. It was what she always did. Kannan. Pepper, who bites. Chinna at the kitchen door. Gopan at the chain. Bhaskaran, Unni, Raiu with the cowlick. By Gopan the number had thinned to a mutter. By Raiu it was gone.

Janu stood at the cistern well, a good ten paces from the foot of the stair, with a cup in each hand. She held one out and did not come nearer. Neela crossed the court to take it.

"Buttermilk," Janu said. "You were up and down that stair like a squirrel. I watched from the kitchen. What are you hunting?"

"The best view of the pass."

"The best view is from the wall, where a woman has something to lean on." Janu drank and studied her over the rim. "You're grey. Did he keep you up?"

"The rain did."

"Mm." The old woman was not fooled, though she was fooled in the wrong direction. She lowered her voice, which for Janu meant that only the cistern, the kitchen and two sergeants could hear. "Since I have you alone. He's barred the shutters again."

Neela's cup stopped halfway. "Has he?"

"Don't play simple, child, they're your shutters. Every dusk since the wedding. Thirty years I've run this house, and that room stood open every night it didn't storm. His father's rule. A room should breathe. Not once barred, not the winter his mother died. Now the bar goes across when I light the evening lamps, and the whole fort knows it. The girls talk of nothing else. Chinna has a wager."

"On what?"

"On why." Janu counted on her fingers like a clerk. "Chinna says a man with a new wife wants his privacy. Bhaskaran's lads say he's gone queer from the damp. Gopan says it's my grandmother's tale. A grown man, warden of the border, afraid of a story. I told Gopan the boy was never afraid of anything but quiet."

"And what do you say?"

"I say shy. Shy men bolt doors."

"He doesn't strike me as shy."

"He doesn't strike you as anything, he's a locked chest. That's why I'm telling you." Janu reached out a dry hand and pinched Neela's wrist, hard, as women do who feed people. "Ask him."

"I'd sooner ask the goat."

"The goat would answer." Janu took back the empty cup and looked her over again. "All wrists," she said, with deep dissatisfaction. She went back to the kitchen, giving the stair a wide berth that she did not seem to know she was giving.

Neela stood in the steaming court. The whole fort knew.

That was the part that turned in her like a key.

* * *

She did her sums in the warden's chamber, with the door shut and the shutters open to the afternoon.

The bar lay along the wall on its two iron brackets, seasoned teak, thick as her forearm. She could lift it one-handed. She leaned out over the ledge, set her palm to the slats and pushed. They held. A bar does not care what has claws.

She laid it out on her fingers.

The hounds, kennelled since the wedding, who had run the yard loose for years. The sentry pulled off the rampart above this window. The corridor lamp left dark. Raiu sent to the head of the stair. Four things, and every one of them let a hunter in.

Then the fifth. The bar, every dusk, known to every maid and goat-boy in Kettamala. It shut a beast out. It left exactly one living creature in the fort who could lift it from inside.

She sat on the ledge and said it plainly to the afternoon. A beast's work, found in a room no beast could enter, and beside the body a woman with a registry paper in her hand. The pass-seal in trust until the council named another. Two days ago she had read that clause at his shoulder and put it away to use. It had a second face. A widow in black with the seal in her lap. The council's men would not need an hour.

The crown would hang her for it, and that was the lesser ruin. Chathu would lose his best tool, and he balanced every loss in some other coin. Ammu was the nearest coin he had.

So. On purpose, or by chance?

By chance, he was a man who slept badly and feared the dark. Janu had said as much. Hounds shut in to spare their howling. A sentry pulled because Muthu complained of wall duty. Oil saved. A bar for a bad dream. Each was an ordinary order, and every one had a plain answer if she asked.

On purpose, he had distrusted the match from the start, as any man would. He knew what the council might send up his hill. And he had built a cage out of small orders, one at a time, each with its excuse, so that if the bride was what he feared, her kill would hang her. It was how she would have done it herself.

Neither reading cost her a seam. She tried them again from the other end and they held just as well.

She did not like not knowing. She liked even less how much she wanted to.

* * *

Thirty-one nights.

She counted them at dusk while he sat at the table with his cloth of wax squares and she sat at the window trimming the bird lamp. When the light went he got up and barred the shutters himself. She watched him do it. He lifted the bar, dropped it into the brackets, and the wood knocked once, softly. He did it like a man closing a purse. He did not look at her.

He lay down on his side of the bed. She waited, and counted his breath, and heard the small hitch come a third of the way through each long count.

Then she lifted the bar herself, in the dark, and stood it against the wall. She uncorked the flask. The smell was scorched tamarind and pine sap. She drew the cold smear over her throat and wrists, and the burn came up through the old scars like a name being called, and then the long pull, and the floor tilted away.

The hill took the hunger out of her first. She worked it down for hours, wet-bellied and quick, the way you work a cramp. Near the small hours she came back along the parapet where no sentry stood. The ginger tom on the wall gave her a long insulted look and left. She went in through the shutters she had unbarred and dropped to the floor without a sound.

The room came at her in smells. Sandalwood soap. Old iron from the sword that lay on the floor on her side of the bed, hilt toward her, as it had lain since the first night. She had never understood it. The cold, clean smell of the brass kindi on her side, spout toward the pillow. Full again. Janu's girls, she supposed. Janu filled everything.

She crossed to his side.

The bed was low. His throat lay a hand's length under her jaw, with the pulse moving in the notch of it, slow and even. The animal in her knew precisely what that was. The hunger said what it said. Her mind ran the kill to its end, one pass, quick and clean, and it was good work.

Then it ran on, as her mind always did, to what the kill would cost by noon.

Her eyes went down, away from the throat, and found his hand.

It lay open on the blanket, palm up, fingers a little curled. The knuckles were ridged with old scars, pale and flat as split seeds, the kind a man gets from years of hitting wood and stone. Seven across the right hand. She counted them. A thin white line crossed the base of the thumb. It was a hand that had hit things for a long time and then stopped, and she could read the stopping in it, the way she could read a path in a yard.

She did not know how long she stood. When she came to herself the count in her head had gone past sixty and she had done nothing.

*Think*, she told herself.

A barred room, a hushed fort that knew about the bar, and a bride the writ had made a widow in advance: no beast could carry this kill, and she would hang for it by noon.

She lowered her head toward the open hand. Slowly. Her whiskers trembled in the warmth coming off his palm, and she stopped a hair short of the skin, with her muzzle level with the scars and her breath moving the fine hair on his wrist.

His breath hitched. The fingers curled a little tighter, as a sleeper's will, and eased, until they lay a finger's width from her nose.

She waited. He did not wake.

She drew back. She went across the stone floor, past the kindi and the hilt-first sword, and leapt to the sill. At the ledge she looked back once, which she did not do.

His hand still lay open on the blanket.

* * *

First light found her on the ledge, human, grey with cold and shaking from her teeth to her heels. The hollow came behind it, the sick wide hunger of the morning after. She went in over the sill with nothing on her and nothing in her, and crossed the room on a floor that felt like ice.

The kindi was where it always was. She drank from the spout with both hands, cold water running down her chin, and then she stood with her forehead against the brass and let her breath come back.

Behind her he slept. The hitch came and went.

Beasts were out, then. What was left was hands.

The guild had taught her the points before it taught her the other thing. There was one at the base of the skull, a thumb's width below the bone, that the old masters called the door. A man struck there with the right weight did not cry out, and he did not look like anything but a man who had fallen. A fall in a pit, in the dark, at the hour no one was watching. No bar. No beast. No bride at a window.

And Janu had given her the hour herself, and the pit lamps had shown her the place. He rose in the last dark hour, alone, and went down to the kalari under the east wall. He trained there before first light with no guard and no witness.

The ache could count one night. Tomorrow she would leave the flask in its fold of cloth.

It was a good plan. She told it to the kindi, to the grey window, and to the long shape of him under the blanket, and no one argued.

Behind her the hitch came, and went. A man asleep.

She went to find her clothes.
