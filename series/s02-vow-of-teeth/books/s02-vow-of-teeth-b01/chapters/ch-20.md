# Chapter 20: The Name Given Back

Ammu was sitting in the cleft under the second spur with Neela's shawl folded in her lap.

Neela stopped at the mouth of it, naked and shaking, still tasting the hollow of the turn-back. First light had dragged her out of the other one's skin on a bare ledge of rock, and she had crawled the last stretch on her hands. Her thighs trembled. Her mouth tasted of ash.

"Sit," said Ammu. She held out the shawl. "Before you fall."

Neela took it, wrapped it, and sat. The cache cloth and the gourd were where she had left them. Ammu had touched none of it. That was a message too.

"You followed my scent."

"I followed the—" Ammu stopped before the noun, as they all did, even by day. Her mouth twisted. "I followed the other one. Straight from the wall to here. You never even looked back." She did not smile. "I was in the grove, chechi. All night. I watched the east wall."

"Then you watched me work."

"I watched you kiss the mark."

It was not an accusation. Neela had braced for one and it did not come. Ammu's voice was low and unsteady, and her hands, which were never still, lay clenched on her knees.

"I told myself I was seeing wrong," Ammu said. "The light was bad. But I know your back. I know how you stand when you want something, with your weight on the toes. You've stood like that once in your life." She swallowed. "You put your hand on his chest. You didn't use it to find a rib."

"It was a way in. He's careful. A man who's kissing—"

"Don't." Ammu said it gently. "Don't do the blade thing with me. I'm not Chathu."

Neela shut her mouth.

"A mouth is where the word lives," Ammu said. "Whatever else he is, a husband has a mouth. You know what the House does with a girl who loves her mark? It doesn't beat her. It waits. It lets her get fond. Then it sells what she's fond of back to her, one piece at a time." Her eyes were bright and furious. "I saw Kirri get fond of Chathu. I'm not watching it again."

"It isn't that."

"Does he know what you are?"

Neela weighed the question like a coin she could not afford. If Ammu learned that Devan had watched her turn, Ammu's own rule would take over: *safe as long as nobody but us has seen*. A witness she could not unsee was a page she had to write. And Chathu had other eyes on this hill. They would not forgive a sister who sat on it. The truth would not cost Neela alone.

So it was a first lie, and she told it tired and smooth.

"He thinks I'm a bride who sleeps badly and walks at night."

Ammu's eyes narrowed. "He didn't turn round, chechi. I watched that too. The other one went past his boots, close enough to touch, and he stood there like a post."

That one Neela had not been ready for. She built the second lie as she spoke, and it came out easy. "He was facing the terraces. He thinks it was the fort tom, the ginger one with the notched ear. It's always on that wall. Half the garrison feeds it."

Ammu searched her face for a long moment. Neela let her, and kept her eyes steady, and hated how easy it was. At the end of it Ammu let out a breath, and the clenched hands loosened, and the relief in her face was so plain that it hurt to watch. She wanted it to be true. She took the lie the way a cold child takes a blanket, and did not look at its seams.

"All right," she said. "All right. Then there's time."

"How much?"

"One week." Ammu held up a finger, shaking. "I write nothing for one more week. Not what I saw on the rampart, not the three bare nights, nothing. If Chathu hears it from his other eyes first, he'll know I sat on it. So a week. In a week you either have a plan that ends in a body, or I write what I saw and you tell me I'm wrong, and I'll be so glad to be wrong."

"A week."

"Don't thank me."

"I wasn't going to."

That got a short, cracked laugh. Ammu pulled her knees up and began to hum, under her breath, and it was not the fishing song. It was slower, a heavy, rocking tune that Neela half knew from the tapping sheds, the kind the old men sang to keep the knife in time. Ammu's voice slipped into words without her seeming to notice:

*Tap the bark when the sun goes low,*
*let the black sap run and the lantern burn slow.*
*What the dusk has taken, the dusk will say,*
*the name given back at the closing of day.*

She stopped. Her face went blank, like a girl who has stepped on a stair that was not there.

"I never heard that last verse," Neela said.

"You were always counting, not listening."

"What does it mean? The name given back."

"It's a song." Ammu rubbed her nose with the back of her wrist. "The tappers made them up to stay awake. They don't mean anything. Half of them are about goats."

"Ammu."

"It's only a song, chechi." She stood and brushed the dust from her blue cotton. Her eyes went to the sky, to the sun clearing the ridge, and her voice went quick and bright and wrong. "You'll want to hear the real news. I didn't come here to sing."

Neela waited. She had learned a long time ago that the way to hear a thing was to let someone avoid it for a little while first.

"Chathu's sent word again." Ammu's mouth thinned. "It came in with the last cart, while you were out. If the account is still open at the new moon, he sends Vellan."

The cold went through Neela slowly, from the cleft floor up.

"Vellan."

"Nine nights. Chathu says it's not a punishment, only a kindness, so the work gets done before the pass shuts and you don't have to spend the winter under his roof explaining yourself. He says it like he's offering you a seat." Ammu's hands twisted in her cloth. "Vellan doesn't come to talk, chechi."

Vellan, who carved small wooden birds for the youngest girls and left them on their sleeping mats. Vellan, who had stood over two children in the yard, big as a hut, and let them live.

"I know what he comes for," Neela said.

"Then do it," Ammu said, very low. "Or do something. Anything. Just don't let him find you stalling." She leaned down quickly and kissed Neela on the forehead, which she had not done since they were girls. "I'll leave an unsplit reed in the cup on the seventh night. If it's there, I've kept my word."

She went out into the morning and did not hum again.

Neela sat with the shawl round her and counted the ridges in the cleft wall. Nine. She counted them again and got nine.

*Nine nights to the new moon. A week of grace.* She tried to feel the lie she had just told as a coin spent, and found the bill was already there: Ammu's face, relieved, and trusting her. A reprieve bought with falsehood, and a bear walking toward the new moon.

She looked at her shaking hands, and then she began to dress.

* * *

By dusk she had worked out three ways to say it, and discarded all of them.

The chamber was dim. Neela had closed the shutters herself before the light went, slat by slat, with the third one creaking as always. The corridor lamp outside was unlit. The fort was settling toward night in small sounds: the chain, a dog, Janu's voice scolding someone in the court below.

Devan had said only, at the evening meal, in front of Janu, "You didn't eat," and had put a plate of rice and fried pepper in front of her without asking. Now he stood by the bed, undoing his sword belt, and she saw him decide to wait for her to speak.

She had the flask in her hand. She had not opened it.

"I want you to promise me something," she said.

He laid the belt down. "If I can."

"Never to say it." The rasp of her own voice surprised her. "Not in anger. Not drunk. Not in sleep. You say you won't, and I believe you mean it. I want you to promise it. Never."

He looked at her for a long time. In the dim, his face was hard to read, but she had learned to read his hands, and they were very still.

"No," he said.

Her chest went tight. "No."

"I won't promise that." He came a step nearer, and he spoke slowly, because he was saying something he had clearly turned over many times. "If I promise never, and I mean it with all I have, I'm still promising what I can't hold. A fever doesn't ask. I said *cat* a hundred times, Janu told me, while I was out of my head, and the only thing I've been grateful for in eight years is that was the word I had." He drew a breath. "Sleep doesn't ask either. I can promise to try. I can promise I'll never say it awake. But you'd be hearing a promise that breaks the first night I'm ill, and you'd hear it in the dark."

"That's the point of a promise."

"No. The point is that you can lean on it." His jaw worked. "I'd rather give you something that holds."

She felt the old cold in her chest, the shape of every kindly voice that had said *trust me* with a debt behind it. She waited for the bargain.

It didn't come. Instead he put his hand in the pouch at his belt and drew out something small, and held it out in his open palm.

A river pebble. Smooth, grey-brown, no bigger than a plum stone.

"Gopan taught me this when I was sixteen," he said. "On the cistern wheel, the winter watch. Forty nights awake on a post with nothing to do, and old men talk in their sleep standing up. The old watchmen hold a stone in the cheek." He tucked it behind his molars, between tooth and cheek, and spoke around it. "Like thish."

It came out a soft, wet smear of sound. She heard the shape of words in it and no word at all.

She stared. "Say it. Properly."

"Can't." Another smear. He took the stone out and set it on his palm. "A man can mumble round a stone. He can hum. He can't make a clean word that anyone could mistake for one. Not asleep, not in a fever. Gopan tested it on me with a bucket of cold water." One corner of his mouth lifted, and went. "It's no promise. It's a plug in the mouth. But it's a thing that holds whether I'm brave or not."

"Every night?"

"Every night you smear." He said it flatly, as he said facts. "From tonight. I'll sleep badly with it. It'll go in at dusk and out at first light, when you're back. I'd sooner choke on a stone than on that word."

She had a white pebble of her own, in the pocket of her day cloth, a gift from a goat-boy. She dug it out. It was a little rounder than his and a lot whiter, and she held it up between two fingers.

"Try mine."

"Kannan's?"

"It's cleaner."

He laid his grey stone on the sill, took hers, wiped it on his sleeve with ridiculous gravity, and put it in his cheek. Then he looked at her, grim as a judge, and said, "Cashh."

She laughed.

It burst out of her before she knew it was coming, the same rough peal as in the hall, and she bent over the flask with her free hand on her knee. He raised his eyebrows at her, bulging one cheek like a boy hiding sweets, and said something that might have been *Cat* or might have been *Ought* or might have been the name of a goat.

"Stop," she said. "Stop, I'll drop it."

He took the stone out. His eyes were laughing, and his mouth was not, and then both were quiet.

And so was she. The laugh stopped in her all at once, like the cistern chain caught mid-clank.

She stood in the near dark with a stopped flask in her hand and a man in front of her holding a damp white pebble on his palm.

Eleven years the word had hung round her neck, hers to carry and hide. Chathu held it as a leash. Ammu held it as a silence too dangerous to break. Everyone else's mouth was only the place it could fall from.

No one had ever said, *This is mine.*

"Neela," he said quietly.

"I'm all right."

"You've gone white."

"I'm all right." She had to say it a second time. "It's stupid. I'd just never thought of it as something you could carry."

"It's heavy enough," he said. "I'd rather carry my share."

He did not ask her to say what that meant. He held the white pebble out to her. Before he could do the rest for her, she closed her fist on it, and felt it warm and wet against her palm.

He nodded once, as if she had answered a question, and took his own grey one from the sill.

"About the rampart," he said.

She went still.

"I stood where I was because you ran. Not because I didn't want to follow." He said it to her face, plainly, as he said everything. "I won't ask for it again at dusk, with resin on you and a word in my mouth. When you want it, you'll find me. In daylight, if you like."

Her throat did something she had no name for. "In daylight."

"I'm told it's allowed."

Outside, the cistern chain clanked once. The slats of the shutter glowed a last dull orange and went dark.

She uncorked the flask. The resin smelled, as always, of scorched tamarind. She drew it across her throat and her wrists, and for the first time in her life she did not feel alone in the minute of waiting.

Devan put the grey stone behind his teeth, and sat on the floor with his back against the bed, facing the door.

*Fifty-nine,* she counted, and the burn began.
