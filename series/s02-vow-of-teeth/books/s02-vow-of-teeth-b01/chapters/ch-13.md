# Chapter 13: The Cat on the Sill

The arrow only kissed him. That was why he rode home on it.

It came from the ridge above the second ford, a hundred paces off, and laid a line along the outside of his left forearm, shallow as a thorn scratch. Muthu's men ran down the two who bolted. Devan knotted a rag over the cut, gave the order to turn for the fort, and thought no more about it.

By the time the padippura came up out of the dust, the arm was a hot post. By the time he climbed down in the gate yard, Unni's face had gone strange and wide, and the cobbles had begun to lean.

"Warden?"

"It's nothing," said Devan, and sat down on the mounting block, because the ground was making another suggestion.

* * *

By evening he had been bled twice.

Eravi, the fort's physician, was a stooped man with a voice like a dry gourd. He had opened the vein in Devan's right arm with great ceremony and filled a brass bowl. Now he stood over the bed with his hands folded, wearing the face of a man who has done everything and would like it noted. Red lines ran up the inside of the left arm toward the shoulder, thin as threads. The wound itself had gone the colour of a plum. Raiders smeared their arrowheads with dung for exactly this.

"It is in the blood now," Eravi said to Janu. "We draw what we can. Tomorrow's sunrise will tell."

"Tomorrow's sunrise," said Janu, "tells every fool in the country something. What does it tell *me*?"

Devan heard them as if through a wall of water. The ceiling swam. He knew the room, the bed, the kindi, the third shutter slat that never stopped creaking. He knew that dusk was coming, because his bones knew it. He tried to say so and got only a dry click.

Then the door opened, and the room changed its mind about the air.

Neela did not run. She walked in fast, with her sleeves already pushed to the elbow and her hair tied back with a strip of kasavu. She looked at the arm the way she had looked at his neck in the pit, as something with a place in it that mattered. It was not the same look.

"How much have you taken?" she asked.

"Two bowls, madam, a proper measure for—"

"He's had enough." She laid two fingers on the inside of his wrist. His pulse jumped under them, hard and sudden, and her eyes flicked to his face and away again. She counted under her breath. "He's thin in the pulse. You're draining the one thing he's fighting with."

"The poison sits in the blood, and bleeding—"

"Draws him down with it." She said it without heat. "Janu. I need a pot of water boiling hard, a clean cloth boiled with it, and toddy spirit, the sharp kind from the store. Turmeric and honey. A knife, the small one from the kitchen. Boil that too."

Janu looked at her. Devan saw it happen, through the swimming ceiling: the old woman's face, which had been braced for grief, found something to do instead.

"Chinna has the sharp toddy," Janu said. "I'll bring it myself."

"Tell Chinna it's for the warden. She'll hurry." Neela glanced at the door. "And have Raiu send up Kannan with whatever neem he can strip. Tell them thank you."

She said it as though it cost nothing and they would remember it. Devan thought he had known that about her already.

"Madam," Eravi began.

"Do what she says," said Devan.

It came out in a thick voice he hardly recognised. Every face in the room turned to him. He had not meant to give an order. He had meant to be quiet.

Neela's eyes met his, level in the failing light, and for a moment she looked as if she would say something. Then she looked away and took up the lamp.

* * *

He lost some time.

He came back to the sound of her counting. She sat on the edge of the bed with his left arm across her knee, pressing the hollow behind his elbow with her thumb, then a place below the knee, then the inside of the other wrist. Marma. Healing points, the ones that pull heat down out of the body. He knew them. He had only ever used the other kind.

"Forty-one," she was saying, very low. "Forty-two. Forty-three."

Beneath the counting there was pain, and then there was the knife. He felt that only as a distant, orderly pressure, like a man being swept. The water was hot. The spirit was fire. She cut the dead edge from the wound and he heard a small sound go out of him that he would have preferred not to hear.

"I know," she said. "Forty-eight. It's nearly done. Forty-nine."

She was not speaking to him. She was speaking to the number. But he held on to it, and after a while the number became a rope.

Somewhere in that, the light behind his eyes went orange. Dusk.

His body had known it first. Then it came to the front of his mind with a horror so complete that for a moment he was clear.

The shutters were open.

Janu had opened them herself while he was gone. *Your father's rule*, she'd said. *A sick room breathes.* A last slab of copper sky lay across the floor, and the wide stone ledge outside the window stood out against it, empty and waiting.

He tried to say *bar them*. What came out was "No," and then, with great effort, "Janu."

"Hush," said Janu, from somewhere. "Hush, my heart. Lie still."

"Janu," said Neela. Her voice was easy and exact. "He'll take broth if it's thin. Would you? Not the first pot, the second. And ask Chinna for another jug of the boiled water, cooled. It will take a while."

Janu went. She went grumbling, which meant she was relieved to be given a job. The door closed.

Neela stood up.

For ten nights he had watched her do this through his lashes, with his face a sleeper's and his breath kept slow, and he had done it well. Tonight the fever had taken his lids away from him. They hung half open and would not be told. She had her back to him. She took something from a fold of cloth, a small corked flask, and drew the cork with her teeth.

Then she turned and looked at his face. He could not even close his eyes. She studied him for the length of one slow breath, the way she might look at a lamp to see whether it would burn till morning. If she read anything in the slit of his eyes, she did not show it. They were glazed, and the room was dim, and what she saw seemed to satisfy her. She turned away.

The resin went on at the throat and the inner wrists, the old pale ridges taking it. He smelled it across the room, burnt tamarind and pine sap. She climbed onto the ledge with her hands braced on the stone, and her shoulders drew together.

He had seen it ten times before tonight. It did not get easier. It got worse, the more of her he knew.

The bones moved. The spine bowed and lengthened. The sound was soft, a wet creak like a green branch bent, and then gold came up under the skin like a lamp turning, and she was gone, and the cat was there.

It stood in the last of the light. Its flanks were still pulling for air. It looked back at him over its shoulder, steady and level, and in the green-gold eyes was something so clearly her that he nearly said her name.

Instead he said nothing. There was only one word he could say, and it was the wrong one. It was right there in his mouth, whole, smooth as a stone in a sling.

On the sill the cat settled and curled its tail around its feet. It faced the room, not the hills.

Then the door opened.

"There, I've the second pot and Chinna says if—where's the girl gone?" Janu stopped. Her voice went high and thin. "Something's on the sill. There are eyes. Devan, there's an *animal*—"

The bowl fell. Broth ran across the boards.

"A great—" said Janu. "Mercy, it's the, it's the—"

The word came up in him like a swallowed coal.

It would have been so easy. He had the whole shape of it ready. His mouth began it without asking him: the tongue lifting to the roof, the first hard sound, *L*—

He bit down.

With everything he had, he bit on his own tongue, and the pain went through him white, and the taste of copper filled his mouth. The sound broke off against his teeth. The room sharpened. For a moment he could see Janu's hand on the doorframe and the dark shape on the ledge, and he knew exactly what he was holding.

"It's the cat," he said. It came out thick and wet and slurred through blood. "Leave the cat. Draw the curtain."

"That is no cat, Devan—"

"The *cat*, Janu." He had no strength for more. He put all of it into her name. "Draw the curtain."

She crossed the floor on stiff legs. She never took her eyes off the ledge, but her gaze stuck on the two points of light and slid off again, as people's gaze does. She dragged the cotton curtain across the window with a rattle of rings. The cat was gone behind it, and the shape of it, and the shine.

Janu stood there with her hand on the cloth, breathing hard.

"Eyes," she said, to no one. "Only eyes, in the dark. I'm an old fool. I've told those stories too long." She turned. "And where's that girl? She never said she was leaving."

"Sent her to sleep," said Devan. He made it flat and quick, the way a true thing sounds. His tongue was swelling. "Sit, Janu."

She sat. After a while she took his right hand, the bled one, and held it in both of hers.

He lay with blood pooling under his tongue, and he did not swallow it.

* * *

The other one lay behind the curtain with her chin on her paws and listened.

The cloth had cut off the last of the sky. It did not cut off much else. The other one's ears took in every word in the room, and her nose took in the rest. Hot cloth, spirit, turmeric. Janu's old fear-sweat, sour and brave. And through all of it, blood.

Not from the arm. This was fresher, sharper, and it came from his mouth.

He was raving. She could hear the pulse in his throat tripping, and the words came thick and wrong. *The cat. Leave the cat. Draw it.* Then a stretch of nothing but breath, then *cat* again, and a slurred, sticky sound that began a word and gave it up. He'd bitten his tongue in the fever, she supposed. Men did. She had seen a man in the Resin House sheds, shaking with ague, chew his lip to ribbons.

He had told Janu she was asleep, too, in a voice so level it nearly fooled her. A fevered man's kindness, she thought. He did not like Janu to worry.

The cat. Janu's stories had put it in him, she thought. Or the notched ginger tom that walked the parapet in the mornings, whom the warden had been feeding off the table since the second week and had never mentioned. It was a small joke the house kept. He was raving about the tom.

She thought nothing more of it. She didn't have room.

The stone had been the plan: she was to be there by the middle watch. There was a flat of cut laterite in the pala grove on the second spur, and a cup in its top, and Ammu would be crouched at it by now with her ears flat, waiting for a mark. A report. *The warden is guarded; the opening is narrower than I said.* Twenty-nine nights. The hollow dawn hunger would come for her at the end of this one, as it had after every turning, and she had perhaps that many mornings left to finish. And Chathu's message, five days old, had said what would be done if the account stood open at the pass's closing.

She had come up on the ledge to go. She had stood there with the night full of hill scent and the stone a mile off. She had looked back into the room.

Under the pulse she had counted all evening, his breath had gone wrong. That was all. The wide, quick, shallow pull of it, the lines of red going up the arm. She had known that breath. She had heard it on a girl in the sheds, two nights before they carried her out.

A man dead of a raider's arrow needed no cat. No one would ask her anything. The guild would call it the world's work, and the account would close by itself, and she would be a widow with a seal in her lap and a name coming. She had weighed that on the ledge, quite coldly, with her tail twitching. She had weighed it and laid it down. Then she had sat.

It was the third time in two days she had not done the sensible thing. The other one had no shame about it. The other one only knew who was breathing in the room.

Janu slept at the second watch, upright on the stool with her hand still locked on his. Neela could hear the cistern chain clank the hours, and the dogs, and the creak of the third slat when the wind changed. Beside the bed, the kindi slowly warmed. Twice in the dark he said *cat* and once he said a name that was not hers, and it was *Asha*, and the other one lay with her eyes closed.

Toward dawn the sweat came.

She smelled it before she saw it: the sour break, the heat going out of him all at once. His breath slowed and deepened. The hitch came back into it, a third of the way through each long count, as small and steady as a thing she had memorised without knowing.

In the last dark, when the lanterns burned low, a jackal called from the second spur.

It was thin and far across the terraces. Three short yips. A pause. One long note, climbing.

The other one's ears turned toward it before she had decided anything. She and Ammu had made that call in the yard behind the sheds when they were eleven and nine. *Tomorrow, I will find you.* It had never once been used by someone who was not coming.

She did not answer. She could not have, with that throat. The call came again, longer, and went up and up, and then the hills took it.

* * *

The grey came, and the change took her.

It pulled at the throat first. The bones shrank and slid, and she was on her hands and knees on cold stone, shaking, naked, with a hollow inside her where all the hunger of the night had gone to sit. The ledge scraped her palms. She got her cloth from the corner where she had left it folded and dragged it over her head.

Behind the curtain, the room smelled of nothing but a man asleep.

Janu had gone, leaving the stool crooked and a bowl of gruel cooling beside it. The lamp was out. In the grey light the warden lay on his back with the bandaged arm across his chest and his mouth a little open. A dark crust had dried at the corner of it.

Neela put the back of her hand to his forehead. Cool. Damp, and cool.

Her knees were swaying under her.

*Go*, she told herself. *Wash. Find Ammu. You missed the stone. Explain it.* Tomorrow, in the guild's tongue, meant whatever came after the dark you stood in. That meant this day. Ammu had waited all night with an empty cup and then come to the ridge to look. By the gate-bell she would be in Valli's market shawl, asking Bhaskaran for the warden's lady, and Neela would have to say what she had been doing instead.

She had a hundred sensible things to do, and the hollow place in her was louder than any of them. Her legs did not move. They folded, quite gently, and set her down on the boards beside the bed with her back against the frame and her knees drawn up.

The cistern chain clanked once. The first light came gold through the curtain's weave.

Above her shoulder, his right hand hung over the edge of the mattress, open, a finger's width from her hair. She felt it there as she'd feel a fire.

She did not touch the hand. She did not stand.
