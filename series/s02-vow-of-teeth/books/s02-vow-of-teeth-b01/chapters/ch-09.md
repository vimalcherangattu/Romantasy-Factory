# Chapter 9: The Goat Path

Janu had got as far as the yellow eyes when the warden put down his cup.

"—and my grandmother swore it came down the pass every dry-season dusk," Janu said, ladling rice onto Neela's plate without being asked, "big as a cart-pony, with a coat like spilled lamp oil, and it took a man off the road above the second ford. Just took him. Left his sandals standing side by side, neat as a prayer. And the next night it came for—"

"Not tonight, Janu."

He said it quietly, with his eyes on the rice. The old woman's ladle stopped in the air. For a moment Neela thought she would argue. Janu argued with the cistern chain.

"No," Janu said. "No, well." She set the ladle down. "It's a bad tale for supper anyway. Eat, child. You're all wrists."

Neela ate. She watched the warden over the rim of her cup.

He did not look up. He tore his bread and ate it. Two seats down, Muthu opened his mouth for a joke and thought better of it, and that was strange in itself. She turned it over and found the simplest reading. Some men could not abide a story. Her own guardian's accountant had been the same, and had walked out of rooms if anyone began a song.

*He dislikes stories*, she decided. She set it on the shelf with his other dislikes: market day, bells, gratitude. There was no harm in a man who hated stories. It was a good thing to know about the man she was going to kill.

She did not think about the pass-seal. She had read that clause twice at his shoulder, kept her face as still as water, and put it away where she put things she could not use yet. She did not think about how it had felt when he went quiet beside her.

"The council has written," Devan said, to the table in general. "They expect the warden's wife to be shown to the pass villages. They want her seen." A pause so small she would not have heard it a week ago. "I'll take her down to the lower waystation tomorrow. I need to inspect it anyway."

"Both birds," said Muthu. "One stone."

"Eat something, Muthu."

Neela said, "Will I be inspected as well?"

"Only by the goats," said Devan.

Janu laughed so hard she had to sit.

* * *

Thirty-two nights.

She woke to the count, to the hollow shake in her hands, and to the thought that today she would watch him work. Her own work was to watch. For eight days she had mapped his chamber, his rota and his hours, and he had handed her half of it unasked. She had not once seen him do his real work, on a road, at a distance from walls.

By the first mile she knew three things about him, and the first was that he was slow.

He did not take the cart road at a trot. He sent Unni ahead, then called him back, then sent him ahead again when the ridge showed bare. At every bend he stopped his pony and sat listening. He checked his own girth, and then hers, without comment. He had her walk the last stretch before the first village, so that people would see a woman on foot with her husband, and not a bride carried in on a beast.

"We'll be there by noon at this rate," Neela said, "if the monsoon holds off."

"It will."

"I've seen a snail with more ambition."

"The snail isn't carrying anyone." He looked at the road. "Nobody is asked to be fast on this road. They're asked to arrive."

She thought it was caution of the dullest kind. A man with that many men and that many walls had no business creeping like a thief. She made a note of the pauses anyway. He stopped at every bend, always for the same count of ten. At the third ford he went across upstream of the usual crossing. He had never once used the same place twice. A man who wished to be waited for would be easy to wait for. Without meaning to, he had given her a lesson in how not to be.

She did not like how much she wanted to ask him why.

The villages came and went. Women at the wells looked her over and decided, and she knew most of the names by the second house. Saroja, who sang at the well. A grandfather called Kesu with one eye. A girl who held up a hen as a gift, and was told by her mother in a furious whisper to put it down. Devan stood by with his hands behind him, silent, while Neela said everything that was needed. She felt him watching her do it. Twice he nearly spoke.

The langurs started in the early afternoon, on the cliff above the narrow gorge below the last village. They had been barking all morning, a hoarse and tireless racket that bounced off the rock until you stopped hearing it.

They stopped.

All of them, together. The silence came down the pass like a cloth dropped over a cage.

Devan's hand went up. Unni's pony stopped dead. Neela, who had her hand on the bridle already, felt the back of her neck prickle in a way that had nothing to do with him.

"Strangers," she said softly.

"On the road, or above it." He did not turn his head. "Both of you stay."

He went forward alone. She watched him go. He did not draw his sword. He walked his pony to the bend and stopped for ten counts, and then he made a small flat gesture to Unni that said, quite plainly, *back*. It occurred to her that the slowness had been a tool for a day exactly like this one, and that she had been laughing at the wrong end of it.

The rockfall lay beyond the bend.

It had come off the cliff and filled the cart road wall to wall. Red-brown boulders, the size of oxen, slabs of sheared laterite, a drift of loose stone to the knee. Fresh dust still clung to the biggest, and the air tasted of it.

"Dry week," Devan said, when she came up. "Dry cliff. Nothing rains stone on a dry week."

"Rock falls."

"It does. Not at the one place on the road where a cart has to stop." He looked up at the rim, where the langurs sat motionless in the thorn. "Somebody wants us standing here, looking at it."

Her body had gone cold and easy, the way it did before work. She counted the ways out. The way they had come was open. A line of boulders at the foot of the slope made a good place for a bow. The cliff offered a ledge she would have chosen herself.

"Unni," said Devan. "Back to the fort. Tell Bhaskaran the road is shut at the gorge, and I want twenty men with bars. Go by the high ford."

"Warden, I'm to guard the—"

"You're to ride. Go."

Unni went. His pony's hooves faded up the track, and the silence closed behind them.

Devan turned to look at her. He was working out how to ask her to wait somewhere safe, she could see, and also working out that there was nowhere safe. For the first time she saw him honestly at a loss. It did not look like weakness. It looked like a man laying two bad choices on a scale.

"There's a path," Neela heard herself say.

"I know of none."

"Left of the split rock. Up the scree and over the shoulder, then down through the thorn to the old well. Goats take it." She was already moving. "Give me the pony."

She did not know why she said it. Her feet knew. They had turned toward a gap in the thorn that she could not see yet, and she followed them, and there it was, a threadbare line of dung and beaten earth no wider than a hand, rising behind a boulder cloven by lightning. She had not looked for the rock. It had been where she had expected it.

*Left of the split rock.*

Nobody had told her that. She had read the scouting notes on the pass until her eyes burned. There was no goat path in them.

She went up the scree with the pony's bridle in her fist. Behind her she heard the warden's boots, steady, a half pace slower than hers on purpose. He did not ask until they were over the shoulder and the thorns closed in.

"How do you know this?"

"How do you know where a ford is shallow?"

"I walk it."

"Then you know what I'd say." She kept her back to him. "Mind the root. It's on the left."

There was a root on the left. She heard him step round it.

"That isn't an answer," he said.

"No. It's a question, and you've asked it the wrong way round." She glanced back, and his face was calm, but his eyes were on her feet. "Would it help you to know, Warden? Or would it just be something else you kept?"

He considered that, with the same patient weight he gave every question. "I won't ask again today."

"That's generous."

"It's not generous. It's tactics." The path bent, and he followed. "I would like you to keep on knowing it."

The old well was where she had known it would be. They came down through the thorn into a quiet fold of the hills, and below them, a long bowshot off, stood the lower waystation. They had come around the rockfall in the time it took the langurs to start up again, all at once, loud and bored and scolding. The strangers, whoever they were, had gone on.

Neela stood still a moment with the bridle in her hand. She had a strong wish to sit down.

* * *

The waystation was a low laterite house with a well, a long carters' shed, and a yard trodden to powder. No one lived there between the seasons. A spiked board by the door said that the warden's tally was needed for the pass. The gate stood open.

Devan went into the yard like a man entering a sickroom.

He crouched. He did not touch anything. He looked at the ground for the better part of a hundred counts, and she stood at the gate and watched his eyes travel, left to right and back, the way a clerk's do down a column.

"Six men," he said. "Two nights back, not three. They came in from the north on foot and left to the north on foot, before dawn. One of them dragged his right foot a little. Here, and here. Two lit a fire in the shed, with the door shut so the glow wouldn't show. They slept in shifts." He looked at a smear of ash. "They ate a hot meal, so someone fed them. Rice. This was a rest, not a raid."

"How do you know it was rice?"

"Because one of them dropped the husk." He held up a pinch of it. "Raiders from the coast live on dried fish. These men weren't hungry. They were waiting for something."

"For what?"

"A cart." He stood. "I don't know yet. Tell me what you see."

She had not meant to go in. She went in and crouched at the edge of the churned dust, where the clay was damp at the well's lip, and the prints were sharp as stamped wax. It was not tracking. She could not have followed those men over a hill. This was the other thing, the thing the guild had drilled into her until it stopped being a skill and became a habit of the eye. It was the study of feet.

A hundred patrols had walked past her window, and she had learned them by their boots.

"They aren't raiders, or not the way you mean," she said. "Look at the heel. This one is ground flat, straight down, in a ring. He's stood a lot of watches in one spot. Soldier. And this pair." She tapped a print. "The outer edge is worn thin, left more than right. That's a man who rides. Somebody trained them, and fed them, and these soles aren't a month old. A town cobbler did the stitching. See the double row?"

"I see a line."

"It's two lines, close together, in thread that was waxed. Coast work. Nothing from up here. They've been mended for a long road by someone who could pay for it." She looked up. "These men didn't walk from the sea. They were brought most of the way, and set down."

He was quiet. Then he said, slowly, "I'd have said six men on foot."

"I'd have said six men."

"I'd have missed the boots entirely."

"I'd never have found the husk." She heard herself add, drily, "Or the foot."

"The foot was easy."

"For you."

The corner of his mouth moved. She watched it, helplessly, as she would have watched a knot come loose.

They stood in a dead yard with a well and a fire-black shed, and between them they had made the thing whole. One of them would have had half of it. Neither could have had the rest. He had read where they went and how, and she had read who they were. It was the first time in twenty years that she had met someone whose skill ended where hers began.

She did not like it. She was careful to be clear to herself about that.

"Whoever sent them," Devan said, "wants me to find this, or wants me to find nothing. I can't tell which." He looked at the shed. "Neither can you."

"No."

"Good." He seemed to mean it. "I'd distrust you if you could."

* * *

They rode home at dusk by the cart road. The twenty men with bars had reached the fall by late afternoon and cut a lane through it, wide enough for ponies led single file, and the langurs had greeted the sight of them with a roar of professional outrage.

Neela rode alone beside the warden. The men and their bars had gone back an hour ago.

The light was going. Over the western ridge a long bank of cloud had been building all afternoon, the first heavy cloud she had seen that year, bruise-purple underneath and gold along its edges. The wind had changed. It carried a smell she had not met since the spring, of dust waiting for water.

She had no resin.

She had not carried it. No woman carries a flask to inspect a waystation. It lay in its fold of cloth, in a chest, in a room with a lamp she had pinched out last night. The sun was going down on a road two miles from a gate, and for the first time since the wedding night she was nothing but what she looked like: a woman on a pony, with a tired back, beside a man.

No ache would count tonight. One night did not matter. She would take the resin when she reached her room, and be gone before the moon.

Not yet.

*One mile*, she thought. She let it be a small bargain, with no one to witness it. She would have this for one mile.

He rode half a length ahead, as he always did, so that his shoulder covered hers. He had said nothing for a long time. He did not fill silence. She had once thought that was a trap, a man leaving a gap for her to fall into. Now it seemed more like a courtesy. He was leaving her room.

"You were right about the boots," he said at last.

"I was."

"You'd make a good tracker."

"I'd make a bad one. I'd find the shoes and forget the road." She watched the cloud. "You'd make a good thief."

"Why?"

"You know where people put their feet. That's all stealing is."

He laughed, once, short and low, like the one on the wall. She felt it in her chest.

She was not afraid. Out on the hill tonight she would have been a quarter mile from the fort, belly low, ears forward, every hair a nerve, a creature that did not trust the wind. Here she could hear the pony's hooves and a man's breath and her own voice with nothing behind it. She could smell clay and thorn and the coming rain. The man she was paid to kill rode a length ahead with his back to her.

She did not look at the back of his neck. Not for any good purpose.

The first mile ended at a milestone, a squat worn thing with a pepper sprig cut in its face. She saw it come and she did not look away. The second began.

The wind pushed a long cold breath down the pass. High up, the first drop struck her hand, big and warm.

"Rain," said the warden, and then, after a moment, "A real one."

She almost laughed. She didn't know why.

Ahead, the lamps of Kettamala came on, one at a time, up the red wall. She counted them without meaning to. Eleven. Then she started the road again from the beginning, as if she had something to learn by it, and could not think what.

She still had no idea how she had known the path.
