# Chapter 19: Resin on the Rampart

The split reed lay across the cup of the meeting stone at dawn, and Neela did not need a note to read it.

*Show me.*

In the Resin House a reed across the cup meant one thing. Not a report on paper, not a tale told over sweets. The work itself, done where the sender could watch. Ammu had waited three weeks at an inn for a woman who slept three nights human beside her mark. She was done being told.

So at sunset Neela stood on the east rampart with the flask open and the cork between her teeth.

Fifteen nights. The sun had gone behind the keep and left the wall in blue shade. Beyond the terraces the second spur climbed, higher than the rampart, to the pala grove, a dark line of trees against the last of the sky. Somewhere in that line, Ammu was lying in wait.

Neela had watched the pairs all evening. They passed at the far corner and swung inward, and east of the cistern housing the wall made a low shoulder. There she would have thirty counts with no one to see her go over. She had checked it twice.

She tipped the flask. The resin was black and slow and smelled of scorched tamarind. She drew it across her throat with two fingers, then over the inside of each wrist, along the old ridged scars.

*Sixty.*

The count began the way it always did, without her choosing. Cold on her skin first. Then the faintest heat, like a lamp wick lit a long way off.

*Fifty-six.* A door scraped in the man-high stone housing beside her, and Devan stepped out with chain oil on his hands.

He saw the flask. He saw her wrists. He did not look surprised, which was somehow worse than if he had.

"The pair on this stretch are at the padippura," he said. "Bhaskaran has a cart he doesn't like. It will hold them until the next clank."

*Fifty-one.* "You arranged that."

"You watched the east wall all through supper. Twice you looked at the shoulder and your lips moved." He wiped his hands on a rag. "I'd rather be the man who made the gap than the man who found out how big it was."

She ought to have been angry. The skin of her throat was too busy for anger.

"And the cistern?"

"Gopan's chain jams in damp. I had the wheel apart, and that part is true." One corner of his mouth moved. "It's also a very good place to stand."

*Forty-six.* The heat had reached her wrists and begun to climb. She corked the flask with a hand that did not shake, and was proud of it.

"My sister wouldn't come up here after dusk," Devan said. He said it to the dark over her shoulder, easily, the way a man mentions weather. "Asha. She'd walk into a crowd of drunk soldiers with a stick and a joke, and she'd go the long way round by the lit court rather than cross a dark yard. She counted the steps on the stairs. Under her breath, all the way down. She said numbers kept the dark from filling in."

Neela said nothing. Forty-three.

"You do it too," he said.

"I don't."

"Your mouth moves. At the window, at table, on the stair to the hall. Fourteen steps, and you take the turn on the eleventh." He looked at her then, not at the flask. "I noticed in the first week. I didn't say so, because I thought it was private."

"Forty," she said.

It was out of her mouth before she could stop it. He frowned.

"Forty what?"

"I'm counting down." Her voice came out dry, almost light, and she held on to that. "I smeared this before you came out. It takes about a minute. We're at forty."

His hands stopped. The rag hung from his fist. "Then you should be on the other side of that wall."

"Probably."

"What do you count?" he asked. "The rest of the time. I'd like to know."

It was the plainest question anyone had ever put to her. No bargain in it. No hook. She felt for the usual answer, the one she built for strangers, and found she had not made one for him.

*Thirty-six.*

"Exits," she said. "Steps. How many strides it takes a man to cross a room. How many breaths I've been holding." The heat had reached the corners of her jaw, and she swallowed. "Lately I count yours."

"Mine."

"It hitches. A third of the way through, when you're lying awake and pretending you aren't."

He was quiet for two of her counts. "I didn't know it did that."

"It does."

"Thank you for telling me."

That was all. He didn't ask what she meant to do with the knowledge. He took it the way he took the sword hilt, the kindness and the fact together, and left it where it lay.

She looked at him. She had been looking for weeks and had pretended it was study. The faint stripe across his knuckles where Janu's ladle had landed. The oil under his nails. The set of his shoulders, which had been braced for eight years against a blow that never came, and which tonight, in the blue light, were not quite braced.

*Thirty-one.* Half a minute, near enough. Her bones would start soon, and if she stayed in reach the turning would begin against his chest. He had watched it from the bed and from the dark of the chamber more times than she had known to count. He had never watched it this close, with his hands on her.

*Thirty.* The grove. Ammu in the trees, who had laid a reed across a stone at dawn and gone to sit with a lifetime of rules in her head. *We're safe as long as nobody but us has seen.* Whatever Ammu saw tonight, she would carry home to Chathu with a face full of love.

*Twenty-nine.* His mouth. A good mouth, quiet and careful, and it held the one word that could close a door on her for good. A man lost in a kiss might say anything. He might say it with his eyes shut and mean only her name.

*A mouth that is busy cannot say much.* She thought it with the dry bitterness she kept for her own cleverness. It was also the most honest thing she had thought all week.

Three reasons, and the sum came out with no remainder. She wanted it anyway. No debt on the far side of it, no plan, no tin of sweets.

*Twenty-eight.* She did not go to him. She went the other way, two paces to the housing door, and stood in its lee, where the thick laterite flank and the low parapet shoulder met. She had judged it from the wall-walk, level with the housing, and from there it was a wall of stone. No watcher in the thorn would see a thing.

Then she crossed the three steps between them.

He stayed where he was. She put one hand flat on his chest and felt his heart going under the linen, fast and uneven, nothing like the calm he showed the world. His hand came up to her face and stopped a finger's width from her skin. Waiting. Asking in the only way he knew, without a word.

"Yes," she said, and kissed him.

His hand landed light, as if testing whether ice would hold. Then it held. He kissed her back with the same patient attention he gave everything, a kalari master learning a new form. He tasted of pepper tea and cold metal. His thumb found the pulse under her jaw, the one the resin had already begun to heat, and stayed there.

She stopped counting, and knew it only because the number came back like a slap. *Twenty. Nineteen.* His breath caught on the hitch, a third of the way through, in her mouth. She almost laughed.

Then the hitch was gone, and his breath ran ragged. His free hand fisted in the cloth at her back and dragged her an inch closer. A moment later he drew back a hand's breadth, and she knew that look, the look of a man about to be sensible. "Neela, the resin—"

She pulled him down again and he stopped being sensible.

Her wrists were burning now. The ache climbed her forearms like warm wire. His free hand closed on hers, on the scarred inner skin, and the touch should have been agony and was instead the opposite, a hand laid on a wound and not flinching from it. He knew what the ridges were. He did not pull away from them.

*Twelve.*

And then it came. The first burn, at the throat, a white line drawn tight across the skin as if someone had finally tightened a thread. It took the breath out of her.

*Not here. Not in his hands.*

She tore her mouth free and stepped back out of the circle of his arm. His hand was still open in the air where her face had been.

"Neela—"

She ran.

The east stair was a narrow flight cut into the thickness of the wall, ten paces along the rampart. She took it at a sprint, boots slapping stone, the burn moving down her spine like a hot nail. At the stair head she looked back, because she couldn't not.

Devan was exactly where she had left him, hands open at his sides. His boots were planted like the wall itself. He was breathing hard and looking straight at her, and he did not take a single step.

A man who wanted to catch a woman would have followed. A man who wanted to keep her would have caught her wrist. Devan had stayed where he was, as if his own feet were the lock on the door.

She could not tell whether that was the kindest thing done for her in years, or the coldest. She noted that she could not tell, and she went down into the dark.

* * *

Four turns of stair. A niche at the second, where the wall made a shelf for a lamp that Gopan never lit. She tore the day cloth off her shoulders and shoved it in the niche with the flask on top, and the burn took her at the knees.

It was never gentle. The bones went first, a long grinding shift in the back and hips, then the skull drawing forward, then the teeth. The scars at her throat and wrists turned to bands of fire. She dropped to her hands on the cold step, and her hands stopped being hands, and the stair-dark that had been black went suddenly grey and sharp and full of smells.

Lamp oil. Wet laterite. Chain grease. Pepper tea, still faintly, from a mouth she had left behind.

The other one shook herself and stood.

She went up the stair on soft pads, and the rampart opened ahead of her in a new language of sound. Far off, the cistern chain turned its slow wheel. Above, on the spur, the pala grove breathed. On the walk, ten paces from the stair head, a man stood with his back turned to the stair.

Devan had faced the wall. Not the stair, not the dark where the cat would come from. He stood looking out over the terraces with his shoulders squared, and he did not look round when she passed behind him. His hands were flat on the parapet as if he were holding it down. She went by his boots close enough to feel the warmth of him, and he did not say a word.

In the animal way that comes before thinking, she understood what the silence cost him.

The shoulder of the wall took her in two bounds, and she crouched on the stone.

The night came in whole. The terraces lay below, ranked in pale steps, and beyond them the spur rose to the trees. At the top of it, at the edge of the grove, where the thorn thickened, something sat very low and very still, and two eyes held the last of the light.

Green-gold. Round. Set close and unblinking, at the height of a dog sitting on its haunches.

A jackal's eyes.

They did not move. They did not turn to follow her up the wall, as a watcher's would, to catch the clean line of the leap. They stayed fixed on the housing door, ten paces back along the rampart, where a man stood alone with his hands on the stone and his mouth shut. Fixed on the spot where, not two hundred counts ago, there had been two people.

She saw her mistake from up here, as plainly as a slate wiped clean. She had judged the lee standing on the wall-walk, where the housing's flank was a wall. But the grove stood higher than the rampart, and Ammu had been looking down. The housing was only man-high, and the low parapet shoulder beside it hid nothing from above. A stone wall had only hidden her from herself.

Ammu had asked to be shown the work.

She had been shown the kiss.

The eyes held a long moment more. Then they went out, the way a lamp goes when a hand closes over it.
