# Chapter 6: Green Wax

Janu held the sandalwood box out at arm's length, the way she held a fish she didn't trust.

"The queen's own wax," she said. "I've never seen it so close."

It sat in the middle of the storeroom floor between a rice sack and a chest of Chirayil linens. Neela had known what it was from the doorway. Everything else the wedding had brought was brown paper and string. This had a thumb of green wax over a knot of red cord, pressed with a small bird, wings spread.

"A rider brought it before noon," Janu said. "Sat in my hall and ate for three. Sudhi, he said his name was. I wanted to hit him with a ladle."

"Sudhi," said Neela, and put it away with the rest. Maniyamma and Thankappan, who had carried the chests. The two Chirayil cousins who had sent the bolt of yellow cloth and not come. She had learned them all while Janu talked. It was a bad habit for a woman who was meant to pass through a house like water. She had never been able to stop.

"Open the lamp first," Janu said. "It's the only one from your own people, and you've been polite to everything else."

The lamp came out of its straw heavy and cold, a standing lamp of brass with a bird worked into the stem and five cups for oil. Someone had paid for it and chosen it, and wrapped it so it would not scratch. It was part of her family. She had no family. A stranger in the Chirayil house had sat down and thought, what would a ward carry to her husband's fort? And had decided on a bird.

She rubbed a thumbprint off the rim with her sleeve.

"Handsome," said Janu, grudging. "Put it by the window. It'll catch the evening."

The warden's step sounded in the passage. He didn't knock. He stopped in the doorway and looked at the box before he looked at either of them.

"You sent for me," he said.

"I sent Kannan to tell you there was a box," said Janu. "From the palace. I thought you'd want to cut it yourself."

"I do." He crossed the floor, crouched, and drew his belt knife. The red cord parted. He slid the blade under the green wax and lifted, and the whole seal came away in one piece, the bird still crisp on its face.

Inside, on a bed of dry cloth, lay a betel set in silver. A tray, a lidded box for lime paste, an areca cutter with a sunbird for a handle. Janu made a sound of pure greed.

Devan barely glanced at it. He turned the seal over on his palm and tilted it to the slit of window. He ran his thumbnail along the underside, then the edge. He held it close to his face and breathed in, once, the way Neela had done with the council's wax in the hall. She had caught the smell herself when Janu set the box down: crushed leaf. Not clove.

He was at it longer than he'd given the silver or the cord, and the cord was cut through.

"Well?" Janu said. "Her Grace's own hand chose it."

"Her Grace's staff chose it."

"It's *lovely*."

"It is." He closed his fingers over the wax. "I'm not going to eat from it."

Neela's mouth moved before she'd checked it. "You don't like the gift."

"I don't trust gifts from the palace."

He said it plainly, to the floor, in the same voice he'd used for the weight of a berry. Janu made a noise like a kettle. "Devan! In this house! Under my roof!"

"I'll say it under hers, if she asks."

Neela knelt very still among the straw. She thought of the Resin House, and of sweets. Before every contract, Chathu sent a tin of them to her room. Jaggery and sesame, soft, still warm from the kitchen. *For luck, child.* She had eaten all eleven tins. She had also learned, around the fifth, that being given something was the start of a bill.

"Then we agree," she said.

He looked at her at last. It was a short look, and a surprised one. "Do we?"

"I was raised by a pepper house. Anything given free costs double." She kept her voice light. "Ask any trader."

Something eased at the corner of his mouth. It didn't become a smile. It only seemed to be thinking about one. "I will," he said.

He turned the seal over once more. Then, to her surprise, he held it out across the box, green wax flat on his palm. "What would a trader say it cost?"

She looked at the bird, wings spread, and not at him. "The price of what they'll ask for next."

He nodded once, the way a man nods when a weight matches his own scale. He closed his hand and tucked the wax into his sleeve. "Janu, lock the set in the granary with the hill seed. Nothing from that box goes into our chamber or our mouths."

"A queen's silver in the *granary*—"

"Yes."

Nobody in this fort had asked what she thought of anything that wasn't pepper. She noticed that she had been asked, and that some small, stupid part of her had wanted to be. She put the feeling with the other things she would have to ignore.

"One more thing," said Janu, who could not let a quiet go by. "Warden. The cat."

"What cat?"

"The one in the pepper store! Two sacks torn open this week, and droppings I could use for a doorstop. I've told the boys to sweep and it comes back. Turn the hounds loose again, a night or two. They'd have it out by morning."

"The hounds stay in."

"Then what am I to do?"

"Leave the cat," said Devan. "It keeps the rats down."

His eyes were on the box lid as he said it. Neela's hand had been resting on the lid, one finger tracing the grain. It stopped.

*The cat.*

Her heart did one hard knock and went on. She kept her face where it was.

It was Janu's word. Janu had said it three times this week, of the pepper store, and the old woman at the ford had said it, and Janu's story at supper had used it for a creature half a hill away. Everyone in this fort said cat. A man who answered a steward's complaint used the steward's word. Of course he did. And a cat in a pepper store was a cat in a pepper store.

She lifted the finger. The lid closed on its own weight.

"I'll carry the lamp up," she said, "before it's lost under all this."

She did not look at his sleeve, where the green wax was. She did not look at his face. She took the lamp by its bird and went out into the passage, and counted her steps up the stair to forty, to be sure they were even.

* * *

At dusk the lamp stood at the window with a thin line of evening running down its stem. The shutters were barred. The sword lay on the floor, hilt toward her. The man who owned both was already in the bed with his back to the door, breathing slowly and evenly, which she had learned meant nothing.

She counted him to three hundred. Then she went to the window.

Thirty-five nights. Thirty-five, and tonight she was not going to spend one watching a wall she'd already measured.

The bar. The third slat, a crack like a knuckle. The resin on throat and wrists, black and bitter, with its old burn lighting under the skin, and the rush of smells that came over her in a flood. The other one took the ledge in a single flowing step. It looked up, and the rampart over the window was empty. The rampart had been empty two nights now. Neela had stopped calling it luck. She noted it and pushed it behind her.

The leopard didn't go to the yard this time. The yard was learned. It went along the foot of the west wall and dropped to the first pepper terrace, then the next, and over the lip of the last into the dark of the hill.

The hill was loud with small lives. A hare screamed somewhere and went quiet. The animal's hunger came with the night, and it was not hers, and it ate at her anyway. The leopard passed a hollow where a hare breathed in the grass, a fast and tiny pulse. It let the hare breathe. It was there to work.

*Find the weak hour.* It was the thing the contract needed, and the thing she couldn't get by looking down from a ledge. She went back over what she had. The chain at the cistern, and Gopan, who kept it. The pairs changing on the third clank after the middle watch. Raiu holding the torch in the passage outside the chamber, a boy who nodded off over his own elbow by the end of the night. Chinna snoring at the kitchen door. The cooks who woke when the sky went grey. The men on the wall in the last long hour before first light, when the night pairs had stood longest, with their lanterns burned low and their heads gone heavy.

The last dark hour. Just before first light the whole fort was at its poorest.

The hour had one flaw, and she found it as she ran. The man she was paid to kill woke in that hour. He rose before the light and went to the pit alone, and for as long as he drilled he was awake and armed and in the open air.

She checked herself mid-stride and made herself be honest. She knew it only from Janu's grumbling and from the pit lamps she saw lit below the east wall. She had never once been in the chamber to watch him rise. At that hour she was always out on the hill. It was a thing she had been told, not a thing she had seen.

She would not take him in the pit. She would take him in the bed, before he woke, or not at all. Which left the hour before the hour. A narrow door, but a door. She would look at it again at dawn, with her own eyes.

She was a good way up the second spur, in the pala grove, when the wind changed.

The scent came down the slope on it, and the leopard stopped dead with one forepaw lifted. Under the green smell of wet leaves, under dung and old rain, there was a sharp thread of musk. Warm. Fresh. Neela knew it the way she knew the dormitory blankets at the Resin House, thin and grey and smelling of one particular child.

*Jackal.*

The leopard went up the slope low and fast. At the top of the spur stood a split slab of laterite, knee high, with a lightning scar through the middle and a pale cup cut in its top. Every meeting stone in the guild was cut with a cup. A mark was left in it, or a thing was left under it, and no one who passed would see anything but a rock. She had been told to look for this one before she left the Resin House. Now she had found it.

Wet at the base, where the pink earth had been splashed. Fresh. The jackal had stood here at dusk, or an hour after, and said her piece in the oldest way a jackal could.

Ammu was close.

She put her face to the mark and breathed it in. It was what anyone else would call a jackal's mark on a stone. It warmed her like tea taken hot.

The leopard could not speak. It could not leave a word. It could only press its cheek against the laterite and drag it, once, slowly, from one end of the slab to the other. A leopard's answer, with its own scent laid down over hers.

*I'm here. Come.*

She stayed until the wind moved, then turned for the fort. The grey was a long way off still. She ran the paths home with her head up, and the hunger forgotten.

* * *

She took the terraces at a lope and the wall in three bounds and was crouched on the ledge before the chain rang the last hour of the dark. She hadn't thought of it as the last. Now it was. The sky behind the eastern wall was the colour of a dove's back, and the shutter stood open the width of her shoulders.

The bed was behind her. She could hear him from here. Slow. Level. His breath did the small hitch that it always did, a little stumble a third of the way through each long count. She took it for the sign of a man in the deepest part of sleep.

Still abed, then. At first light, again. She set it beside what Janu said about the pit lamps, and the two did not fit. She would count it again tomorrow, and the night after.

The grey came up. The other one went out of her all at once.

It always hurt, and it always hurt the same. The bones took it first. Then the skin of her wrists and her throat peeled hot and ran cold, and she was a woman crouched naked on a stone sill with her teeth chattering, one hand flat against the wall for balance. The hunger came after, hollow and shaking, scooping her out from the inside.

She breathed. She looked up for the sentry's lantern, and there was none, and in its place sat a cat.

It was on the parapet above her, in the very spot where the boots had stood. A scruffy ginger tom, one ear gone to a notch, his coat in ropes. A dead rat hung from his jaws. He looked down at her with the flat hostile calm of a creature who had held this wall long before she had, and he was covered in pepper. It lay on his paws and whiskers and on the top of his head like flour. He looked at her for a long moment.

Then he sneezed.

It was a tiny sneeze, outraged and very wet, and the rat jumped in his mouth.

The laugh came out of her before she knew it. It came out loud, ringing off the laterite, and it was a real one, not the careful bell of a Chirayil ward. She clapped her hand to her mouth, and the laugh went on behind it, shaking her shoulders, hunger and cold and all.

*Leave the cat. It keeps the rats down.*

That was the cat. That was the whole of it, a pepper-dusted tom with a notched ear. She had stood in a storeroom and let a word go through her like a nail, and it was a ginger ruffian with an opinion about Janu's sacks. She leaned her forehead on the stone and shook with laughing, silently now, tears in her eyes.

Behind her, in the dark of the chamber, the breathing hitched.

She froze, one hand still pressed to her lips, and counted. One, two, three. The breath settled and ran level. A man turning in his sleep.

But the hitch had come on the very beat of her laugh. Not a third of the way through, like every other night. She filed it. She told herself that a loud sound wakes anyone halfway, that sleepers stumble, that it was chance. She did not look at the bed.

The tom looked down at her with great contempt. He picked up his rat and walked away along the parapet, his tail kinked at the tip.

"Thank you," she whispered, and climbed in.

She dressed in the dark, quickly, with her teeth shut on the chatter. The cold bangle went on last, over the ridged skin. She lowered the bar into its brackets with both hands. By the time she turned, she had half decided she'd have two helpings of Janu's rice, and was thinking very hard about rice.

Then, from the tree line past the lowest terrace, a jackal cried.

Three short yips. A pause long enough to count. Then a single long note that slid up and cracked at the top, very thin on the cold air.

She stood with her back against the shutter and her skin gone tight. She knew the call. They had practised it in the yard behind the sheds, eleven years old and nine, bent double with laughing, until Master Chathu came out to tell them the Resin House had no use for dogs. Three and one meant *tomorrow*. It meant *I will find you*.

The call came again. Three. A pause. One.

She did not answer. She could not, and it did not matter. She stood in the half-dark of a stranger's chamber, with the sword on the floor and the sleeper in the bed, and heard her sister tell her, over the length of the hill, to hold on one more day. And behind the call, quieter, the question she had filed and could not unfile: had he meant the cat?
