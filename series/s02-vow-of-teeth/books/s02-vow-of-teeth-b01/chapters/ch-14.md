# Chapter 14: Varanad Coin

Neela heard the route before she understood it was an argument.

She was in the kitchen passage with a basin of boiled cloth. The warden's voice came through the hall door, level and a little too slow, like a man climbing stairs one at a time.

"Out the east postern before the first clank. Down to the second ford. Up through the lower narrows to the shelf, and home by the cart road before the day watch."

"No," said Muthu.

Muthu never said no. He said *Warden* and *come now* and *by my mother's pots*. The flat word sounded like a stranger's. Neela set the basin on the step and stood where the doorway shadow was deepest.

Devan sat at the long table with his left arm strapped across his chest. The brown of his face had gone grey underneath. Janu stood behind his chair with a cup of broth she kept lifting and lowering, as if the cup might argue for her.

"Eravi gave you a week in bed," said Muthu. His big face had no laugh in it. "Yesterday you sat down on a mounting block because the yard tilted."

"I sat because it was there."

"You were *put* down. By a poison. In front of the whole gate."

"The narrows are where Raman died." Devan said it without weight, the way he said every fact. "Spring, on my patrol, in single file under that shelf. His boy is nine. I'll not send eleven men through that cut and sit in a bed counting them."

"Then I lead it."

"You lead the second file."

"And if the arm opens on the shelf?"

"Then you'll have the pleasure of telling me so."

Neela did what she had learned to do in other people's houses. She listened to the road inside the sentence. East postern, first clank. The ford. The narrows. She had seen the cut from the east terraces, a notch in the far hills, and she knew the kind of going it held: a ledge of laterite along a cliff foot, one horse wide, the only dry way through. A rider on it could be reached from above by anyone with a bow and patience.

She caught herself having measured it. Her mind went quiet and cold and said, with no heat at all: *Twenty-eight nights. And a road that is nearly a gift.*

Valli had not come to the gate by the bell, either. Neela had watched it all morning with the patience she gave a lock. No grey shawl, no pepper trader's girl. She did not know what that meant, and she disliked not knowing more than she disliked the road.

"Madam."

Devan was looking at her. Muthu's ears went red.

"The whole passage heard you," said Neela. "I'm told the cistern chain did too."

"Then you'll have an opinion."

"Would it change the route?"

"No."

"Then I'll keep it for something that might." She crossed to him and looked at the strapping, not his face. The knot was Janu's, the dressing hers. It had held. "I'll see that arm tonight."

"I'll be asleep."

"Then I'll look at it asleep."

His mouth went still, the way it did when he was pleased and hiding it. Muthu barked a laugh in spite of himself. "She's said no to you in four lines, Warden. I've been at it eight years."

* * *

By dusk he was in bed, flat on his back, his right hand on his chest and his eyes shut. Janu had banged the room into order and gone. The shutters were closed.

Neela lit no lamp. She unwound the dressing by feel and tilted the arm to the grey light at the slats. The red lines had faded to pink threads. The wound was clean at its edge. She wrapped it again, slowly, and he did not stir.

His breath hitched, a third of the way through the count, the way it always did. She found she had been waiting for it. *Chance*, she told herself, and put the thought away with the others.

She lifted the bar herself. She drew the curtain, took the flask from her cloth, and pulled the cork with her teeth. Resin at the throat and wrists. The burn, the old ridges taking it. She went up onto the ledge, and the dusk took her bones.

The other one came down the east terraces in the last of the light, low under the pepper vines, past the goat shed and the white goat who bit, and out over the wall by the lantana. She did not go to the stone. She went to the narrows.

She did not know why, and she did not ask. The other one only knew the hills were wide and the road in them was narrow, and a man would ride it at dawn.

The wind came up the cut, and with it the news. Cold fat. Bow-wax. Old ghee going rancid in a skin. Bronze, which smelled of nothing and so of something. Two men. No smoke.

She went up the back of the cliff by a chimney of broken laterite, belly low, and lay in the rocks twenty paces above the shelf.

The men had made a cold camp in a notch of the lip. Two bundled shapes in wool, backs to the stone. One sat awake with a bow unstrung across his knees. The other slept with his hand on a round thing wrapped in cloth. A mirror. The other one knew the shape, because Neela had been taught what a mirror was for. At first grey they would string the bows. When a column came along the shelf in single file, one of them would flash the count to whoever waited beyond the cut.

Neither had eaten. A man who plans to move at dawn does not spread his stores.

She lay there while the stars came out, and Neela made herself do the sum.

A warden with one good arm, riding at a walk on a ledge one horse wide, under two men who could not miss. Last night she had only needed to sit still while a fever did it. Tonight she needed only to lie still and let two strangers' arrows do it, with not a claw of hers on the work. The guild would call it the world's work and close the account. She would be a widow with a seal in her lap. No cage, no ache, no page read aloud at dusk.

It was the cleanest road she had been offered in twelve contracts.

Then the other side of the sum. The stone had stood empty last night. If she went to the narrows, it would stand empty again at the hour Ammu looked, a second cup with no mark in it, and Ammu would count that as she counted everything. Valli had not come to the gate. Whatever that meant, Neela would not be there to learn it. And Chathu's word hung over both: *after the writ lands, every day counts double.* Two days she could not spare, spent on a man she was paid to kill.

She counted it all, to the last coin. Every column said lie still.

The cistern chain came up faint from the fort, once, twice, and the stars wheeled. She did not move. Behind the sum came the names. They arrived without being asked, as they always did. Raiu and his cowlick. Unni, whose mother sold salt fish. Bhaskaran at the gate with his stubborn jaw. Eleven riders behind a man who would not send them where he would not go. Raman's nine-year-old, whom she had never met.

She had known every one of them by name for nearly two weeks. That was the trouble with her. The guild had given her a number, and she had spent her life refusing it.

The chain clanked again. Four hours to first light, then three.

*It is the contract*, she told herself. *He is mine. I will not have a stranger's arrow take what I was paid for.*

It was a lie, and it was a good one. She used it.

The watcher turned his head toward a loose stone that had not moved. The other one went over the lip.

It was short, and it was not clean. The man with the bow had time to open his mouth. The sleeper woke under her weight and got a hand up, and the blow took it away. A knife came out and scored her shoulder, hot and thin. Then there was no more knife. The wool heaped in the notch went still, and the wind took the smell of it down toward the ford.

A leather purse had burst under her forepaw. Coin ran across the rock, bright in the starlight, and the other one, mid-snarl, breathed it in.

New silver. Not the dull, thumbed smell of a coin that has been in a hundred pockets. This was sharp and clean, like a blade fresh from the stone, with oil still in the milling. She turned one over with a claw. A pepper sprig on one face. Varanad's sprig. The edge was cut so fine it nicked her pad.

Nobody had spent these. Nobody had even handled them.

She nosed the dead man's satchel open. More silver, wrapped in a rag. A scrap of paper. And a split-ash tally stick with a thumb of wax on its end, and from the wax, faint and unmistakable, clove.

She knew that smell. Janu's kitchen. The broken tally on the hall table, the day the patrol came in. Real council wax, poured to keep it from cracking in damp.

*Why would a raider carry that?* Neela asked, and the other one, who had no use for questions, took the strap in her teeth.

Neela did the thinking while the animal dragged. The warden had broken one tally open in the hall. One tally was a story; two were a case. Here was a second, and he could have it. That was one reason, and she let herself have it.

The other was colder. Left up here, the satchel would be found with two men mauled in a camp, and the fort would hear a cat-kill on its own hill and start asking what cat. A bag on the path made them raiders, and made the coin the story.

And it had a price. A bag lying where no bag should be told the warden someone had stood on this ledge before him. He was not a fool. He would ask who. She weighed that against a patrol riding past a dead camp and never learning what the scouts carried, and chose.

The path was a long way down. She dragged the satchel over the lip and down the scree, a hundred paces of slithering stone, and dropped it on the shelf at the exact place where the first horse would set its foot. Then she climbed back to the camp, and the bodies, and the bronze mirror still wrapped in its cloth. She left the mirror where it lay.

She meant to leave the men. The other one stood over them with her sides heaving.

*Yes*, said the hunger. It had no other word. It was the animal's, and so it was hers, and it came up through her like a tide.

*No*, said Neela.

It was the hardest thing she did that night. She turned her back on a meal still warm, put the wind in her face and walked away stiff-legged, with her jaws shut so hard her teeth ached. Every step was a thing she had to choose again. She had killed to keep a man alive. She would not also be the thing that ate them.

She went by the long way to the cleft in the rocks under the second spur, and on the way she passed the meeting stone. She stopped there, late, with blood drying on her shoulder, and dragged her cheek across the cup in its top. A scent-mark, no more. It would be a night old by the time Ammu read it, but it would be there.

The cleft held a bundle she had hidden that morning, because she had woken on the floor beside his bed and never meant to wake there again. A day cloth, a shawl, a water gourd. The guild taught it: *keep your dawn where you can reach it.* She lay down in the cleft with her nose in her paws, and shook, and let the night go by with its hunger unfed.

* * *

The cut on her shoulder pulled first, a hot thread through the cold. Then the change took her.

She came back to her hands and knees on frozen rock, naked, with the sky going the colour of dishwater above her. Her teeth rattled. The knife cut had opened in the turning and bled a bright thin line down her arm. She dragged the cloth on over her head and wrapped the shawl twice round and could not feel her fingers on the knots.

She ate nothing, because there was nothing to eat, and walked home.

Her feet bled quietly on the laterite between the pepper vines. A goat bit her hem. By the time the cistern chain clanked for the first time that she could count, she was on the east terrace, wrapped to the chin, with her back against the wall and the whole valley in front of her.

The narrows showed from here, a notch in the far hills like the bite in a flatbread. At the third clank, the line of riders went small and dark into it.

Then they stopped.

It was a long way off, and a small thing. A bunching, a spreading, one rider climbing off his horse. No flash came from the lip. Neela watched for it and it did not come, and the thought turned over in her belly. Whoever waited beyond the cut had been promised a signal. They would be wondering now why the mirror was dark. A man who wonders sends someone to look.

Birds were already turning above the lip of the cliff, slow circles, and then more of them. And on the far ridge beyond the cut, a dot moved. One man, coming over the skyline and down toward the dark camp while the column still stood bunched on the shelf. The scrub took him. She watched the place where he had gone and he did not come out.

She could do nothing. By day she was a woman on a wall with bleeding feet, and the ridge was a morning's climb away. Her hands shook so hard she sat on them.

The riders came home at midmorning by the cart road. She watched from the terrace above the gate yard as they rode through the padippura in a column that held its shape too carefully. No horn. No man down. Muthu rode in front with a leather satchel across his knees. Behind him Devan sat his horse with his bad arm strapped and his face the colour of ash. In his right hand he held something small and bright, turning it over with his thumb.

He did not look at the terrace. He lifted his head and looked at the hills. He looked at them for a long time, at the notch in the east where the kites were still turning, and then along the ridge toward the second spur, and then at nothing.

Neela stayed very still against her wall. A thin cut on her shoulder, a night of fasting in her belly, one clean road thrown in a ravine: she did not feel brave. She felt like a woman who had put her own foot through a floor.

* * *

At supper she ate like a field hand.

Janu watched her go through the third plate and beamed. "There. Good. A wife should eat." She did not ask where Neela had been all day, and Neela did not tell her. The hall was loud with it anyway. The whole fort knew by noon.

"Two of them, mauled to the bone," said Muthu, tearing bread. "Bows, a signal mirror, a cold camp on the lip above the shelf. They were waiting for us, madam, I'd swear to it. And something got them first. A leopard, the men say, and I'll tell you, I've never seen a mark like—"

"A cat," said Devan.

He said it mildly, without looking up. Neela's hand stopped on the bread.

"A big one," said Muthu. "Fine. A *cat*."

"Left them, did it?" Janu said, and made a small sign against her chest. "Left the meat? Cats don't."

"This one did." Devan set a single coin on the table beside his plate. "Look at the edge."

Muthu picked it up, grumbling. Janu leaned. Neela did not need to lean. She knew what it looked like.

"Sharp," said Muthu. "So it's new. Loot, from some trader. Is that what you think?"

"I won't answer that."

Muthu opened his mouth and shut it. Devan took the coin back and turned it once under the lamp, and said nothing more. Neela noticed what he left out. He had ridden home with a satchel and a second tally and a long silence, and he gave the hall an edge and a sharp coin and no more. A man laying out a trap does not describe it to the room. Her eyes went, without her leave, around the whole hall: Chinna at the kitchen door with his bad hip, the servers with their pots, the open door to the yard where anyone might stand and listen.

"So who killed the scouts, then?" said Muthu. "And why leave the bag where we'd walk on it?"

"I don't know."

"Well, if it's a friend, I'd like to meet him."

"I'd like to thank him," said Devan.

He reached for the dish of fried fish and, without a glance, pushed it toward Neela's side of the table. "The pepper-store cat has been busy," he said.

Janu snorted. "That ginger rogue? He hasn't caught anything since the fever year."

"He's been busy." Devan's eyes came up, level and brown, and rested on Neela for the space of one breath. "Someone should leave him a bowl of milk. He's earned it."

It was said lightly. His face was grave. The corner of his mouth might have moved, or might not.

Neela looked at him over the rim of her cup and turned the sentence like a coin to find its edge. A joke at the table, over Janu's fish. A cat, the whole fort's cat, the one who walked the parapet and was fed from his plate. Or something else, so lightly handed across that no one but her could have picked it up.

For twelve days she had read his face, and she knew most of its weather. This she could not read.

"I'll see he gets it," she said.

"Do," said Devan, and went back to his rice.
