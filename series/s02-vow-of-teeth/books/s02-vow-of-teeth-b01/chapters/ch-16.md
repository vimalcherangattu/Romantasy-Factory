# Chapter 16: Terms Withdrawn

Devan stood in the middle of the pit with the practice staff in his good hand and could not finish the form.

He had come down after leaving her, with her voice still in his ears, and begun as he always did. Plant the left foot. Drop the weight. Let the staff speak. For an hour his body did it by rote while the rain beat the red earth to paste and the lamps guttered in their niches. He wondered, once, whether the slap of his palm on his shoulder carried round the curve of the wall to the west window. He did not know where she was. He did not let himself guess.

Then the rain thinned to a drizzle, and in the quiet something surfaced that he had not thought of in twelve years. His left foot stayed where it was, halfway through the third form. The strapped arm ached with each breath. That was not the trouble.

*I will make the offer once. I won't make it twice.*

He heard it in his own voice, level and unhurried, and then he heard it in someone else's.

At the border school, in his second year, an instructor had read the class a letter taken from a dead guild courier. It had been addressed to an odiyan who had balked. The letter was kind. That was what made the room go quiet. *Your silence about the matter pleases me, child, and I shall be pleased to go on keeping mine. I expect you will find a kindness of your own to return.* The instructor had folded it and said only, "That is how they do it. Nobody is ever struck. A debt is simply mentioned."

Devan had been twenty. He had thought it the vilest thing he had ever heard read aloud.

He set the staff's butt in the mud and leaned on it.

The offer had been meant as honesty. He had told her exactly what he would give and exactly what he wanted, and named it plain, so she could refuse it plain. And she had. *I wouldn't buy my life from a man who can take it with one word.* She had said it with a knife in her hand and the fur still drying on the boards. It was the truest thing anyone had said to him in eight years.

His silence for her name. A kindness, and a debt mentioned. A word kept behind his teeth for the day it became useful.

For eight years he had laid his hands on no living body, and he had told himself the rule was about his hands. Then came the west stair, the morning Janu found Asha, with Janu's scream rising from the court below and his sister's blood gone brown on the step. He had stood there and made a second vow, the one that stretched the first. *Whatever I am, I will not be the one who holds a throat.* A hand was not the only way to hold one. A word would do it, if it was the right word and you were the only man who could say it.

He had built a man around that vow. He had cut every kind of power off at the root so it could never be turned on anyone.

And at first light he had sat on the edge of his own bed, holding the one power he could not cut away, and put a price on it.

He had even been pleased with how fair it sounded.

"Warden?"

Muthu stood at the top of the north steps with a cloak over his head and a cheerful, careful face. "You've been stood there since the second clank. Gopan's laying wagers on whether you've died upright."

"I am considering."

"Considering what?"

"A mistake."

Muthu came down two steps. "Is it one of mine?"

"No." Devan looked at the lamps. "Mine. I'd like to mend it before dusk."

"Then I'll bring broth and not ask." Muthu went back up, and at the rim he stopped. "For what it's worth, Warden, you've the look of a man who has decided."

Devan hadn't. But when Muthu was gone he found he had planted his left foot. He ran the third form through once, badly, one arm tucked against his ribs, and stopped with his answer clear.

You cannot hand a woman her own cage back with terms attached. You hand it over, or you do not.

* * *

At dusk the room was grey, and the shutters stood open on a sky the colour of wet ash. Rain tapped the sill. The cistern chain clanked once, far below.

She was sitting on the chest at the foot of the bed with her back to the wall, where she could see both the door and the window. Her hair was wet. She had eaten; the rice bowl was clean and set aside. The knife lay across her knee. The cork flask sat beside her, and she was not looking at it.

He stopped inside the door and did not come farther.

"Before the light goes," he said. "I have something to take back."

Her eyes were on his hands. He turned them palm out, empty, and then felt foolish and let them hang.

"This morning I offered you my silence for a name." He kept his voice flat. It was the only way he knew to say a hard thing. "I called it a bargain. It wasn't. It was what the guild does. A word held over a person, and a price named for not saying it. I heard myself doing it and I did not stop. I have been trying all day to find a gentler way of putting that, and there isn't one."

She did not move. The rain went on.

"I withdraw it. The whole of it. There's no name to pay, now or later, and no favour owed. I won't ask for the name as the cost of anything." He drew a breath. "What I saw, I keep. That isn't a gift with a string. It's yours. You don't owe me for it, and I won't count it against you."

"You can say it any night you like." Her voice was low and had no heat in it. She was laying the fact on the table like a card. "That's what it is. You know that."

"Yes." He did not look away. "That's the one thing I can't mend. I can't unwitness myself. I can only tell you I won't, and let you watch whether I do." He nodded at the window. "The shutters are yours. Barred, open, whatever you want. I won't touch them. I'll sleep, or not, and I won't watch."

He turned to the door. His hand found the latch.

"Why?"

It came sharp and quick from behind him, the question of a woman who had been holding it all day and had finally run out of room to hold it.

He stopped, with his back to her and the latch cold under his thumb.

* * *

"Because I don't want to own anyone."

Neela weighed the words, rapping at them the way she'd rap a wall for a hollow. They rang solid all the way through.

He had not turned round. He said it to the door, quietly, and then did turn, and she saw his face in the last of the light. It was the same face as ever, brown and spare and shut, with a tiredness at the eyes that had nothing to do with the arm.

"That isn't an answer," she said. "That's a motto."

"It's the whole of it." He shrugged with one shoulder. "I was a bad master this morning. I would rather not be a better one."

"Everyone wants something."

"I do." He said it at once. "I want the name. I'd be a liar to say otherwise, and I don't lie to you. I want it badly. But I won't buy it, and I won't get it by sitting on you."

"And if I never give it?"

"Then I'll go on wanting it." The corner of his mouth moved, and was gone. "It won't be the worst thing I've wanted and not had."

She waited for the rest. The clause. The small, soft *but.* In eleven contracts she had never met a gift that didn't arrive with one, and she watched his mouth the way she'd watch a lock for the click.

It did not come. He nodded, as if she had answered him, and went out. His feet went away down the passage, unhurried, as they had that morning.

She sat in the grey room with the knife across her knee.

*Reread it*, she told herself, and did, the way she'd go back over a house after a job. The first half. All of it, from the night of the wedding.

The shutters had gone up barred at dusk from the second night, so no servant came upstairs and saw what climbed the sill. She had called it a private man's habit. Bhaskaran's lads called it privacy, Gopan called it Janu's old tale, Janu called it the damp, and Chinna ran a wager on it at the kitchen door.

Then the hounds. Fourteen of them had run the yard from dusk to dawn since before he was warden, and he had kennelled them from the second night. A dog bays at a cat on a wall. She had noticed that, and linked it to *Rain* at dusk under a clear sky, the one thing he wouldn't say plainly. She had not followed the link to the end. The sentry above the window had been pulled a few nights on, so no lantern could hang two spans over the ledge.

Then there was the cat. *Someone should leave him a bowl of milk. He's earned it.* She had heard that line across Janu's fish and taken it for a joke. But he had said the word every night. In the fever he had said it over and over, with his lids half open and nothing in his head to guard him, and it was always *cat*. Never the other word, the one that locked a cage. A man could say *cat* a thousand times and no one in the fort would think to hunt anything on their own hill.

Raiu stood at the stair head after dusk, with the corridor lamp left unlit beside him, so that no one in the passage would see a shadow cross the window.

Last of all, Janu's stories. Janu had told one at supper once, of a man-eater of the pass that left a traveller's sandals on the road. She was halfway through it over the rice when Devan said, "Not tonight, Janu." He said it quietly, with his eyes on his bowl. Janu had gone red and stopped, and the tale had never come back.

And after every morning Neela came in hollow and shaking, a bowl of rice had been waiting. She had thought it was Janu being Janu. She had never once asked who told Janu.

Her breath had got short. She put her hand flat on the cold lid of the chest and counted until it was even.

Each of them was a kindness. All of them together were a fence built stone by stone around a single thing, so carefully that no one could see the thing without looking at the fence.

Or they were one other thing, if you stood where she'd stood for twenty-one years.

Chathu had never struck a new intake. She remembered that now. He learned which girl cried at night, and left a tin of warm sweets by her mat, and asked after her cough. He saw that she was fed, and kept her from the older girls, and was gentle for years. Every girl in the yard would have walked into a cage for him. Then he read the page.

A caged odiyan would guard a pass. A caged odiyan would be a weapon no council could argue with. The one thing in the fort worth more caged than killed was her.

*Twenty nights*, said the quiet voice. *Nineteen now. He needs only wait for one dusk when you are most useful, and say it.*

And today he had given back the one thing she could use against him. He had made himself harmless in her sight. If that was Chathu's craft, it was the finest she had seen.

She couldn't prove it. That was what she hated. She couldn't prove it, and she could not disprove it either, and he had stood in the door and said *I want the name* in the same level voice he had used to say *rain*.

So there were two mistakes open to her. If she trusted him and was wrong, she lost her life, and Ammu's behind it, and the cage for every sister who ever took a kindly word at face value. If she distrusted him and he was honest, she lost only this, whatever this was. A man at a door who asked for nothing.

The first mistake ended her. The other only hurt. She would make the one that hurt, and make it on purpose, and she would not call it anything else.

A single night could not prove mercy. A patient man would behave beautifully tonight too. But tonight his word could not touch her, because with no resin on her there would be no other one to cage. What she would test was her body: a woman lying down human beside a stronger man who was awake. Would the sword move? Would a hand come near her? Would he reach for the knife in the gap by her hip?

She picked up the flask and held it to the window. The light was nearly gone. The cork sat tight in the neck, the bitter smell of scorched tamarind leaking round it.

It had a price, and she made herself read it off. No resin meant no other one on the second spur, and no report left in the cup of the meeting stone. She had missed it once already, on the fever night, and her mark on the night of the narrows had come late. Chathu had said every day counted double now. A second bare cup would tell Ammu that something was wrong, and Ammu would come asking. Another night went unmarked against the moon, too, and the ache, nine or ten days off by her count of rationed nights, kept its own ledger.

She would pay it, and she corked the flask harder than she needed to and pushed it deep into the fold of her cloth.

The dark came on. The cistern chain clanked. The lamp stayed unlit on the chest. Somewhere in the passage a door closed, and some time after, the latch of their own door lifted, and she heard him come in.

She did not turn. She lay on her side with her back to the room and her face to the wall, in her day-shift, with her hands and throat clean. She had never felt so naked in her life. The knife lay in the gap between mattress and frame where it always did. She did not touch it, though her fingers wanted to, and she kept them curled under her chin.

Behind her the boards creaked. Something was set down on the floor with the soft knock of a steel pommel on wood. Hilt toward her. It had always been hilt toward her.

She could not tell any more if it was trust or a leash with a handle.

The bed shifted at the far edge. Sunset had come and gone, and her body noticed. It began as an itch deep in the old scars at throat and wrist, the skin expecting a burn it was owed. She pressed her wrists against her breastbone and breathed through it with her teeth together, and it faded. Craving, and nothing more. There was no resin on her for it to turn.

He lay down on his back, and the quilt rustled. She listened for the thing she had come to test. No hand. No creak of the sword lifted from the boards. No shift of weight toward the frame and the knife. He lay as far from her as the bed allowed, and he did not move at all.

His breath was too even. For a long time it ran smooth as a ruled line, with no catch in it anywhere, and she knew that was a man taking care. Then, a third of the way through a long count, the hitch came. Slow. Deliberate. Exactly where it had always been, and exactly as long.

The hitch had never been sleep. It had been him, all twenty nights, and now he was giving it to her on purpose, so she could count him.

So she counted. She lay still with her eyes open on the wall and counted that catch into the dark, over and over, and could not make the number come out to mercy or to patience.

Somewhere past midnight she counted something else. Grey was five hours off. At grey the watcher in the pass village would walk the hill's edge and read her posts, and find not one of them fresh. A second refusal, written down for Chathu in a stranger's hand. The cup at the stone would be bare, and Ammu would be climbing the spur by noon.

She closed her eyes on it, human, with her back to him and the knife untouched.
