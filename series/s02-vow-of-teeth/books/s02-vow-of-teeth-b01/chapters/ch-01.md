# Chapter 1: The Kasavu Bride

Four exits. The stair behind her, the wall-walk running east, the wall-walk running west, and the drop to the pepper terraces, which only counted if she didn't mind breaking something.

Neela counted them again while the sun went down red behind the Kettamala rampart. Then she held out her left hand, because a man in a grey mundu was waiting to put a bangle on it.

"Your wrist," Devan said.

He didn't take it. He waited for her to turn it over. That was the first thing she noted about him: he asked before he touched.

The ridged scar lay pale on the inside of her wrist, the shape of a drawn-out thumbprint. Resin had made it, and a hundred nights like the one coming. He saw it. His eyes stopped there for exactly as long as it took to decide not to ask, and then he slid the plain gold bangle over her hand and closed the hinge. The metal was warm from his palm. It covered the scar to the last ridge.

"It sits wrong," he said. "I had it made from a measure the council sent."

"It sits fine."

"You haven't tried to move your hand."

She flexed her fingers. The bangle slid a thumb's width and stopped. "Fine," she said again.

Something in his mouth might have been the start of a smile. It went nowhere. Behind them the whole fort stood in the last light, a few hundred faces in the courtyard below, and an old woman with a lamp stood closest of all, breathing hard through her nose.

* * *

Twelve.

That was the number she held under every other number. The twelfth contract, and she was Seventeen in a ledger a day's ride off, a girl in the book of the Resin House with a page that could be read aloud. Twelve contracts earned release. Release meant the page burned and a birth name handed back to her. She had never known that name. Not one syllable of it.

The Monkey Pass closed in about forty days. The rains came and sealed the cart road, and nothing she did after that would matter to anyone who'd paid for it.

So: forty days. A man who drilled alone at dawn. A fort with one great gate and sixty-odd soldiers. A wedding that cost somebody a great deal of money.

"Thirty-two," the old woman muttered, close enough to be heard, far enough to pretend she hadn't been. This was Janu, steward of the fort, who had been dabbing at her eyes with the end of her shawl since the first vow. "Thirty-two, and thin as a pepper vine in drought. I told him. I said, you'll eat at your own wedding, and look."

"Janu," Devan said, not turning.

"And her." Janu's whisper climbed. "Twenty-seven and not one word. Not a word through the whole rite. A girl that quiet is either holy or sulking."

"Neither," Neela said.

Janu's mouth shut with a click. Devan looked at the sky, and the corner of his mouth moved again.

"Then you can speak," he said.

"When there's something worth saying."

"I'll note it," said the warden of Kettamala, "for the council."

* * *

The feast ran long, as feasts did.

Neela sat on the dais in her ivory kasavu with its gold border heavy across her shoulder and collected names. It was an old habit and a stupid one, and she had never been able to put it down. The guild had given her a number. The least she could do was refuse to give the same to anyone else.

Kannan was the goat-boy, nine or ten, plastered against a pillar with a garland he'd been told to carry and had clearly decided to keep. Chinna ran the kitchen door, a broad woman who slapped the leaf plates down like a judge handing out sentences. Raiu held the corridor torch outside the hall, a young guard with a bad haircut and an excellent memory for who had gone past him and when.

Four exits from the rampart. Seven from the hall, if you counted the roof. She counted the roof.

Devan ate little. She watched him do it. He moved rice from one side of his leaf to the other and spoke to nobody who wasn't brought to him, and when a captain with a laugh like a dropped pot clapped him on the shoulder, he allowed it the way a man allows weather.

"Captain Muthu," he said, when she asked. "My second."

"Does he sleep in the fort?"

"Yes. Do you want his hours?"

She hadn't expected that. "Do you offer them to everyone?"

"Only to people who ask." He took a mouthful of rice, chewed, swallowed. "Mostly he's in the lower barracks. He wakes me for the dawn watch, twice a week. He will not tonight."

"No," Neela said. "I suppose he won't."

Later, when the lamps burned low and the singing had turned to the kind that needed no words, he walked her down the corridor to the warden's chamber. Raiu stood at the turning with his torch.

"Thank you, Raiu," she said. "Mind the draught by the stair. It'll gutter your flame."

The young guard flushed to the ears and said something that wasn't quite a word.

Devan had stopped. He didn't stop for much, she'd noticed, but he'd stopped for this. He looked at Raiu, then at her, and for a moment the careful flatness of his face gave way to something she could only call attention.

"Nobody presented him to you," he said. "You never went near him at the feast."

"No."

"He's been on this corridor since dusk. How did you learn his name so fast?"

She could have said that Janu said it. She could have said she'd overheard. She said, "How long did it take you to learn it?"

He was silent a moment. Raiu looked at the ceiling.

"Four months," Devan said at last. "I had to ask twice."

"Then ask me again tomorrow," Neela said, and went into the chamber ahead of him.

* * *

She'd meant to be quicker about it. But the guild's rule was to learn a room before you needed it, and she stood just inside the door and did that while he latched it behind them.

A high room, whitewashed over laterite. One broad bed. A low lamp, sesame-scented, on a stand. A brass kindi beside the pillow, its spout bright. A sword on a rack and nothing else on the wall. And across from her, taking up the whole west side, three tall teak shutters, closed against the dark.

Behind the shutters, she knew, was a ledge broad enough for a crouching body. The guild's drawings had said so. They hadn't said the wood was old and the ledge worn smooth by weather.

Chathu's voice came to her then, the way it did when she was careful and alone. It was soft as always, the patient voice of a man reading her a column of figures she had no choice but to accept. *Like a leopard kill, child. Throat and floor, and tell the rest as the beast would. And not before the marriage writ comes back sealed from Kodiyur, mark that. Not after the rains. The buyer is particular.*

The tooth was sewn into her waistband. It was a real one, yellow and worn, and she was to leave it in the wound. She could feel it there against her hip bone, a small hard question.

"You can ask," Devan said.

She turned. He'd taken off the wedding cloth and stood in his plain mundu, shoulders square, looking at her across the width of the room as if it were a distance he intended to respect.

"Ask what?"

"Whatever you've been counting since you came in." A pause. "You do count. Your lips move."

*Damn.* "How many guards on the stair at night?"

"Two on the west stair. Four at the padippura. Six on the wall, in pairs, changing at the cistern chain."

"The chain?"

"It clanks on the hour. They change on the third clank after the middle watch."

She stared. "You're telling me."

"You asked."

"You're the warden. Wouldn't you rather I didn't know?"

"I would rather you asked me than found it out." He said it flatly, without warmth, which made it somehow harder to dismiss. "Is there anything else?"

She made herself ask the real question, wrapped in a harmless one. "Why did you agree to this? A bride from a Kodiyur pepper house you've never met."

"The council pressed the match. I couldn't refuse without saying why." He stopped. "I won't answer the rest of that."

"Which rest?"

"The why."

It wasn't comfort. It wasn't even courtesy, exactly. It was a closed door with a plain label on it, and she found, to her annoyance, that she would rather have that than a smooth lie.

"The shutters," she said. "Do they stick?"

"The third slat creaks." He glanced toward the window. "I've never fixed it."

"Why not?"

"I like to know when the wind comes in."

It was an answer, and it told her nothing. She noted that, too.

The bed was wide. There was no way to ask which side he took without the question meaning something, so she looked at the floor and he said, "The left is mine," and went around to it without looking at her. She sat on the right, where the lamp's reach ended. Her hands lay in her lap, her left one heavy with gold.

Then he did something she hadn't planned for.

He took the sword down from its rack. He drew it half an inch, checked the edge with his thumb, and slid it home. He knelt, and laid it on the floor on her side of the bed, along the edge of the sleeping-mat, with the hilt toward her hand.

"What is that for?" she said.

"I sleep badly."

"That isn't an answer."

"It's what I'm going to say." He rose and went round to his own side. "If you wake and find me restless, don't touch me. That's all."

She looked at the hilt. Plain wire grip, worn at the thumb. It lay within reach of her right hand, and he had put it there with his own, in front of her, with the lamp still lit.

Either he was a fool, or he wanted her to think so, or he wanted to see what she did with it. Chathu had taught her which of those to bet on.

"Good night, Devan," she said.

"Good night, Neela."

He pinched the lamp himself. Dark rushed in, with the smell of hot oil and a draught from the third slat.

* * *

She lay on her back and counted his breaths.

One to ten, steady. Eleven to forty, slowing. By a hundred he had gone still in the particular way of a man who has given up fighting the dark and let it put him down. Twice his breath snagged and smoothed. At two hundred and twelve there was a catch, a hitch like a bucket knocking a well-wall, and she held every muscle until it passed.

Two hundred and sixty. Two hundred and ninety. Three hundred.

She didn't move yet. She gave him thirty more, because thirty was cheap.

There was no choosing tonight. The guild kept a watcher in the pass village, and every dawn he walked the hill edge and read which of an odiyan's marks had gone fresh. A first night with none read as refusal. Refusal went to Chathu, and Chathu answered refusal with a balance sheet. So the other one had to be on the hill before light, and Neela had to get her there past a sleeper.

She rose slowly, one hand flat on the mattress to take the weight, and stepped over the hilt of his sword. It stayed where he'd left it. He did not stir.

The floor was cold. She crossed it in her bare feet and stopped at the west window with the dark all round her and her pulse in her ears. The shutters were old teak, oiled and dark, held only by an iron latch. The long teak bar hung unused on its hooks beside them, and she noted that: a man who expected nobody at his window. She lifted the latch, eased the first shutter, then the second.

The third she did on purpose. She laid two fingers on the slat and leaned, and it creaked, a small dry crack like a knuckle. Better to learn now, with her clothes on, whether the sound woke him. She stood with her hand on the frame and counted twenty. Behind her the breathing went on, long and even, unbroken.

*Asleep,* she told herself. *He's asleep.* And she believed it, because she needed to, which was the kind of belief she'd been trained not to trust. A man who has trained himself to sleep awake does not change his breathing either.

Beyond the shutters lay the ledge and the night. The sky was black and full of stars, and the wind came up from the pass smelling of wet red laterite and pepper leaf. Somewhere below, the cistern chain clanked the hour. A dog barked once and was hushed.

She slid the bangle off her left wrist and set it on the sill. It was still warm. For a moment she looked at it, a small round weight of gold that a stranger had closed around her like a promise, and then she took the corked flask from the fold of her cloth and drew the stopper.

The smell struck first. Scorched tamarind and pine sap, bitter as a mouthful of ash. It was the smell of the Resin House and of every night she had ever been good at her work. It was the smell of being useful.

She shrugged off the wedding cloth and folded it on the sill. The wind raised gooseflesh along her arms.

One rule Chathu had drilled harder than the knife. Anyone whose eyes were on her for the next minute became a witness. A witness needed one word, said in her hearing while she wore the other shape and the night still held, and she would wear it for life. She already carried two witnesses, Chathu and Ammu, the way she carried her own name for the dead. A third would hold a cage in his mouth every night she slept under his roof. If the man behind her was awake, that was what she was handing him.

She tipped the flask against her fingers and the resin ran black and slow, and she drew it across the ridged scar on her right wrist, then the left, then up the line of her throat where the oldest marks lay.

For a breath, nothing.

Then the burn took her.

It came in like a hot wire drawn through each scar, from wrist to elbow, from throat to sternum, and she bit down on her own lip until she tasted blood. She had done this a hundred times and she had never once become used to it. Heat crawled up her arms. Her knees went, and she caught the sill. The stars smeared. Her vision narrowed to a long dark tunnel with the wind at the end of it, rich with a thousand smells she had no name for.

Behind her, in the room she'd mapped, a man lay breathing or pretending to.

Her spine began to bend.
