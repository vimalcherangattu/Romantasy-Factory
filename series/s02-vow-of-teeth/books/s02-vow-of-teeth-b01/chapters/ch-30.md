# Chapter 30: The Tooth

The cistern chain struck for dusk, and Neela lifted the bar off the west shutters herself.

It was heavy teak, black with years of hands. She leaned it against the wall where she had leaned it every dusk since the second night, and she swung both leaves wide. The third slat creaked. Rain blew in at once, a cold fine spray across the floor and her bare feet. The sky beyond was the colour of wet slate. The monsoon had no use for evenings, so it simply went dark.

She did not close them. She did not look at the door or count the steps to it. Six. She knew it was six.

Behind her the bed ropes creaked.

"The bar's off," Devan said.

"It is."

"It's raining in."

"It has been all day." She hooked the cotton curtain back on its peg.

He sat against the wall with a blanket over his knees, which Eravi allowed and Janu did not. His ribs were bound under a clean tunic. Yesterday he had stood in a courtyard and arrested a councillor of Varanad. Tonight he looked like a man who had been wrung out and hung up to dry. A river pebble sat in his palm, and he closed his fingers over it when she turned.

"I'm not turning tonight," she said. "Ammu's resin is in its pouch and it can stay there. I wanted the window open with nothing behind it. Just me, and you, and the rain."

"Your nails."

"Are bad. I'll pay for it." She crossed to the bed and sat on its edge, with the open window at her back. For forty days she had never sat with her back to a window at dusk. Her shoulders waited for something to happen to them. Nothing did.

"I owe you a thing," she said. "Two things. The first is smaller."

She opened her fist.

The tooth lay on her palm, yellow as old ivory, as long as her thumb joint. She had unpicked it from her waistband that afternoon with her own teeth and a needle, and the stitches still clung to its root like a stem.

"It came from the House store," she said. "A real one, off a real beast. It was meant to go in your wound. A physician finds a tooth, the fort says a cat, and no one asks why the warden's bride slept through it." She kept her voice level. "I sewed it into my waistband the day I left Kodiyur. I wore it to our wedding."

He looked at it for a long moment.

"Tell me the rest," he said. "All of it. I won't stop you."

So she did.

She told it in order, as she would have made a report. The contract, and the twelfth place on a list, and what twelve bought: her page burned, her name returned. The pass closing in forty days. A chamber with shutters facing west and a ledge broad enough for a crouching body. How she had counted his guards through the wedding feast while the drums played. How the House sent a tin of sweets before every kill and called it luck. Chathu's last message, that when the pass closed with the account open, he would force the resin and read her page aloud at dusk.

"I didn't know who bought it," she said. "The House never tells. I learned it was Ittan from a slip of paper. That's why it's his name I gave you."

"I know."

"I never did it. I want you to hear that I never did it, and also that I meant to. Both are true." Her thumb moved on the tooth. "I stood over you the first week with a knife six inches from my hand, and I wasn't thinking of you. I was thinking of a number. I'd been a number for twenty years."

"And now?"

"Now I'm in a window with the shutters open." Her mouth twisted. "You'll have to take that as an answer."

"I'll take it." He held out his hand. "May I?"

She tipped it into his palm. He turned it to the lamp and closed his fingers around it, and the point went into the pad of his thumb.

A bead of red came up on his skin. At the same instant a sharp sting opened on her own thumb, and a thin welt rose, bright as a thread.

They looked at each other.

"You did that on purpose," she said.

"I did not."

"You've bled on me before I've had my supper." She was laughing, and it was a ridiculous noise. She put her thumb in her mouth. "Put it somewhere safe, Devan. Somewhere that isn't your hand."

He wrapped it in a corner of the blanket. His mouth moved, a little. Then it went still.

"Ask your question," he said. "You've been carrying one since the third day."

* * *

She had not known he knew.

"You said you wouldn't answer," she said. "When I asked why you bar the shutters. And the next dusk you said 'rain.' It was clear sky from here to the sea."

"It wasn't an answer. I knew it wasn't. It was the closest I had to quiet." He turned the wrapped tooth over once. "You may use your one refusal in the question game, remember. I've never used mine. I'll not use it tonight."

"Then why?"

"The first night I left the shutters as they were. Open, as my father kept them. I watched you climb up onto the sill." His voice was even. "Raiu was at the corridor torch—"

He stopped. The rain filled the pause. Neither of them looked at the door.

"Raiu was at the corridor torch," he said again, slower. "He'd have been the first to look up. The sentry walked the parapet above. Janu's girls carry water to that door. If any of them had looked, that person would have been your witness. A witness has the word in his mouth for good. I did not want one more person in this fort to carry that."

"So you barred them."

"The second dusk I barred them. The hounds went to the kennel within a few nights, the sentry after them. Janu's stories took me longest." A faint pull at the corner of his mouth. "I asked her at the table, by day. She was very hurt. I told her I was tired of leopards."

"You never said the word to me."

"I said it to Janu, in a kitchen, with the sun up. You weren't in the room." He looked at her. "I'd decided before I picked up the bar. Whatever I'd seen, I would not use it. Not on you, not for the council, not to bargain. If the fort thought its warden mad and its bride cold, that was cheap. I wanted the thing to stay mine to hold, and no one else's."

"You didn't tell me."

"I didn't know how to say it so you'd believe it." His voice was dry. "You'd just spent the evening counting my doors."

She laughed, and it hurt, and she let it. Rain ran in at the sill and found the back of her neck. She did not shift away from it.

"Ask me another," she said. "Your turn."

"Do you want to know when I stopped being afraid of you?"

"Were you afraid?"

"Yes. For about nine days." He said it the way he would have reported a patrol. "Do you want to know when it stopped?"

"I do."

"You came to the bed in the dark." He did not look away. "You thought I was asleep. I wasn't. You put your fingers at the base of my skull, there." He touched his own nape, just under the bone. "I know that point. Any kalari man does. It's quick. You pressed until I felt it, and you counted."

She had not breathed. Her mouth was dry.

"Three breaths," he said. "I felt you count them. Your lips moved against my hair. Then you took your hand away and lay down and said nothing, and in the morning you were at the window with your face turned out as if it were any other morning."

"Your breath was too even," she said slowly. "It hitches. A third of the way through each count, I've listened to it for weeks. It didn't, that night. I thought you were deeper than usual."

"I was holding it still."

"You were letting me." The room had gone very quiet. "You lay there and let me."

"I was tired of being the wall." There was no weight in it, and that was the weight. "And then I thought, no, I'd like to see what she does with her hand. I had a great deal of reasoning about you by then. Reasoning is not knowing. That was the night I knew."

She had nothing ready to say. It was the first time in her life someone had told her what he had seen when she thought no one was watching, and it had not been a bill.

* * *

"I thought it would be a leash," she said.

Her voice came out strange. She went on anyway.

"Chathu watched me turn the first time, and every night after that I was a thing someone could say a word over. Ammu saw it too. Ammu never said it. But I never stopped knowing she could." She pressed her thumb to the welt.

"And now?"

She looked at him: the bound ribs, the bruised hands, the pebble he had forgotten in his fist. Behind her the window stood open on the dark.

"There's rain on my neck and a man who's seen all of me," she said, "and I'm not counting the door."

"Six," Devan said.

"Six," she agreed, and nearly laughed again. "You've known it was six all along."

"Since the first night."

"You put a stone in your cheek every resin night since the rampart. So a sleeper's mouth wouldn't slip."

"It was a small thing."

"It wasn't." She turned and took his hand off the blanket. "Yesterday you took the warden's power and used it, in front of sixty soldiers, on a councillor of the realm. I watched your face. I expected you to come back colder."

"I thought if I ever used it, I'd become what used it on Asha." His thumb moved across her knuckles. "Nothing since has made me less myself. I think that's you, not me. I wrote it with your name beside mine. I wasn't alone with it."

"No," she said. "You weren't."

His hand stayed over hers. That was the comfort. He had never pretended to offer another kind.

* * *

She had meant to say it carefully. She had practised in her head, on the ride and on the stair and while she unpicked the tooth. In the end it came out the way things came out of her now, which was plainly.

"I love you."

His fingers tightened on hers once.

"I don't know when it started. I could name a night. I could name a morning, but I'd be guessing." The rain was loud on the sill. "You asked once what I wanted, and I said to be left alone, and that was a lie. I wanted somebody to leave the shutters open and not look. I'd have said it was the worst thing a person could want."

"It's not."

"I know. You taught me. I love you, Devan."

He did not answer at once. He had never in his life filled a silence with something untrue, and she watched him choose the words.

"I love you," he said. "I said it to my sister, badly, in the kitchen, the week before she died. I haven't said it to anyone since. I don't say a thing I haven't measured. I've measured this one for a long time."

She believed him. That was the strange part. She had spent her life weighing what people said against what they wanted, and she had come to the end of that sum with him and found it balanced.

"Say it again," she said unsteadily. "So I can check."

"I love you."

"It holds."

"It does." The corner of his mouth moved. "Neela is the name I have for you."

"It's the one I'd keep," she said. "Even if I get another."

Then she kissed him, in the open window at dusk, with the shutters wide and the rain on her back and the dark coming up the gorge. He kissed her back with a care she had come to know, and winced once against her mouth. A thread of heat pulled along her left ribs, which was his, and she drew back and laughed against his cheek.

"Sit still. You're bleeding on both of us."

"Unavoidable." He leaned his forehead on hers. "I think I'll learn it."

* * *

It was a long time before she said the last of it. She had to say it to the lamp first, and then to the floor. Her hand went to the pouch at her waist, to the small hard thumb of Ammu's resin, and rested there.

"I want to ask you for something."

"Ask."

"You've never had to say yes to me. Nobody has. You made your vow at sunset with a council behind you, and I made mine under a name that wasn't mine, with a knife in my cloth." She made herself lift her eyes. "I want to be married to you. Not because I was sold. I want a morning one."

He was quiet.

"Tomorrow, at noon," she said. "In the courtyard. In front of Janu, and Muthu, and Kannan and the goat. Rain or none. With my own words, and every person in this house standing there who's ever carried my water." Her fingers closed on the pouch. "Ammu should have been at my first one. She'll hear this one."

"Neela." He did not say it gently. He said it exactly. "Muthu will stand in that courtyard. Whatever you say goes into a grey-sealed letter. It waits four months for the road, and then it goes to whoever reads them. Your own words, in your own voice. Ittan asked whose hand he was. Someone will want to know whose hand you are."

"I know."

"You've spent twenty years not being written down."

"And you've just watched me sign a crown writ." Her voice caught, and she let it. "They'll write it either way. I'd rather they copy something I said. I've never asked anyone for a thing I couldn't take. I'm asking."

"You are." He looked at her as he looked at weather, closely and with respect. "I'd have said yes from the second word, you know. I was waiting to see the third."

"You were not."

"I was. I've been waiting to see the third word of everything you say for six weeks." His hand turned under hers and held it, palm to palm, the bangle between them. "Yes. Noon. I'll stand where you tell me."

"You'll stand next to me."

"I'll stand wherever there's room for the two of us." He tilted his head toward the open window, the rain, the black shoulder of the pass. "I have only said a vow once, and there was something behind it. I don't know what one weighs with nothing hidden."

"Neither do I," she said. "That's why I want to say it."

The lamp burned low. Beyond the shutters the last grey went out of the sky, and she sat with her back to the dark and the dark did nothing, because the one person who could have used it was holding her hand.

Far below, across the wet court, one window still showed light. The guardroom lamp. Ittan was awake down there, with four months of rain between him and any rider, and nothing to do but wait for whatever he knew to be asked of him.

Her fingers found the pouch again. Tomorrow at noon she would say her name in front of Muthu. Tomorrow at dusk she would open this and turn in front of Devan on purpose, and at dawn she would have to turn back where he could see.

She watched the small yellow light until the rain blurred it. She did not count the door.
