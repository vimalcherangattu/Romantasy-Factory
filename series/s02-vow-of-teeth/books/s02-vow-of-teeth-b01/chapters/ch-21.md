# Chapter 21: The Captain at Twenty-Four

"Why won't you spar?"

Neela asked it without looking up, the way another woman might ask about the price of thread.

Rain had shut Kettamala since before dawn. It came down the red walls in sheets and filled the kalari pit to the knee, and Devan had taken refuge in the tack room beside it, where the thatch leaked in two places and he had set a bucket under each. He was restringing a bow. Neela had come down the east path under a sack, with a pot of tea and no explanation, and sat on an upturned feed bin as if she had always sat there.

Muthu had left a quarter-hour ago, streaming water, laughing. He had asked for his round on the seventh day, as he had every seventh day for eight years, and Devan had said no, as he had every time. Neela had watched the whole exchange over the rim of her cup.

"I'm not going to answer that yet," Devan said.

"Then we need a rule." She set the cup down. "A question for a question. You ask, I answer. I ask, you answer. Either of us can say *I won't answer* once, and the other lets it lie."

"Once a day?"

"Once. Total."

He tested the string against his thumb. He had spent half his life refusing to be asked things, and he found he was curious what it felt like to be asked by someone who offered to pay.

"Start small," he said. "Then I'll know what the price is."

She considered. "Ask."

"What did they feed you at the Resin House?"

Her mouth twitched. "Boiled millet. Dried fish on feast days. Before a contract, a tin of jaggery-sesame sweets, still warm." The twitch went flat. "I don't eat them now."

"Mine," she said. "Who fills the kindi?"

"I do."

"Every night?"

"Since the second week. Spout toward the pillow, because you reach for it left-handed in the dark." He said it to the bow. "You thought it was Janu's girls."

"I did."

"They'd have put it on my side."

She said nothing for a moment. The rain drummed on the thatch, and a drip landed in the left bucket with a sound like a coin.

"That's two," she said. "Ask."

He asked what she had been afraid of at the House, as a child. She said the kennels, and a big man's shadow in the yard, and being the last one picked for anything. She said it fast and bare, a list.

Her questions came sharper. Which of Janu's stories had made him ask her to stop? He told her: *the one about the man-eater of the pass that left a man's sandals on the road.* Which scar was the oldest? Whether he slept.

"Badly," he said. "Eight years."

"Since Asha."

"Before Asha." He ran the string through his fingers. "Since the ford."

She let that lie. It was his turn, and he had been wanting to ask one thing since the first week.

"Why do you take the turn on the eleventh step?"

She had been reaching for her cup. Her hand stopped.

"The stair to the hall," he said. "Fourteen steps. You're light on ten, and on the eleventh your whole body turns, as if you'd heard something there. Every time."

"I don't know."

"You don't—"

"I don't know." Her lips moved, a small shaped count with no sound in it, and her thumbnail dragged along the edge of her forefinger nail, back and forth. "It bothers me. I've tried to stop and I can't. My feet do it." She saw him looking at her hands and put them in her lap. "That isn't a refusal. I've still got my one."

He thought of Asha, and let the thought go, since it was not hers. He nodded and went back to the bow. She breathed out, and he understood that he had been given something and had been wise enough not to pick it up.

She looked up at him then. She did not ask what the ford was. She set her cup on the bin, and he knew the next question was coming before she opened her mouth.

"Why won't you spar? Not *can't.* You could break any man in this fort. Muthu asks every seventh day and you look at him as if he'd offered you poison."

It was the cheapest-looking question of the afternoon. He had known since the first minute that it was the one she had come down the hill to ask.

He could say *I won't answer that.* She had given him the right, and she would keep her word. He laid the bow across his knees.

"I was twenty-four," he said. "Eight years ago, in spring. We took a raider captain at the lower ford after a village burned. Twelve dead, and children among them. He fought like a bull until Muthu opened his arm, and then he dropped his sword and knelt in the mud and put up his hands."

Neela did not move.

"That is the law. A man who yields is a prisoner. I'd taught it to every recruit in the fort. I was standing over him with a bleeding hand, and he looked up and spat on my boot. Then he said what he'd done in that village, in detail, in a pleasant voice." Devan turned the bow in his hands. "I put two fingers to the notch below his ear. A marma point. Done right, it stops the heart in the time it takes to say a short word. I knew where it was. I'd been taught what it was for."

The rain went on.

"He was dead before he finished falling. Muthu was ten paces off and saw all of it. Nobody said anything. The captain was in the wrong, and I was in the wrong, and the village was still burned." He kept his voice level, a report. "I haven't struck a living person since. Not in practice, not in anger. I drill alone. If I held a sword against Muthu, I'd know exactly where my hand wanted to go."

He waited for the words he had heard from Janu and from his own head. *You were provoked. Anyone would have.*

Neela said, "He was on his knees with his hands up. You hit the point under his ear with two fingers and you killed him. He had surrendered and you had the right to hold him, and you chose the strike."

It was exactly what he had done. Hearing it said plain was like stepping into the cold pit water.

"And then you stopped," she said. "You didn't do it twice. You didn't do it to the next man who made you angry, or the one after that. You've had that hand and that rage, and every day since, you've stopped." She tilted her head. "I'm not saying it makes it smaller. I'm saying I see both. He's dead and you stopped. Neither cancels the other."

Devan found his eyes were stinging, and he was irritated, and then he was not. Nobody had forgiven him, because he had not let anyone get near enough. Janu had tried. Muthu had tried with jokes. They had all wanted to lift the weight off. This woman had looked at it, named each part of it correctly, and left it exactly where it lay.

He picked the bow up off his knees. He found the loose end of the string he had abandoned and began to wind it, round and round the nock, and his hands were steady again.

"That's the first time anyone has said it that way," he said.

"It's the only way it's true."

He nodded. His throat was not quite his own.

"My turn," she said, "and I'll give you one before you ask. You think it's the hand you don't trust."

"Yes."

"It isn't." She spoke gently, which was worse. "A man who didn't trust his hand would spar with a padded stick and find out. You won't touch anyone, because you're afraid of what you'd learn. That it isn't about the hand at all. It's about wanting to."

He should have been angry. He sat very still and let it be true.

"That's an answer, not a question," he said.

"Then here's the rest, and it's not a question either." She wrapped her hands round the cup. "I count the doors in every room I sleep in. Twenty-one years. I've never once been asked why."

Nothing came to him. He thought of a man who lay down each night with his back to a wall and his sword on the floor, every door in the fort watched. He thought of her at the foot of a stair, counting. Neither of them said what the two had in common, and he saw that she had not expected him to.

He drew the knot tight and did not bargain over it. "What do they call you? At the House. Not the cover. Not the Chirayil ward."

"Nothing."

"You don't have to—"

"I'm not refusing. I mean nothing." She turned the cup in her fingers. "I'm Seventeen. The seventeenth girl in the House's book. I answered to it for twenty-one years, the way a goat answers to a whistle. There was a name before that. I was six when they took me, and I lost it somewhere on the road. Chathu has it in a ledger, I think. He's never offered."

Seventeen. He turned the number over and found that it sat in his chest like a stone he had swallowed.

"So I learned everyone else's," she said, and shrugged. "It's a bad habit in my trade. A woman who wants to disappear should leave every servant a stranger. But I kept finding out. Raiu's mother is dead. Chinna's got a bad hip. Kannan's goat is called Pepper and bites. Unni's mother sells salt fish." Her shoulder did not quite make it up. "Somebody should know them. Nobody ever knew me."

He had watched her do it since the first week. He had watched her say *Raiu* to the boy at the stair-head and see him straighten. He had taken it for craft, a spy's easy charm. She was collecting names for people the way other women kept birds, and every name was a thing she had never been given.

"Neela," he said.

She went quiet.

"That's yours," he said. "I know it's the cover's. I've said it every day since the wedding, and it's the one I'd answer to if it were mine." He stopped. "I'm saying it badly."

"You're saying it fine." Her voice was not steady. She picked up the pot and poured tea nobody had asked for. "Don't do that again until I've finished this."

He didn't. They drank in the sound of the rain.

* * *

She did not take the resin that night.

She said so at dusk, in the dim of the chamber, with the flask stoppered and standing in the niche where it lived. "There's nothing to hunt in this, and the scent's washed off the hill. I'd just be wet." Then, more quietly, to the flask: "Another bare night. It goes on the count."

Her nails had been itching all afternoon. He had seen her pressing them against her thumb. He said nothing about that, and she gave him a look that thanked him for it. He kept a tally of her bare nights, as she kept her steps, without ever having decided to, and he knew what a full moon of them would do to her hands.

He kept the pebble in its pouch. Tonight, for once, he would not need the stone.

She slept first. She slept the way she did everything, by the numbers, flat on her back with one hand over her breastbone. He lay beside her and listened to his own breath hitch and to the cistern chain striking the third watch. The rain had thinned to a whisper on the slats.

Past the middle watch she woke. He knew it by the change in her breathing. She did not move, except that her right hand came up and pressed her nails hard against her thumb, one after another, as if testing them for cracks.

"Eight," she said, to the dark. Flat, a sum. "Eight nights to the new moon."

He did not know what ended on the eighth night. He knew that she did, and that she had said it to the ceiling and not to him.

The question was there at once, whole. *What happens then?* He could ask it. She was awake, he had asked her harder things that day, and the right to refuse was already spent on neither side. He lay with it behind his teeth. She had not offered it, and a thing taken from her in the dark was the leash she feared. He would hold it through the night, and through whatever came of tomorrow, and spend it only if she turned to him first.

It cost him. He felt every breath of it.

Presently her hand fell back to her chest, and she slept again.

Later, much later, she began to count something else.

It was barely sound. Her lips shaped it more than her voice did. "Four. Five." A pause, a catch. "Six."

He turned his head on the pillow, slowly, and did not breathe.

"Seven. Eight." The cadence was careful, a step and a step, a child going down into water. Her hand had slid off her chest and lay open on the sheet, the fingers curling. "Nine. Ten."

She stopped.

He heard her swallow. Then a small sound came out of her, high and wet, a sound he had heard once from a hound that had come home across the ford with a broken leg. Her brow pulled together.

"Don't," she whispered. "It's broken."

Her fingers closed in the sheet. A tremor went down her whole arm.

"It's *broken.*"

Devan held still. Every part of him wanted to reach for her, and he did not. A woman waking in a dream is a woman who does not know where she is, and he had no notion whether the hand that touched her would be his own in her mind. He thought of the Resin House. Of kennels, and the big man's shadow in the yard she had listed so fast, and a tin of warm sweets with a bill inside. Whatever was broken in there, the guild had done the breaking. Some stair in that yard, some girl's bone. She would have grown up counting steps toward the place where the bad thing waited.

After a time her breath came back down to a slow measure, and her fingers eased open. She did not say the number again.

He lay awake and watched the dark ceiling, and the thing he had refused to look at for eight years came up out of him and stood in the room.

Asha had counted stairs. She had said numbers kept the dark from filling in. Since the day she died he had gone to sleep every night with his back to the wall and his sword on the floor, every door in the fort watched and nobody at his shoulder. Six years of wall, laid over the two before it, over the ford. He had told himself that was strength. It was only the shape the grief had taken.

And she lay a hand's breadth away, who had counted at the bottom of the same dark. She had been paid to put a knife in him. She had pulled the strike in the pit, and killed for him in the narrows, and told him to his face what he was afraid of. She had done it with her eyes wide open, as if she had measured the whole of him and found him worth the telling.

He did not want a wall at his back. He wanted her there. He wanted her where he could hear her counting, near enough to hear her break, near enough to be told when he was wrong.

It frightened him more than the captain's spit ever had.

Beside him, very softly, Neela turned her face into the pillow and said a number he could not make out. He lay and listened, and he wondered what stair she was on, and who had broken it.
