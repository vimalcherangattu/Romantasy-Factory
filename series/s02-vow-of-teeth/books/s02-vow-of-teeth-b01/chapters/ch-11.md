# Chapter 11: Dawn Drill

The smell caught her halfway down the east path, and she was sick in the dirt before she knew it was coming.

There was little in her to lose. She knelt in the dark with one palm flat on the cold red ground and heaved up water and bile, while the thing that had done it hung in the damp air around her. It was sharp and green and rank, like a crushed bug on hot rubbish. Lantana. It grew down the whole slope under the east wall in tangled banks higher than her head, with flowers like small orange fists, and her feet had trodden a mat of its leaves into the path.

Neela had hated that smell for as long as she could remember, and had never found a reason. In the Resin House sheds she had been the one to stand upwind of any hedge that was being cut, with a cloth tied over her face, and nobody had asked her why.

Wiping her mouth on her wrist, she told herself her hands shook from the cold.

Thirty nights.

Last night she had lain beside him as nothing but a woman, with the flask in its fold of cloth and the shutters barred. She had listened to him breathe. The small hitch had come a third of the way through each long count, over and over, until the last dark hour. Then the breathing had changed. The bed had moved by the width of a man leaving it, and there had been no other sound. She had counted sixty and followed.

Rising was something she had only ever heard of, from Janu and from the lamps in the pit. Now she stood up out of the lantana with the stink in her hair, put the back of her hand over her nose, and breathed through her mouth down the last of the slope.

The pit lay below the east wall like a dropped coin. It was a round hollow, twenty paces across and shoulder-deep, its sides cut into the red earth and shored with laterite blocks. Lamps burned in niches all round the rim. The air above it smelled of sesame oil and, under that, of hot clay. Steps were cut into the north side. She crouched at the lip behind the last of the bushes, where the lamplight did not reach, and looked down.

He was already working.

Barefoot, bare to the waist, his mundu hitched high, he moved across the floor of the pit with no sound except the snap of cloth and the hiss of breath. She had braced herself to see skill. She had seen skill. Vellan was skill, all of it packed into a body like a bear's. This was something else, and for a moment she could not name it.

It was arrival. The strike was over before she knew where it had been aimed. A turn, a drop, a palm that stopped a finger's width from nothing. His forearm cut across at the height of a throat. His heel came round at the height of a knee. Between one count and the next he was ten feet away with his weight low and his hands loose, as if he had stood there all along. Vellan hit harder. This man simply got there first.

All of it was aimed at a man who was not there.

She watched his hands and began, against her will, to read them. The temple. The hollow under the ear. The soft place beneath the ribs on the left. The notch under the skull that the old masters called the door. Each blow landed on a point, exactly, on an enemy only he could see. He had put every one of the points in the air and killed it, over and over, in a pit with nobody in it.

*Eight years*, she thought. Janu had said it in the first week, over the supper pots. *He used to have a partner.* Eight years of rehearsing the death of a stranger, and no one ever allowed to stand in the place where the stranger went.

She counted his turns. At the sixth she saw it.

He turned to the right, fast, with the right hand sweeping high. And the left hand came down, only half a hand's width, to rest at his hip. It was the most natural thing in the world. It would also open him from the cheek to the belt for the time it takes to say a name. In the whole pit there was nobody to see it but her.

She had not come to be useful. She had come to kill him. She went down the north steps anyway.

* * *

He stopped before her foot reached the third step.

Not startled. He simply stopped, in the middle of a turn, and the stillness was worse than a shout. For one count she felt how far she was from any wall.

"Neela."

"Warden."

He stood with his chest rising and falling slowly. Sweat ran down the old scars on his ribs. He did not reach for anything, because there was nothing to reach for. He looked at her the way he looked at a ford, patiently and from every side at once.

"It's early," he said.

"So are you."

"I'm always early."

"I couldn't sleep." That was true. It was a pleasure to say a true thing.

"The lantana's bad on that path." He looked at her face, and then at the damp on her sleeve. "Gopan keeps meaning to cut it."

"It's only a smell."

"Mm."

She came down the last steps and out onto the pit floor. The earth was warm from his feet and came off red on hers. She went to stand a few paces from him, where a bride out for a walk might stand, and took care that her hands were empty and in plain sight.

"Show me a form," she said.

"Which?"

"One with two people in it."

"No."

He said it without heat. It was the same voice he used for *Not tonight, Janu*.

"I don't spar," he said.

"Janu says you did."

"Janu says a great deal."

"For how long have you not?"

"Eight years in the spring." He gave the figure as plainly as a tally of grain. Then, after a moment, as if he owed it to her: "Muthu asks me every seventh day. I say no every seventh day. He's a patient man."

"Is that a kindness to him, or to you?"

"I haven't decided."

She did not smile, though she felt the place where a smile would go. She looked at the swept ring of clay where he turned and said the next thing as lightly as she could.

"Then let me look at you. I won't touch you with anything but my eyes. Your stance is wrong."

For the first time something moved in his face. It was not offence. It was a kind of astonished interest, the look of a man who has been handed a bill and finds it correct.

"Who taught you to look?"

"A man who guarded pepper." It came out smooth. Kunjan Chirayil's warehouse had kept two guards, and one of them had once stood on a quay with a cudgel while she did not need him. "He thought a girl in a counting house should know when a man was about to lie to her, or hit her. He said they started in the same place."

"Where?"

"The feet."

He studied her. She had given him a true thing again, wrapped in a false one, and she wondered whether he could tell which was which.

"Go on," he said.

* * *

"Take it from the turn."

He went back to the place where he had stopped. She walked a slow circle round him, at the distance of a long arm, and said *again* twice, and the third time he went through the whole movement at half speed with her at his shoulder.

"Right turn. Hands high." She watched them. "There. Your left hand drops. Not much."

"I know my hands."

"You know where you put them. Again."

He turned again. The left hand came down.

"You see?"

"I see you telling me that I have something I don't."

"Then I'm wrong, and you can laugh at me later." She moved in behind him. "Your head's wrong too. It rides forward when you turn. May I?"

The silence in the pit was exact. A lamp spat. Somewhere above them, behind the wall, a goat coughed.

"Yes," said Devan.

She laid her fingers on the back of his neck.

His skin was hot and damp and there was a ridge of old muscle under it. She pressed the head back an inch, where it belonged, and he let her. His chin came down. His neck stood straight and bare in the lamplight, with the short hair clipped close to the nape.

At the base of the skull, a thumb's width below the bone, her fingers rested on the door.

Her hand knew it before she did. It had made this exact shape on sandbags and sheep, in practice, and once not in practice. The heel at the right angle. The weight dropping through the wrist, not the arm. A quarter-turn and a push, quick and clean. He would not cry out. He would fall as a man falls in a pit in the dark when no one is looking. By the time the day watch came on, there would be a widow in black with a seal in her lap and every honest man in the fort to say that the warden had always been careless in his own kalari.

She felt the pulse under her fingers. It was slow.

"One," she said, so low it was hardly a word.

The lamp flame leaned. A bar of grey had come into the sky above the east wall. She could do it by the next breath and be up the steps and in the chamber before the chain clanked. There was no bar to explain, no beast, no cage. This was the plan she had made, and it was a good one. She was free to be a woman who had finished her twelfth contract. Her page would burn and she would have a name.

"Two."

He had not turned his head. He had heard the number, and the next, and he knew what they were counting down. His hands hung at his sides, open, the palms half out. She knew that hand. It had lain on a blanket in the dark with seven pale scars across the knuckles, a finger's width from her nose, and it had not closed then either. His shoulders sat low and quiet under her palm, like a man's in a chair he trusts. She thought him a fool, or this a trap, and waited for the trap to close, and it did not. He only stood and breathed.

Then she thought of nothing at all. That was the strange part. The pit went quiet around her the way the langurs had gone quiet at the gorge, all at once, and her mind did not run the kill to its end. It stopped short of it, like a hand at a closed door.

"Three."

On the third breath he let the air out of his chest, slowly. He did not draw the next one at once.

He was waiting. He had heard every number, and he knew what her hand was resting on. She understood it with her whole body in the space of that held breath, and still she stood there with her fingers on his neck.

She lifted her hand.

"Your guard drops on the left," she said. Her voice came out level. "When you turn right the hand comes down half a palm. Somebody fast enough would use it."

Devan did not move at once. When he did, he turned slowly, as a man turns when he does not want to be mistaken for a threat. The lamplight lay on the plain, tired look of his face.

"You'd have had me," he said.

"Yes."

That was all. She had expected him to ask her something, and the question hung ready in the lamp-smoke between them. It never came. He looked at her as if she had given him a figure he had expected, and he was quietly checking it against his own count.

She did not explain. She had no explanation to give him that she could stand to hear herself say.

"The left," said Devan, at last. He said it to himself, in the voice of a man filing a rule.

Neela turned, and went up the north steps.

* * *

Her knees did not give out until the top.

She stood at the lip with one hand on the cold laterite and the other flat against her ribs, and the shaking came up from her heels in long waves. Her hands were wet. She looked at the right one, the one that had touched him, and could not make it close.

Below her, he had not gone back to his forms. He stood in the middle of the swept clay with his head bent and one hand lifted to the back of his own neck, exactly where hers had been. He held it there as if he were testing a bruise.

She went into the lantana with her breath held, and the smell found her anyway. At the bend she gagged again. Nothing came up.

* * *

The chamber was empty and the shutters were still barred.

She lifted the bar and set it against the wall. Grey light came in through the slats and laid ruled lines along the floor. She sat down on the edge of the bed and put her hands between her knees to hold them still.

*Reasons*, she thought. *Make a list.*

The east wall, first. The pair on the walk above the pit were never off it at dawn. If she had struck, a man might have looked down and seen a bride bending over her husband. That was the first reason, and she turned it over and found it sound.

It stayed sound for about as long as it took to remember that the east wall pair faced the hill. She had watched them from the lantana. Neither had once turned round.

The flask, then. She had come with no resin and no beast. If the strike went wrong she had nothing behind it. But the door did not go wrong, and she knew it better than she knew her own name, which was a poor joke, because she had never known her name.

The ache, then. One night off the flask already counted against her. A woman starting that count should not take chances. That was almost reasonable. She tried it twice and it would not stay put.

Something else would not stay put either, and she made herself pick it up. Four first lights in a row she had come in off the hill and found him abed. Last night she had stayed in, and he had gone down in the dark. On the nights she was out, he lay there until she was back. On the nights she was in, he went to his pit. She laid the two side by side and they fit like halves of a tally stick. It could be chance, or habit, or his bad sleep. But a man who rose by his own clock would not care where she was.

Was he waiting for her?

She had no answer, and the question was bigger than the one she had started with. She put it down unfinished and did the sum she had been walking around. Chathu's message had been plain. Once the writ landed, every day counted double. Thirty nights on the calendar were fifteen by his ledger. If the account stood open when the pass closed, he would put resin on her himself and read her page at dusk. And Chathu balanced every loss in some other coin. Ammu, humming her fishing song, with a nick on her knuckle.

Neela let out a long breath and said it aloud to the empty room. "It was the wrong morning. Thirty nights is plenty. I wanted a better one."

It was a good lie. It cost fifteen nights and a girl at an inn. She told it again anyway, and it sounded more like a plan. She got up and poured water from the kindi into the basin and scrubbed her hands to the wrist, over and over, until the skin went pink. The lantana came off, and the smell of him with it, clay and sesame oil and clean sweat, and then there was only water.

Men who are about to be killed flinch. She had seen it a dozen times. They flinch and they twist and they turn their heads at the last, whatever they have promised themselves. He had stood quite still and breathed out.

That did not go on the shelf with the rest. She had no shelf for it.

Above the east wall, Gopan's chain began to clank the hour, and she stood with her wet hands over the basin and counted every stroke.
