# Chapter 28: Wound-Share

The sky went grey, and the cat let go of her.

It began in the spine, a pull like a wet rope drawn through her bones. Fur sank into skin. The great warm weight across Devan's chest thinned to a woman's, lying in red mud with the rain gone cold on her back. She had no cloth and no voice yet. Her teeth were chattering too hard for either.

Then the cold stopped mattering, because her side was on fire.

She pushed up on one hand and looked down. A welt ran along her left ribs from under the breast to the hip, raw and red, beaded with blood at its length. It was not a wound she had taken. Nothing had cut her side. Her own blood was at her throat and wrists, where they had held her.

It lay exactly where the black stain showed on his tunic.

"Neela." His voice was a scrape. He was looking at her face, only her face. "Is that my wound?"

"It's yours." She pressed two fingers to it, and he hissed through his teeth at the same instant, and his hand flew to his ribs. They stared at each other.

"On me," she said. "It came up when I turned." She could hear her own voice climbing. "Ammu told me. A cut on you opens on me. She didn't say it would feel like this. Every time. Every cut you take. For the rest of my life, Devan."

"Yes." He did not soften it. "And you'll cost me the same."

He touched his own throat. His collar was stiff and dark. Beneath it, when he pulled the cloth aside, ran a thin red line where an odiyan's scar would be, though he had none, and another showed at each wrist where his sleeves had ridden up.

"The resin," she said. "They tore me open. It's on you."

"I thought it was the fever." He looked at the lines, then at her wrists. Her fingers had swollen overnight and still shook. "They were bleeding through the turning?"

"Still open when I said it. That's when it took." She held them out before he asked. "Here."

He did not say he was sorry, or that he had done it to her. He tore a strip from the hem of his tunic with his teeth and one hand, and bound her left wrist, and then her right, quick and tight, not touching the open parts.

"You can't wrap yours," she said.

"Then I'll bleed in pairs." The corner of his mouth moved. "Seems fair."

It nearly made her laugh. It came up through her chest and caught, and she found she was crying instead, without any sound. He said nothing. He moved his arm and let her put her face against his shoulder where the mud was thinnest, and he held her head.

Muthu found them like that. He stood a way off with a horse blanket, his face grey under the stubble and one sleeve black to the elbow, and made no noise at all. He set the blanket down on the mud and walked back to the gate.

* * *

The yard was empty. That was the first wrong thing.

No hunters, no lamps. The long shed stood open, and a net lay trampled by the cook-house door. Chathu had taken his people and gone into the Blackbark Wood in the night, and he had taken the book. A burned leaf was one leaf. Every other page he held was still a leash, and he knew it.

Vellan had gone with him. A bear's track ran from the yard into the mud at the tree line, three clear prints and a fourth that only dragged, a long furrow where the ruined foreleg trailed. It led straight into the trees.

She found Ammu where the trees began.

She lay on her side under a black-barked giant with her knees drawn up, small, a woman again after a night as a jackal. The spear had gone in under the ribs. Her face was turned to the earth, and her braid had come all the way down. Muthu had put a coat over her, and it was too big. The sleeve covered her hand.

Neela knelt. She had thought there might be something to say.

"Ammu," she said.

That was all there was. Twenty years, and it was a name. She turned the shoulder gently, and the face was not afraid. It looked cross, a little, as if Ammu had been interrupted halfway through a joke. Her mouth was open on the next word.

Neela closed the eyes with two fingers. She did not cry again. There was none left to do it with.

At the foot of the tree, in the roots, lay a small brown bundle: the cloth Ammu wore as Valli, the pepper trader's girl. Muthu had found it at the cook-house corner, where she had shed it, and laid it at the tree. It was folded in thirds, as Ammu always folded things. Beside it was a cord pouch with a thumb of black resin in it, the last she had held back for herself. Neela took it and tied it at her waist. She had no master now, and no ration, and she would not see the House's groves again except over a blade. It was not a gift. It was a debt, and she meant to spend it well.

Muthu had found a spade in the tapping sheds. He dug without being asked, a long way into the soft red ground, and she did not tell him to stop. Once she looked at his back. She remembered a stable, and an order, and Chathu's *a house pays its debts in news*. He had come on horseback, held an arch with a sword against twelve men, and slapped a horse's rump to send a sick man past him. A man could do both things.

She did not ask. She thought, *Later.* She was not sure she was brave enough for later.

When it was deep enough, Devan was there, upright by some stubbornness, a hand on a tree. He had washed his face at the cook-house well. He looked down at the small shape in the coat, and his lips moved once.

"Ammu," he said.

She turned to him. He said it again, carefully, like a man learning a name he had come to too late. "I'd have liked to know her."

"She'd have cheated you at seven wells," Neela said. "In the first game."

"Good."

"She hummed when she was afraid. A fishing song. *Pull and pull, the tide is coming in.*" Her mouth would not go on. He did not ask her to. He took hold of one end of the coat, and Muthu took the other, and they laid her down.

* * *

She would not leave without the kennels.

Devan did not stop her. He lowered himself onto the cook-house step and said, "I'll be here," as though that were an ordinary thing to say, and it was the kindest she had ever heard.

The aisle was as it had been. The deer. The hare. The mongoose, the fox that did not lift its head. Someone had left the end door open, and the light lay along the straw. A hunter's lamp had burned out in its bracket. The troughs were dry, and the racks were bare. No one had come to feed them.

She stood a moment. Then she put her bound hands to the shuttered well behind the cook-house and tore the boards off it, and it hurt, and she did it anyway. She hauled the bucket up and down the aisle until every trough held water. She forked fodder from the loft into every occupied cage, forty-one of them, and carried the cook-house scraps to the fox and the mongoose.

Then she went down to the small cage and stopped.

Kirri slept. The civet was curled nose to tail, and a thread of grey light lay on the silver round its leg.

*Open it,* said the part of her that had been the best at locks. *Open every one. Run them out into the wood.*

And then what? These were not captives with a bolt to draw back. They were creatures with their minds going out like lamps. A cage could be unlocked. A caging could not. The deer would stand in the open and not know what the open was. Kirri would walk into the trees and forget why.

And one trough would not last them. Water and a forkful of hay were a day, three at most. Forty-one animals shut in an empty compound would starve long before she was a week down the road.

*No caging is ever undone.* She had known it all her life, as she knew the number of the stairs. She had never before known it with her hand on the bars.

She put her fingers through and touched the silver anklet. It was worn so thin she could have bent it.

"I can't take you out," she said. "I'd like to tell you I can. I'd like to say it was the cage." Her voice cracked. "I can't give you your name, Kirri. But I'll keep it. I've got yours and Ammu's now."

The civet did not stir.

"You wanted a boat." She swallowed. "I'll find the sea. I'll send the anklet in a fish basket, like you said. Do you hear? I'm coming back. For you, and for the deer, and the fox, and all forty-one." She counted them with her eyes down the aisle, and her voice dropped. "Every one. If it takes years. If I have to take the House down plank by plank."

Nothing answered her. She stood until the light climbed the grille.

Then she propped the end door wide with a stone, wiped her face on the back of her wrapped wrist, and walked out into the morning.

Devan was on the step. Muthu stood a little way off, leaning on the spade.

"They'll be dry again in three days," she said. "I can't open them. It wouldn't free them, it would only put them out to die."

"No." Devan did not look at the kennels. He looked at Muthu. "The first cart goes as soon as the wood road will take a wheel. Four of Bhaskaran's men and fodder. If the rain holds them, they'll lose a day, not more. Then every week the roads allow, until we know what else can be done."

Muthu looked at the spade. "I'll go myself," he said, hoarsely.

"After the sealing." Devan's voice was mild. "I want you at the gate."

Muthu's hands went still on the spade. "Aye," he said.

It was not enough. It was all that was on offer. Neela nodded once, and Devan nodded back.

* * *

They rode all day.

Muthu had Devan before him on the big bay, one arm round his waist. She followed on the grey mare, who had stood in the thicket since the first dusk and now rolled a reproachful eye at her. Neither man spoke much. Devan slept in snatches, and she watched his back go slack and wake and slacken again.

The road ran up out of the wood and the trees lost their black. Behind her lay the only home she had had, and it was closed. Her page was ash. The House would call her rogue. The crown hunted rogues, and no one now could vouch for her to any gate in Varanad except the one she was riding toward.

She had not a guild-sister left to lie to. She had not a master. The only people on her side of any wall who knew what she was were a captain with a locked mouth and a sick man who had followed her through a gatehouse.

Late in the day Devan roused and looked back over Muthu's shoulder. He said, in the flat voice he used for facts:

"The warden's house has room in it. Nobody will ask for your page at my gate."

"They'll ask the next day."

"Then they'll ask me." He thought about it. "I have no one at Kettamala who's mine except Janu, and she's the fort's. I'd like it to have someone who is."

She did not answer. She rode. But she moved the mare up until her knee touched the bay's flank, and she kept it there. Devan turned his face forward again. After a while his hand, on the bay's neck, uncurled.

* * *

Two days after they rode in, the Keeper of the Passes came up to Kettamala with five carts.

He was late. The carts had been due days before, and a rider had come in ahead of them with word that they were held at the lower waystation. A matter of an axle, the rider said. The charter sealing waited on the Keeper's own seal, so every day he lingered below was a day the pass stayed open. Devan read the delay another way. It was how a man waits when he is listening for news from the fort, of a warden dead and a widow holding the seal. The news had not come, so the carts had.

Devan stood under the padippura roof, because he had said he would. Eravi had wanted him in bed, and Janu had wanted him in a chair. He had told them both he would stand in the shade. He had eaten two bowls of rice, drunk a quart of water, and put on a fresh tunic over the binding. The ribs gave a slow, clear ache that was his own, and at his wrists a thin sting that was not. Neela was somewhere up on the west rampart above the chamber window, where the sentry used to stand. The wall bent there toward the gate, and she would see every cart come in. He could not see her.

The sky lay low on the pass. The rain had held off all day, the way an animal holds its breath.

Ittan came on foot beside the lead cart, which he need not have done. A round man in good grey cotton, soft-voiced, with rings on three fingers and a clerk at his shoulder carrying a case of tallies. He had a pleasant face. He had written Devan a kind letter every season for six years, and Devan had kept them all.

He raised both arms from forty paces off.

"My friend!"

He did not slow. He did not check or glance at the gate sergeant. Devan had been braced for the small stumble, the half-breath of a man who had been told that the warden was dying and then finds him standing. It did not come. The step went on over the stones, whole and easy, and the arms came wide, as though Ittan had walked up to a wedding feast and found the groom exactly where a groom should be.

"They said you were poorly," Ittan said, close now, and took Devan's hand in both of his. They were warm and damp. "The whole road said it. I said, no, not our Devan, he is made of laterite. And look at you!"

"A fever," Devan said. "It passed."

"Thanks be." He did not ask which fever. "And the bride? She does not bore you? I have thought of you both, I assure you. A man's first marriage in office, and nobody to tell him what it costs." He laughed. "Pepper never grew in a quiet house."

He smelled of clove, strong, sweet and oily, and it clung to the rings.

"Forgive the scent." Ittan lifted a hand, and the sleeve slid back from a thumb with a stain on it, dull brick red under the nail. "My wife's cough. I've rubbed her chest every night for three winters, and I carry it on me like a sin. The physicians say clove will do her no harm and no good. What shall a man do?"

Clove oil. Brick red, under a thumbnail. Devan knew that colour as he knew his own signet: council wax, poured with clove oil so it would not crack in damp. He set it beside the rest and did not let it reach his face. A man could rub a wife's chest and still handle wax. A man could also be a Keeper whose office held a seal box. It proved nothing. It sat in him like a stone in a boot.

He looked past Ittan at the carts.

Five of them, and the first rode low. Not by much. A carter would not have remarked it. But the axle bowed, and the ox's head hung, and the wheel cut a groove in the track that the next cart did not. A big chest sat roped under the tarpaulin. A carter walked beside it with one palm laid flat on the lid, the way you rest a hand on an animal you do not want to startle.

*On sealing day every cart is opened, in or out. No exception is written for the Keeper.*

Not yet. Tomorrow. He needed Ittan under his roof tonight, comfortable and fed and unwatched, and a clerk's tallies still unsorted.

"You've never climbed to Kettamala," Devan said. "You send a clerk."

"Never, yes. Shameful." Ittan beamed and patted the hand he still held. "This year I wished to see how the match had prospered. I proposed it, you know. A matchmaker's vanity."

"The council usually sends a clerk to seal the pass."

"It does."

"Who sends the Keeper, when a clerk would do?"

The smile did not falter. It did something Devan had not seen a smile do. It simply stayed, perfectly kind, and nothing behind it moved at all.

"I am only a hand, my friend," said Ittan, Keeper of the Passes. "Hands go where they're sent."

He pressed Devan's fingers once, warmly, and let them go. He turned to call a servant about the oxen, and the clove drifted off in his wake.

Devan stood and watched the first cart roll in under the padippura roof, low on its axle. A thin line of pain pulled at his right wrist, where her torn one lay bound on the wall above. She had closed her hand.

A hand went where it was sent. He stood very still and wondered whose palm was open.
