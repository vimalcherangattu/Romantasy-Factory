# Chapter 27: Leopard

They forced the resin on her before sunset, so she would have time to bleed.

Four hunters held her in the cage, and Pachan was one of them. He kept his eyes on the straw. A fifth man took her chin in his fist and tilted her head back until the bars bit her skull. Ammu stood at his elbow with the resin pot in both hands, as Chathu had ordered. Her face gave away nothing.

"Hold still, Seventeen," Ammu said, in a voice that was not hers.

The paddle came down cold across the pale scar at Neela's throat.

She had smeared that resin herself a hundred times and chosen every one. The scars knew her hand. They did not know this. The old ridges split like seams. Blood ran warm down her neck and into her collar, and the cold went to fire. They did her wrists next. She heard a sound and knew it was hers.

It would take her at dusk. She could scream or sing or lie dead still, and it would make no difference. Already a low hum had started behind her teeth.

"The cart is at the gate road," said a hunter at the door. "Master wants everyone."

"Ammu stays," Pachan said, thumbing his belt. "Master says a lesson's better with the eyes. Watch her."

Ammu nodded. The boots went away across the yard, and the latch dropped on the aisle.

Neela counted. At ten, Ammu set the pot down on the straw, and her hands stopped shaking.

"Pachan's belt ring," she said, holding up a key. "He still leans like a man among friends."

She took a flat blade from her braid and cut the wet leather. The strips fell. Neela's hands came free, red to the elbow, and would not close.

"Here." Ammu pressed something small into her palm: a finger of clay, with a thumb-pinch in the wax. "Out of Chathu's sleeve. I've been picking that sleeve since I was seven."

The antidote. Neela stared at it. "Ammu. Your hair. You'll be—"

"Dead, probably. Listen, chechi, I haven't long." The old teasing was gone from her voice, and the speed stayed. "There's a way out. I told you once there was, and you didn't ask, because you never do. Once in a life, to one person who is looking at you, during the dusk turning, you say it yourself. Aloud, in your own voice."

"Say what?"

"What you are." Ammu's mouth shook. "The name given back. That's the verse. That's all the verse ever was."

The song. Neela had sung it a thousand times, tapping bark, and never heard it.

"It voids everyone else," Ammu said. "Chathu. Me. The witness page goes dead in his hands. After that it takes two voices to cage you, and one of them is yours. Nobody can do it without you."

"What does it cost?"

"Everything." Ammu laughed once, breathless. "You share wounds with that person for life. A cut on him opens on you. If he's dying, you go with him. Your entry in the ledger burns, so the guild knows, and you're rogue. The crown hunts you. And you can do it once, ever, to one person, so you'd better mean him."

Neela said nothing for a breath. "You've known for years."

"Since I was fourteen. An old master whispered it drunk, and I swore I'd never tell. I didn't tell you because it costs, chechi." She wiped her face with the back of her wrist. "And because hiding kept us alive. It did. Twenty years. I'm not sorry."

"I'm not asking you to be."

"Good." Her chin came up. "You know who."

"I've known since the stable."

"I know you have. I stopped believing your tom cat a week ago." Ammu pulled her up by both arms. "I'm a bad liar when it's you."

At the cage door she stopped. She dug two fingers into the resin pot and drew them down her own throat, then across each wrist, quick as a woman straightening a collar. She hissed once through her teeth. The black smear shone wet above her cloth.

"I'm not waiting for anyone to hold my chin," she said. "Come on."

They went out past the cages. Neela stopped at the small one. The civet lay curled around the silver on its leg and did not look up.

"I wrote her and wrote her," Ammu said, very low. "She's past the door, chechi. Come."

Neela put two bloody fingers through the bars and touched the grey fur. It was warm. She took her hand back.

* * *

Last light lay red along the yard wall.

The shouting came from the gatehouse before they reached the corner of the cook-house. Steel on steel, and a big voice roaring words she could not make out. A horse burst out of the arch with its rider slumped forward on its neck, iron-shod hooves skidding in the wet. Muthu had not brought a cart. A cart was a day Devan did not have. A very large man with a sword slapped the horse's rump and turned his back on the yard.

He did not look round. Hunters came at him in the arch, and he took them there, one at a time, with the stone at his back.

The horse stopped in the middle of the yard, blowing. The rider slid sideways and hit the mud.

She ran. She went down on her knees in the wet beside him, and her hands found his face.

He was grey. His lips had gone the colour of ash, and the black thread had crept past his belt and up under his ribs. His eyes opened when she said his name.

"Neela." It was barely a sound. He spat something into his palm and closed his fingers over it, a small wet river pebble. "You didn't come back."

"I'm here. Swallow."

She dug the wax stopper out with her teeth. She tilted his head and put the vial to his mouth, and he took it as a child takes medicine, trusting and slow. Some ran down his chin. She held his jaw shut with her hand.

"Swallow."

He did. His throat moved.

"Now listen," she said. "The sun is almost down. I'll change. The resin's on me. Don't look away. Whatever happens in the next minute, keep your eyes on me."

"I've watched every turning you let me see." His mouth shaped the ghost of something dry. "I never once looked away."

"This is different."

"Then I'll look harder."

The shed latch scraped.

Chathu stepped out of the long shed with his lamp in one hand and a black cloth packet in the other. His step was uneven on the left foot. He saw her kneeling, and the open kennel door beyond the cook-house corner, and he stopped. For one moment she saw the old man's face come apart. He looked like someone who had found a hole in a floor he had walked for twenty years.

"Seventeen," he said. "Ammu. Oh, child."

Ammu was already gone. She was a shadow at the cook-house corner, and then she was not.

The sun touched the wall's edge. The hum behind Neela's teeth became a drum.

She stood. She put herself between Devan and the last of the light, so that he would see her and nothing else. Her wrists were running. Her throat was running. The first bone moved.

"Look at me," she said.

He was propped on one elbow in the mud. He looked. Not at her throat or her hands, but at her face, as if it were a thing he meant to keep.

Behind Chathu, a big shape came out of the shed shadow at a walk. Vellan pulled a flask from his collar and smeared his throat as he went. She saw it at the edge of her eye and did not turn her head.

The burn came up her spine like a fist opening. Her knees bent the wrong way. Her mouth filled with the taste of bark and copper, and the yard tilted and swam with a thousand new smells, and every one of them was prey. She had a minute, perhaps less, before the voice went.

She had never said it. Not in eleven years, not to a mirror, not alone on a hill. The other one, she had called it, afraid of its name as she had been afraid of her own.

To him, then. Her voice cracked and held.

"Leopard."

It went out of her like a thrown stone. Something in her chest unlatched and latched again, and settled with a click she felt in her ribs. Devan's face did not change, except that his eyes shut for the length of a heartbeat and opened.

Then her jaw lengthened, and her voice was gone.

* * *

Chathu began to read.

He had the page out of its cloth, a single ivory leaf, brown at the edges, black lines crowding its face. A narrow slip was stitched to its corner with coarse thread. His voice was steady and kind, the voice that had read her to sleep once. "Seventeen. First turning witnessed in this house, Chathu, master. Night-shape—"

He stopped. He took a breath, because the word had to be said.

"Leopard."

Nothing happened.

She stood on four feet in the red mud, the whole yard a bell of smells, and waited for the cage to close. It did not come. There was only the click she had already felt, lodged in her ribs.

"Leopard," he said again, louder. "Seventeen. Leopard."

The ink moved on the page.

The witness lines blackened one by one, the way a leaf blackens in a fire, from the middle outward. A thread of smoke rose from the fine hand she had once copied her letters from. The entry curled and glowed in his fingers, and he cried out. The slip tore free at its stitches and dropped face up in the mud, whole and unscorched. *Intake, sold at six years,* it said in a different hand, with two more lines beneath that she could not read.

The rest of the leaf burned in the mud and was gone.

Chathu stood with his hand held out in front of him. Then he stooped, quick and careful, and took the slip out of the wet. His thumb covered what was written on it. His eyes were wet.

"What have you done?" he whispered. "What have you done to my book?"

Then, over his shoulder, flat and cold: "Vellan."

The turning was done in him. She heard the wet crack of it and smelled scorch and sap, and the bear came on at a run.

Devan was still propped in the mud on one elbow. The antidote had gone into him a minute ago, no more. It had not reached his legs. He could not stand.

It was not a fast beast, but it was a heavy one. Its mass shook the ground. It came straight at the downed man and the cat beside him, forepaws raised.

Neela threw herself at its flank.

It was all she had. Her shoulder hit the black ribs, and the bear's rush turned half aside. Its weight flung her off. She hit the mud and rolled, winded, and the ache in her new limbs said she would not do that twice.

The bear swung back for the man on the ground.

Devan got a knee under him and no more. His arm shook. He had no sword. The great black shoulder came down at him, near enough that the stink of it filled his face, and his right hand lifted from the mud with the fingers crooked.

It stopped in the air.

She saw him find the place. She saw him think of the ford, and the eight years. His fingers crooked tighter and hesitated, the way they had when he told her about the raider captain. His face went very still. He did not close his eyes.

The hand went in, hard and exact, to the hollow of the shoulder.

The bear's foreleg folded. The whole huge body pitched and skidded in the mud with a grunt like a tree coming down, and ploughed to a stop an arm's length from his knees. It lay there bellowing.

Devan pitched forward on his hands and stayed there, his head hanging.

A hot tearing line opened along her own ribs.

*A cut on him opens on you.* Ammu had said it not an hour ago. His old wound had split under the strike, and now it was hers as well. She saw him sway and clap a hand to his side.

He lifted his head. He looked at what he had done, and at her.

He did not look sorry. That was the thing. She had been braced for sorry.

Chathu's voice rose behind them. "Leave the cat! She'll keep till the bear's done. The jackal has Pachan's key. Take the jackal!"

Then she heard it from the cook-house roof. Three short yips, a pause, and one long rising note.

*Tomorrow, I will find you.*

She had practised that at eleven and nine, behind the sheds, until Chathu said the House had no use for dogs.

A fawn shape dropped from the thatch onto the bear's back, raked its ear with a flash of teeth and sprang off. Ammu landed in the mud with her hackles up and her tongue out, grinning. It was a ridiculous look on a jackal. It was exactly her face.

She turned and ran for the trees.

The bear heaved up on three legs. It roared, and the sound shook the yard. Then it went after her, limping, furious. The hunters followed with their poles and nets, spilling past the cook-house. Chathu's voice rang, thin and high, behind them.

*No,* Neela thought. The cat in her bunched to follow.

Devan's hand found her shoulder. It did not grip. It rested.

"Don't," he said. "They want you to follow."

She stood trembling under it. The cat's whole body said run. She weighed it the way she weighed a wall. Ammu was thirty strides ahead, and the nets were thrown for a leopard as much as a jackal. His tunic was black and wet down the left side where the old line had split, and the same fire lay along her own ribs. If she ran and a spear found her, he would bleed for it, with no blood to spare. If she ran, she ran alone, and left him in the mud of a yard that still had hunters in it. If she stayed, Ammu ran alone.

Nobody ordered her. His hand stayed light, and he did not say it twice.

She stayed. She hated it.

Every sound reached the cat's ears, a hundred times sharper than hers. The crashing in the brush and the bear's wounded breath. Then a stretch of nothing but running feet, and a yip that cracked, and then another, too far.

A man shouted, a single word. A spear thudded.

Then the cry. It was not the call. It was short and high and surprised. It rose, as if she meant to say something at the end of it.

It stopped.

The wind turned and came back across the wood. It carried the smell with it: jackal blood, hot and sharp, and nothing living under it.

At the gate, the steel noise had gone quiet too, and somewhere in the arch, a big voice was swearing hoarsely and alive.

Chathu's lamp passed along the yard wall and went into the dark. Neela did not look at it.

* * *

She could not turn back before dawn.

Nothing in the world could change her till the sky did. She was a cat, and there were hours of night to get through, and a body that did not know how to weep.

Devan had not got up again. He lay on the wet earth, his breath shallow and fast, and the antidote had begun to work in him, or he would be dead. The stain on his tunic had spread, low on the left, and she felt the answering sting along her own side.

She stepped over him. She lowered herself, heavy and warm, across his chest and belly, and put her chin on his shoulder. She could feel his heart under her. She counted it.

His hand came up and found her ruff and stayed there, loose. He said nothing. The night came down, and the rain began again.

She did not leave.
