# Chapter 17: The Keeper's Letter

The first rule of the dark was that she could ask him anything.

Nobody had agreed to it. It had simply grown there on the second night, the way a path grows where enough feet have gone. She lay on her back with the lamp out and the shutters open to the rain, and he lay at the far edge of the bed with the sword on the boards on her side. For a long time neither of them spoke.

Then she said, to the ceiling, "Why is the hilt on my side?"

"So you never have to reach across me for anything."

She turned that over. It was exactly the sort of answer he gave. A fact, with a kindness folded inside it like a coin in a cloth, and no comment from him on either.

"You knew about the knife," she said.

"Under the mattress, your side. Since the second week." He breathed out. "I never moved it."

She had suspected as much. It still landed low in the belly. "Were you afraid? That first night."

"Yes."

She waited. In her old life, a man who said yes meant *no*, or *a little*, or *but I mastered it*.

"For about a breath," said Devan. "I know what the word does, and I thought, it will see me. Then it looked away, and went over the ledge like something that had done it a hundred times. After that I was afraid of the wrong thing."

"Which was?"

"That you'd look round."

Rain ticked on the sill. Somewhere below, the cistern chain clanked, and she counted it out of habit and lost the number.

"What would you do with the name?" she said. "If you had it."

"Take it to the high court at Kodiyur, with the seals in my hand." No pause. "I would not kill him. I'd like you to know that, since you will wonder."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were."

She had been. She let it go.

The third question she had carried all day, and it came out smaller than she had planned. "How did she die? Your sister."

The bed did not creak. His breath, which had the little hitch in it a third of the way through, did not hitch.

"I won't answer that," he said.

It was the second time he had refused her that question. Tonight the words sat in the dark as plainly as the sword on the boards.

She was glad. That was the ugly part. Something low in her went slack with relief, as if she had put a hand near a stove and been told not to. She didn't ask herself why. She lay and was ashamed of the gladness instead, which was easier.

"May I ask one?" he said.

"You can ask."

"I'm not asking for a price."

"I know." And she did, which was new. "Ask."

"What do you call it? When it isn't me saying *the cat*."

The room waited. The word sat in her mouth like a stone she could swallow or spit. She had never said it aloud, not to anyone, not to Ammu.

"The other one," she said.

"Thank you," said Devan, and no more. He did not turn to her. After a while his breath went long, then came the small hitch, and she knew he was awake and letting her hear it.

Two nights without resin, and she'd paid the ledger for both. No hunting the ridge. A cup at the meeting stone left bare. The ache would start in a week if she kept on, a slow fever in the nails first. She had made the sums on the sill that evening, and the sums said this was foolish.

She lay in the dark and spent the night anyway.

* * *

Sudhi came up the hill on the ninth day with the royal post in an oilcloth bag, a steaming pony, and a face the colour of a boiled prawn.

Neela knew the pony's name, because she knew everyone's. Biscuit. The groom was Appu, nine years old and in love with it. She stood on the lower terrace with a basket of pepper clippings on her hip, watching the rider hand his bag to Unni at the gate. Muthu came out of the guardroom with his big laugh and a grey-sealed letter ready in his fist.

He was talking about his mother. He was always talking about his mother. Sudhi took the letter without a word, tucked it into the bag, and rode on to the stable to be fed.

*Plain grey wax, no mark.* She put it on the shelf in her head where she kept things she didn't yet know how to use.

Devan took his own post at the gate and said nothing all afternoon. At dusk he came up, shut the door behind him, and lit the lamp himself. He stood with one letter, three sheets, and a face that was too still.

"This is addressed to me," he said. "It speaks of you. I would rather you heard it in my voice than Janu's." He held it out so she could see the seal. "Council wax."

It was brick red, with the pepper sprig pressed deep. She caught the smell when he cracked it, a faint sweet note of clove.

"Read," she said. "All of it. Don't skip."

He stood by the lamp with the rain black in the window behind him, and read.

"*To my friend Devan, Warden of Kettamala, from his friend Ittan, Keeper of the Passes. Greetings, and may the rains find you well roofed.*"

Neela folded her arms. She began, without meaning to, to count.

"*My friend, I write in haste, which is why this runs to three pages. My friend, the council has voted what we all knew it must, that the Monkey Pass be sealed for the season. And I have asked, my friend, that I be the one to do it. A man ought to look upon the gate he locks. I shall climb to you before the last carts go down, a fortnight, perhaps less, if the roads allow. I ask only a dry bed and a plain supper, my friend. I know your Janu by reputation. I fear her plain suppers.*"

He read the joke flatly, as he read everything. It did not make it less of a joke.

"*I hear the pepper is heavy this year. I hear your hounds are well. My friend, I hear many things, and I keep them like an old woman keeps buttons, for no reason but the comfort of having them. And how does your bride, my friend? I think of her often. A young woman far from the coast, and the long rains coming on. Such a comfort to a house, my friend, a wife. Such a comfort, should anything befall the fort in the dark months, to know there is one within its walls.*" He turned a page. "*Give her my regards, my friend, and my hope that she is well and happy and not overwhelmed. Do not trouble to answer, my friend, for the roads are bad and I shall be with you soon enough. I remain, my friend, as ever, your friend, Ittan.*"

He lowered it.

"Eleven," said Neela.

"Eleven what?"

"*My friend.* Eleven times, and *your friend* at the close. Twelve." She tipped her head. "It's more than a man says to a friend, Devan. It's what he says to someone he's not sure of."

"Ittan writes like that to everyone. He's said *my friend* to the queen's cook." But he looked at the page again, and she saw him run his eye down it, counting. "Twelve," he agreed.

"And it asks after me. Not after the fort. After *me*, and what I am to the fort." She kept her voice level. "*Should anything befall.* Befall what? He doesn't say the warden. A man who writes three pages and doesn't write the word *you* there is choosing not to."

"Or he is a fond fool with a lot of ink."

"Is he a fool?"

Devan was quiet. Then, slowly: "No."

"Who is he to you?"

"Keeper of the Passes. He has written me a kind letter every season for six years. Since the winter Asha died." He flipped the sheets over, as if the back might confess. "He has never once climbed this hill. The council sends a clerk to seal the pass, with a sack of lead wafers and a bad cold. Ittan says he is coming himself."

"Then I mark two things," said Neela. "First, that he climbs himself. Second, the comfort."

"The comfort."

"Comfort to whom? You don't send comfort to a man whose house is fine. You send it to a man whose house you've an eye on." She spread her hands. "I don't know what it means. I only know it was chosen."

He set the letter on the chest. For a moment she saw him weigh whether to say the next thing, and decide.

"Here is what I have. Three seals." He held up three fingers, the way a man tells off cattle. "The council keeps seven tally seals. Four are cut with a bar at the sprig's stem. Three are cut with a dot and a split under it. The forged tallies bear the dot. So it's one of three boxes, or a copy of one. Three keepers on the council. Each box passes through a clerk's hands, and clerks have wives and debts."

"And the keepers' names?"

"I have them." He didn't give them. She noted that he didn't, and noted too that he hadn't hidden it. "The Keeper's office holds one of the three."

She stilled.

"I'd be a fool to say that proves a thing," said Devan. "The Keeper's office holds a great deal. Boxes, ledgers, the key to the pass. If the pass is sold, it was sold from his desk or past it."

"And the fourth?"

"Fourth, a man called Sudhi."

She hadn't expected it from him in that voice. "The rider."

"He carries the palace post up and the fort's post down. He carried the sandalwood box. Anything that can be wrapped in a letter can ride in his bag, and no one looks in the bag of a man in palace green." He rubbed his thumb along the chest's edge. "I've watched his hands for three posts and seen nothing. He is a boy with a sore saddle. That doesn't make him clean."

"Muthu hands him letters."

"To his mother." Devan said it quickly, the way a man says a thing he has said to himself on a long night. "Muthu has stood at my back for eight years."

*And you, Devan, have not turned around to look.* She did not say it. She weighed it, though, with a small cold interest. He had no doubt of Muthu. It was the one place his eye did not go.

"A rider, or a keeper," she said. "Which do you think?"

"The seals. A rider is a thief. A keeper is a thing that has to be dug out."

"I'd say the letter." She nodded at the chest. "A man who sells passes by the cartload doesn't write. He sends a man with a purse and no signature. Only someone who wants it on record that he was kind would write *friend* twelve times."

"Or someone who is kind."

"Or that." She heard her own dry note and let it stand. "We'll see which, when he climbs."

Devan looked at her. She had expected the old, shut look. Instead he looked as if she had set a stone on a scale he'd been trying to balance for six years, and the needle had swung.

"We," he said.

She didn't answer, which was an answer of a kind.

* * *

Later, past the middle watch, with the lamp out. The shutters open, the rain gone to a mist that smelled of red earth.

She stood at the window with the flask corked in her fist and did not open it. That made three nights. The cost of a fourth sat on her tongue like a bitter seed.

Devan lay with his eyes shut and his boots off, the whole bed between him and the sword, doing a very good impression of a man resting. She watched his mouth. She had caught herself at it all week, the way she watched a lock for the click, waiting for the lie to land.

"You haven't asked again," she said.

He didn't open his eyes. "Asked what?"

"The name. You said once. You said you wouldn't say it twice. Then you took it back and I thought you'd pick it up again as soon as I'd forgotten. It's been three days."

"I said no terms."

"You meant it?"

"I don't say a thing I don't mean."

"That's not an answer."

At last his eyes opened. They went to her, steady and brown, and stayed. "It is, though," he said. "If I ask, I'm asking for a price, however I put it. You told me what that is. I would rather lose the name than be the man you described."

She stood with the flask in her hand and felt a small, sharp disorder in the place where her plan for him lived. A hunter doesn't say that. A hunter says *take your time*. A hunter keeps the door ajar.

*Or says it, so you'll think he isn't one.*

She gave it up. She could not tell, and she was tired of not being able to tell, and the tiredness itself was a danger.

Out on the hill, thin and high, a sound began.

Three short yips. A pause long enough to count four. Then one long note, climbing, climbing, and held until it broke.

Her hand closed on the flask hard enough to hurt.

*Tomorrow. I will find you.*

She had made that call herself, at eleven, behind the sheds, with Ammu's skinny arm round her neck and the whole Resin House asleep. It was the only thing in her life that had been hers and Ammu's alone. Now it came over the terraces like a hook thrown across a river.

She did not move. Behind her, in the dark, Devan did not move either. His breath stopped for a count, then started up again, a shade slower.

She waited for him to ask. A man who knew what a jackal's yip was and what it was not, who had spent twenty nights learning the shape of her silences, would ask. And she had no lie ready. For the first time in eleven contracts, she hadn't built one.

"That's not a jackal," Devan said.

"No."

He said nothing more.

She turned around. He lay as he had lain, on his back, one arm across his ribs, eyes on the ceiling beams. He had the face of a man who had decided not to look at something and would see it through.

"You're not going to ask," she said.

"I'd rather you told me. If you tell me." He kept his eyes upward. "I'll want you to be able to say *I won't answer that* to me as easily as I said it to you."

"Even if it's about someone who could hurt you."

"Especially then."

"That's not sensible."

"No," he agreed. "It's a term."

She laughed. It escaped her, a short, startled breath, and she covered her mouth with the back of her wrist, bangle cold against her lip. It was not a pleasant laugh. It was the laugh of a person who has been handed something and cannot find the pocket for it.

"Tomorrow," she said, "I'll be gone from the terraces. If Muthu asks."

"You went to the market. Parukutty's girl has a cough." He said it at once, to the beams. "It's even true. She has."

She stared at him. He had given her the lie before she asked, and chosen one that wasn't a lie.

"Go to sleep, Devan."

"I'm trying."

She did not take the resin. She lay down on her side of the bed with her back to the wall and her face to the room, where she could see the lamp-black shape of him and the pale line of the sword. Seventeen nights left. Seventeen was also the number Chathu had written over her page.

The cistern chain clanked. The mist drifted in at the window and lay on the boards like milk.

She lay awake a long time, and the worst of it was the wanting. It was the plainest want she had known in years. She wanted to give him something. Not the name. She didn't have the name, and if she'd had it she did not know that she could. She wanted to put a thing in his hand the way he had put the lie for the market in hers. A clean thing, with nothing behind it.

She went through what she had. A flask. A leopard's tooth in the waistband, the guild's. Eleven contracts that had ended in the dark. Not one of them was a gift.

*Ammu will find me tomorrow.* She held the thought still and looked at it. Ammu would ask where the reports had gone, and what kind of woman sleeps three nights without resin beside a man she has been sent to kill. And Neela would have to say something, and in the whole of her life she had never had trouble with that.

So she chose, there in the dark, what Ammu would get. The seal boxes. The Keeper's letter and his coming. The hill, the pass, the rider. All of it true, and all of it hers to spend. She would not give Ammu the man.

Across the dark the hitch came, a third of the way through his breath.

She counted it. It came again. She went on counting, and for once the number did not come out to a trap. It came out to a man lying awake so she would know he was there, and a woman lying awake so he would know she was.

She slid her hand into the gap between mattress and frame and closed it on the hilt. Six inches of plain guild steel, kept there since the wedding. She drew it out slowly, so the slats wouldn't ring, and leaned across the dark. She laid it on the boards on his side of the sword, hilt toward him.

The hitch stopped. She heard him not breathe.

"It's the only thing I have that's mine," she said, to the ceiling.

He did not touch it. He did not say thank you. After a long while his breath began again, and the hitch with it, and she shut her eyes with her hands empty for the first time since she was six.
