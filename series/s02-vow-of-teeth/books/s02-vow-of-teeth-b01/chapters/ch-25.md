# Chapter 25: Claw Marks

The cistern chain clanked the hour, and Devan set his foot on the first step of the west stair as the last red went out of the laterite.

New moon. The east was already black.

He had doubled everything. Two men at the stair head, with orders to hold it whatever they heard. Pairs on the wall, every one of them mid-change on the hour chain. At the last light he had found the stair lamps drained, not burned dry: the wicks still white, the cups tipped, a thumb of oil left in each. Somebody inside the fort had wanted this stair dark. With every post tied to the chain, Raiu had gone down to the lower court for oil, and Devan took the dark stair himself, because it was the only way to her door.

Behind the barred chamber door, Neela stood at the shutter with her sleeves pushed back. She had not smeared the resin. The other one could not warn anyone, she had said, and she wanted hands tonight. One nail had split at the quick that morning. She had hidden it in her fist, and he had not mentioned it.

He passed the parrot newel, its beak worn smooth, and the shadow behind it stepped out.

He had never seen the man. He knew him anyway. Grey at the temples, a gate's shoulders, a body that made no sound on stone. Neela had described him on the rampart, and her description had been exact.

Vellan was human. His collar hung open and his throat shone black with resin.

"Warden," he said quietly.

The blade was simply there. Devan's sword was half free when it opened him across the ribs, left side, a clean hot line. He felt the edge slide, then the cold behind it. The steel was smeared dark, and a bitter smell came off it like scorched tamarind.

He got the sword up and caught the second cut on the guard. The third he took on the flat. Then he was climbing, backward, his heel finding the steps by memory. Four. Five.

"You don't swing," Vellan said. He sounded almost curious. "Eight years since you struck anyone. Your house keeps good count."

"She told me your name."

"Good girl."

Devan gave ground. He could have killed the man. His body knew where: the notch under the ear, the hollow below the breastbone. Two fingers would do it. His hands remembered every line of it, and went cold with the remembering. A mud-bank. A man on his knees.

Eleventh step. It rang hollow under his heel, over the drain slot, and he almost went down on it.

"Warden!"

Raiu's voice, from the lower court. Then boots, fast, and a torch snatched from a court bracket jerking light up the wall. The boy had dropped the can and come at the first sound of steel, a spear in his other hand. Above, a stair-head man took a step down.

"Hold the head!" Devan shouted. "Whatever you hear!"

The man stopped. Devan had given the order himself, and he heard what it cost.

"Stay back, Raiu!"

Vellan said something under his breath that might have been *pity*. He stepped down and away from Devan, toward the bend, where the wall took him out of the torchlight.

Devan did not see what happened there. The stair turned, and the wall hid it. He heard it: a cracking, a long grunt that rose and thickened. He smelled resin, sharp enough to sting his eyes.

What came round the bend was already a bear.

It was huge and shaggy and black, with a pale muzzle and claws as long as a man's fingers. It took the boy before the torch had finished falling. Raiu got the spear halfway up. The bear struck once, straight across the chest, and the boy went back against the rail with a short, broken sound.

The torch hit the step and rolled. Its flame lay along the stone like a cut.

Devan was down the steps before he had decided. His legs were wrong, heavy, and the cut at his ribs had gone from fire to ice. He did not strike. He put his body between the boy and the beast. The bear swung a paw at his head, and the claws raked the rail beside his ear and took a fist of pala wood out of it.

He thought: *Not a word. He has not said a word.* A man in shape could not speak. Beside that was another thought, small and cold. *I did not see it turn. The wall hid it. I am nothing to him but a man he is trying to kill.* The word would have been only a word.

Then he saw the boy's chest.

Raiu lay across the third step, blinking at the sky. His tunic had been laid open in four parallel furrows from collarbone to belt, deep and wide-set, the edges dragged ragged by something that did not cut but tore.

Devan knew those marks. He had stood over them at twenty-six, in the grey before dawn, with Janu holding a lamp and his hands going out to the cloth over Asha's chest.

*Four ruts. Wide as this. A bear, in the walls. On this stair.*

The numbness had already reached his fingertips. He could not feel the rail under his hand. It rose up his side like cold water up a post, and as it came the thought went through him whole. *Asha. Asha, on this—*

And the poison put it out.

It took the thought as a flood takes a lamp. He could not hold it. He knew only that he had been about to know something, and that it had gone out of him, and that his tongue felt thick.

He braced himself on the rail. The bear reared over him, jaws open.

"Down!"

Her voice came from above, flat and carrying, the voice of someone giving an order on a ledge. "Down! Both of you! Now!"

Light feet on the stair. Even, quick, taking nothing short.

She came out of the dusk at the head of the flight with her day kasavu hitched to the knee and the plain guild knife in her hand. Her hair was loose, her face bare. She was human, down to her breath. She had heard the clash through the shutters, and she had unbarred the door, and she had come.

Behind her the two stair-head men broke from their post at last, spears levelled, lamps swinging high, and light poured down the steps.

The bear saw them. It dropped to all fours. Its head swung up the stair, from lamp to lamp, to the spearheads and to the knife, and the little eyes in the great skull went flat. Three blades, and light. It did not roar. It turned, in one heave of its shoulders, and took the low wall at the landing in a single scrabbling vault. It was gone into the drop and the dark, with a sound of gravel going down.

Neela did not follow. She was already on her knees beside Raiu.

* * *

The boy was nineteen.

Devan had taken four months and asked twice to learn his name. He had stood in the yard and said it wrong, and Raiu had laughed, and it had been the best sound that week. A cowlick that never lay flat. A mother he spoke of at the fire.

Neela had both hands pressed on the torn chest, and the blood came up through her fingers. She said, "Raiu." Her voice was perfectly level. "Raiu, look at me."

He looked at her. His eyes went to Devan, above her shoulder, and some question was in them.

"You held the door," Devan said. It came out thick. "You held it, Raiu."

"Raiu." She said the name again, as steadily as a woman counting. "It's all right. Raiu."

The breath went out of him slowly. She said the name the whole time, until there was nothing left to say it to. Then she bowed her head over the boy's chest, and her hands did not move.

The two men stood on the steps with their lamps, not knowing where to put them. After them came Muthu, enormous and white about the mouth, shouting for the physician. Devan heard it as through water. He had sat down on the step without meaning to.

"Don't," Neela said, not looking up. "Don't try to stand."

"I'm awake."

"You're grey."

She was across the step in a moment, her bloody hand at his side. She opened his tunic with a jerk. The line across his ribs was thin, and the flesh at its lips had already gone the colour of a plum. A black thread was crawling out from the cut toward his belly.

He watched her see it. Her face did not change. Only her mouth went small.

* * *

Eravi came with his case, stooped and wheezing, and hung his lamp from the bedpost.

They had carried Devan up between them. Muthu took the head and a soldier took the feet, and he had let them, because the stair had begun to slide. Now he lay on the bed with his tunic cut away. Janu stood at the foot with her hands knotted in her apron. For once she had nothing to say.

The old physician sniffed the cut. He sniffed it again, and drew back.

"Blackbark gall," he said.

No one answered him.

"I read of it in my master's books. It is not a fort poison. It is..." He looked from Devan to the ceiling, not at Neela. "A sap, rendered, mixed with a vegetable gall. It does not kill quickly. It goes into the blood and walks slowly to the heart. I cannot draw it. I have not the knowledge, and I do not think anyone in this country has."

"How long?" Devan said.

"Three days. Perhaps a little more, if you lie still and are kept cool." Eravi's mouth worked. "And if you are very lucky."

Muthu made a low sound. Devan looked past him. Neela had come to the foot of the bed, beside Janu. Blood was drying on her wrists. She did not look at the physician.

"It's the guild's," she said. "Chathu's, from the groves. There's only one antidote, and he makes it himself. It's at the Resin House. In the tapping shed, in a cupboard with a lock I know how to open."

"That is a day's hard ride," Muthu said. "Through the wood."

"A day there. A day back. I have three." She said it as a sum. "I'll be back before the third dusk."

"No."

It was Devan's voice, and it was louder than he had meant. The room turned.

Neela only looked at him. She wore the face she wore when she had already counted the exits.

"Eravi," she said, "give him whatever will keep him still, and keep him cool. Janu, vinegar and cold cloths, and don't let the fever frighten you. It doesn't kill. Muthu." She paused, and he saw her choose, and he saw her check that the choice was hers. "I need to talk to you outside."

* * *

He should have stayed on the bed.

Eravi had put something bitter in a cup, and Devan had drunk half and spilled half and, when Janu turned to the basin, got his legs under him. The room swung. He went down the passage with a hand on the wall, the left side of his body a piece of cold wood. He kept hold of what he had: a torch rolling on a step, something dark at the wall. Where the rest should have been there was a gap, smooth as a swallowed stone. He reached for it and it slid away.

The stable smelled of dung and hay and lamp oil. A single lantern hung from a beam.

She was saddling the grey mare. She had changed into riding clothes, a dark mundu hitched high and a shawl crossed over her chest. Her knife was in her belt. Her hands moved on the girth with the speed of someone who had done this in the dark a hundred times. As he watched, she pulled the strap through, and her thumb flinched from the buckle, and he saw that three nails had split to the quick where there had been one this morning.

She did not look round.

"Go back to bed."

"You're going to the Resin House."

"Yes."

"The guild's house." His mouth did not want to make the words. He made them anyway. "The place that sold you, and made you, and wrote your name in a book. They know you didn't kill me. Vellan knows. By now, Chathu knows."

"I know."

"Then you know what you're riding into."

She turned. The lantern put gold on one side of her face, and the other side was dark. She looked at him for a long moment, at the way he leaned on the doorpost, at the colour of his lips.

"I do," she said. "I've been in that yard since I was six."

"Neela."

"Muthu will keep you cool. I'm trusting him with your body, not your secrets, and I hate it, but I have nobody else. I've told him what to do if I'm late. Don't argue."

"Muthu," he said. It was all the breath he could spare for the name he had been afraid of since the yard.

"I know what you think of him. I think it too." She tightened the strap. "He'll still not let you die in front of him. Whatever else he is."

"I don't want you—"

"I know what you want." There was no heat in it. She had gone gentle, and that was worse. "You'd have me sit here with your hand in mine and be the woman who could have gone. I won't be her. Nobody taught me this one, Devan. I'm choosing it."

She gathered the reins. He took a step, and the floor tilted, and he crossed the straw anyway. He caught the girth strap where her hand had been and touched one of the split nails. It was rough as bark. She let him.

He could not stop her, and he reached for her anyway.

His hand found her wrist. The pulse beat under his fingers, quick and living. The bone of it was narrow, the pale ridged scar there, and the gold bangle was warm.

"If you go there," he said, "they'll cage you."

He felt his grip begin to go, the way a rope goes slack in a wet hand. He tried to close it. His fingers would not answer. The word he had meant to say next lay on his tongue, and the dark came up over it.

"Neela, they'll ca—"

His hand opened.
