# Chapter 7: There's a Way, Chechi

The tune reached Neela before the girl did.

It came from the far end of the lower market, under the tamarind, thin and cheerful and a little flat: four rising notes, a drop, the same four again. A fishing song. *Pull and pull, the tide is coming in.* Ammu had hummed it through every bad night of their childhood, and Neela would have known it through a wall.

She did not turn her head. She finished with the lamp-oil man, who had shorted his measure by a thumb, and paid him the fair price with a smile that cost him the other thumb. Then she said to the boy at her elbow, "Unni. Isn't your mother's stall by the fish racks?"

Unni went the colour of a plum. He was thin as a ladle and not much past twenty. "Janu said I wasn't to leave you, lady."

"Janu said you weren't to lose me. The whole market can see me, and I can see the whole market. Go and let your mother feed you. I'll call it guarding."

He went almost at a run.

Thirty-four nights. She had counted them on waking, with the hollow shake of the morning still in her hands. She walked toward the tamarind slowly, a warden's wife with a market basket, and let the song get louder.

The stall was three sacks and a scale on a plank. Behind it sat a girl in a patched cotton wrap, her hair oiled flat, her face burned brown by a coast sun that had not been hers a month ago. A tally string hung at her belt. She looked up, and her whole face broke open and was put back together as a trader's.

"Chechi!" said Ammu. "Lady, I mean, lady. Come, come. For the warden's wife the good sack."

In the market, a poor girl might call a richer woman elder sister and nobody would blink. Neela took the stool Ammu kicked toward her. "What's your name, girl?"

"Valli, lady. Coast-side, up with the last cart before the passes shut." Her eyes went over Neela's face, her throat, the high collar, and came back satisfied. "Black pepper. Heavier than hill pepper. Lady has a nose for it, I hear."

"Who says?"

"The whole market. They say the warden's wife can grade a berry blind." Ammu began scooping into the scale, and her voice dropped under the clatter without losing its sing-song. "They say the warden is tall. They say he never smiles and never raises his voice, and the women at the well are split on whether that's a husband or a wall. Which is it, chechi?"

"He tells me the guard rota."

Ammu's scoop stopped. "He what?"

"Over supper. Unasked."

"The old man will faint with joy." Ammu choked the laugh down into the scale. "A handsome man who tells you where all the knives are. Chechi, you've married a gift."

"I've married a ledger." Neela turned a berry in her fingers. It was the coast kind, well dried. "Say what you came to say."

Ammu weighed the pepper twice, which no honest trader does. When she spoke, the sing-song was gone, and the voice underneath was soft and patient and a little sad. It was an excellent copy.

"*Tell Seventeen the writ is on the road.* That's him, word for word. *After it lands, every day counts double. If the account stands open when the pass closes, I'll put the resin on her myself, and at dusk I'll read her page aloud where she can hear it. I would grieve, child. I always do.*"

Neela did not move. She had been braced for it since the jackal's call in the dark, and it still went through her like cold water.

Forced resin was not a thing you forgot hearing described. The scars tore open. You bled for days. You turned because the sun went down, with a man standing in the room, and then he read the page.

She counted the sacks. Three. She counted the coins in her sleeve. Seven.

"What writ?" she said.

"He didn't say. He said it like it was a harvest." Ammu tied off the pepper. "Give me something for the cart, chechi."

There was a great deal she could have given her. That the sentry above the window had vanished two nights ago. That the hounds were kennelled at dusk. That there was a goat-boy's hand in her torn cloth, and a man at supper who had not stopped an old woman's leopard story. That a cat on a parapet had made her laugh until she cried, and a breath behind her had stumbled on the beat.

Every one of them was a thing she could not explain. Ammu would call the sentry luck and the hounds a quirk, and then she would carry them to Chathu, and Chathu would tell Neela what they meant.

"The weak hour is the last dark one," Neela said. "Before first light. I haven't proved it. I need ten days."

"Ten! He said—"

"He says what he says from a chair in the Wood. I'm the one in the room."

Ammu looked at her a moment longer than a trader should. Then she shrugged and reached for the cloth to wrap the packet. "Ten, then. I'll make it sound like nine."

She was humming again, tunelessly, the four notes over and over. Neela watched her fingers on the cloth. They were quick and square and had a nick across one knuckle that was new.

"Kirri's letters," Neela said. "Has she answered?"

The humming stopped.

"I wrote her again," Ammu said brightly. "That's three. Nothing yet. She's out on the water at dawn, hauling nets. She'll never have the hands for a pen again, she'll have rope-hands. And she couldn't spell *tide* when she was the big girl teaching us our letters." She tucked the packet into the basket with a flourish. "Busy, chechi. That's all. A woman with a boat has no time for sisters."

It was the right sort of joke, and it was a half beat too fast. The laugh after it was too round.

Neela said, "Three is a lot of letters for a woman with a boat."

"I'm a sister who pesters."

"You are."

They looked at each other over the pepper. The word *released* sat between them, and neither of them picked it up. It was the thing the twelfth contract bought. It was the door at the end of the road, the burned page and the birth name and the boat on the water. Neela had never in her life asked what stood on the other side of it, because there had never been anything to ask.

She heard herself speak, low and flat. "If anyone ever saw me, Ammu, I'd be finished. Release or no release. You know that."

She hadn't known she was going to say it. A breath had hitched, on the beat of a laugh. She wanted someone to tell her it was nothing.

Ammu's hands went quite still on the cloth. "Not always," she said.

"What?"

"Not always. There's a way, chechi." Her voice had gone very small and very fast. "It costs, it costs a great deal, but there's a way if the one who sees you is—"

She stopped.

She was not looking at Neela. She was looking past her shoulder, and her face had gone blank and pale.

Neela did not turn. She had spent twenty years not turning her head. She let her eyes go idle across the lane and came to the next stall along, three paces off, where a woman was selling turmeric in yellow heaps. A big man in a captain's plain coat was leaning on her plank with a hand full of her best root, laughing at something she had said.

Muthu.

"—costs two panam the measure," Ammu said loudly, "and that's cheap, lady, that's near robbery on my own mother!"

Neela leaned in over the scale, as if to haggle, and mouthed it. *What way?*

"Buy the pepper."

"Ammu."

"Buy the *pepper*, chechi." The brightness in her eyes was something close to fear. Neela had seen it twice in twenty years. The last time was the night of a first turning, when a fourteen-year-old girl had held her friend's hand in a locked room and watched what she became. "It's a market story. It's nothing. I talk."

It was not nothing. There were few things the Resin House punished harder than failure, and one of them was telling a member what the masters had not. Whatever Ammu had been about to say, she had learned it somewhere she was not meant to, and she had nearly said it three paces from a captain of the warden's own guard.

"Lady Neela!" Muthu had seen her. He swung round, still grinning, turmeric yellow on his palm. "Down among the honest thieves. Where's your shadow?"

"Eating fish," said Neela. Her voice came out as pleasant as a bell. "I let him off."

"Poor Unni. He'll talk about it for a year." He looked at the stall and at Ammu, and Ammu bowed her head and became the least interesting girl in the market. "Who's this?"

"Valli. Coast pepper. She's honest, which is rare."

"Is she? Then I'll take four measures for the mess." He dropped coins on the plank without counting them. "The warden hates market day, did you know? He says it's all noise. I say that's the point of it." He winked. "He'll be along. Bet you a copper. He's heard I'm down here with the garrison's purse and he can't bear it."

"Why would he come down for you?"

"Oh, not for me." Muthu laughed his big laugh and the pigeons went up from the tamarind. "Valli, four measures, and don't skimp."

Ammu measured. Neela watched her measure, and the girl did not look up once, and under her breath she hummed. It was the fishing song. Pull, and pull, and the tide was coming in.

* * *

He came at the foot of the lane, as Muthu had said, in his plain brown coat with no sword and no escort. He stopped at the edge of the market and looked at nothing, the way a man looks when he has been told where to find something.

Neela was a long way from the stall by then, with a basket of lamp oil, turmeric and pepper on her arm, and she had told herself she would not look back. She looked back. Ammu had a customer, an old man with a goat, and her head was bent.

"Warden," she said.

"Neela." He fell into step beside her. "Unni says you let him off."

"You've been speaking to him."

"He was coming up the road with a pomfret. He looked as if he'd been given a kingdom." A pause. "I had business with the salt factor."

"Down here."

"He keeps his tallies down here."

It was a good answer, and it was true as far as she could tell. It also did not explain why a man who hated market day had left the fort with no escort on the one afternoon she was out of it. By any count she made, there were three ways he could have known where to find her, and only one was an accident.

*Followed*, she thought. And then: *or he came for his captain.* And then, coldly, because it was how she had been taught: *or he came to watch whom I talked to.*

Her fingers tightened on the basket. He glanced at them, then at her face, and held out his hand for it.

"I can carry it," she said.

"I know." His hand stayed where it was.

She let go a beat too late. He took the weight without comment, but she saw him note the beat, and she saw that he had decided not to mention it. She noticed herself noticing, and walked on.

They went up the road through the lower village, where the houses were laterite and thatch and the cattle moved aside for no one, and a child stood in a doorway with a stick in her fist, guarding a hen.

The woman was at the third house. Neela knew her at once, without having been introduced, because she had stood next to her at the well for a quarter of an hour two mornings ago. Parukutty. Mid-thirties, a bad shoulder from the water pots, a daughter of six and a son of nine. Her husband had been a spearman on the wall, and he had been dead since spring.

A man stood in front of her door with a slate under his arm. He had the soft voice and the sad, level face of a collector. "I'm not unfeeling," he was saying. "The factor isn't unfeeling. But it's the fourth month, Parukutty. It's six measures and a half, and he can only carry a thing so long."

"After harvest," she said. "The queen's gift comes after harvest."

"The factor doesn't wait for harvest."

The warden's step slowed. He said, "Wait here," as if he had remembered a small errand, and put the basket back into her hands, and walked up the lane.

He did not hurry. He did not raise his voice. He spoke to the collector for no longer than it takes to say a name, and the man's face changed, and he took a cord of coins from Devan's hand without looking at the amount. The slate came out from under his arm. A thumb wiped it. Parukutty stood in her doorway holding her shoulder and watched the slate go clean, and did not understand it. The collector said something low and quick, and bowed, and went down the lane the other way.

The warden was already walking back.

He passed the doorway without a glance. The woman called after the collector once, bewildered, and then went in, and a moment later a child's voice rose, high and delighted, about the hen.

"Raman," Neela said, when he reached her. "That was her husband's name."

"Yes."

"Six measures and a half of salt."

"And a tally fee." He took the basket again, and this time she let it go on time. "The factor adds a charge when a debt passes a month."

"The queen's death-gift would have paid it."

"At harvest." He walked. "It's three months till harvest."

The road was steep here, and red dust rose around their feet. She was thinking of tins of jaggery sweets, warm from the kitchen, sent before a death. *For luck, child.* She was thinking of how every gift she had ever been given had arrived with its price written in a hand she couldn't read.

"Do you know every widow's debt?" she said. She meant it as a test, and made it light so it would not sound like one.

"Four widows on the wall rolls owe the factor," said Devan. "That was the largest. The other three I will see to by the new moon."

"By the new moon." She kept her eyes on the road. "You've done your sums."

"Yes."

"And you'll tell no one, and this one won't know whom to thank."

"That is the arrangement."

"A tidy arrangement," she said dryly. "It leaves nobody anything to hold against you."

His step checked, once, and went on. He did not look at her. But the corner of his mouth moved, as it had over the green wax, and she understood that she had named the thing he'd left unsaid, and that he knew she had. It was not a comfortable thing to have been understood by.

"Why?" she said.

He didn't pretend not to follow. "He died on my patrol."

"That's a date, not a reason."

"It's the reason I have."

He said it as he said everything: exact, unpadded, with nothing in it to hold on to. If she had thanked him he would have gone out of the room. She had known him six days, and she already knew it. He had not looked behind him once to see whether the woman saw.

It was the fourth thing he had done like that. The pulled sentry. The hounds. The shutters barred at dusk. And now this. Each time she had put it down to a hunter's craft, a man laying out a long patience for a long purpose. A hunter did lay out patience. She had never known a hunter to wipe a stranger's slate and tell no one.

She thought: *he is keeping a book.* But Chathu kept a book too, and his was the kind that came to collect. This one, she was beginning to think, was the sort a man keeps against himself.

She did not want to think it. It was inconvenient. She had thirty-four nights and a man to kill, and a pair of hands that were still not quite steady from the dawn.

"You're quiet," said the warden.

"I'm counting."

"What?"

"Steps." It was half true. She'd lost the count at the well.

He nodded as though that were a reasonable thing for a bride to do. Up the road ahead, the laterite wall of Kettamala rose red against the sky, with the padippura dark at its foot. The cistern chain clanked the hour across the fields.

At the gate he stopped, and held out the basket. She took it. Her fingers closed on the handle and on the pepper packet wedged beside the oil jar, and found Ammu's knot through the cloth. Not a trader's single tie. A fisherman's double hitch, the one two girls had once learned from a net-mender behind the sheds, and had tied on everything for a summer.

*There's a way, chechi, but it costs.*

The chain clanked again. The warden went in under the arch, and Neela stood a moment in its shadow with her thumb on the knot. She did not undo it.

Tonight she would take the second spur, and she would leave a written question under the meeting stone, whatever it cost Ammu to answer.
