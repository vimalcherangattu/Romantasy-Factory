# Chapter 23: The Keeper's Name

By the time the sun cleared the east wall, Devan had run the long form eleven times and still could not make his hands stop.

Last night was in them. Her wrist under his thumb, the ridges at her throat, her fist in the sheet. When the form ended the eleventh time, he stood in the red earth with his chest heaving and the lamps burning pale in their niches, and heard a step on the stairs cut into the north side.

He knew it. Light, even, taking the third stair a fraction short, the way she took every third thing, as if shortening a stride could make her smaller.

Neela came down into the pit in her day kasavu with a shawl over it. There was red dust on the hem and a burr caught in the border. She had been up the hill. Her face was set the way it was when she was counting something.

"You've been at it since the dark," she said.

"Yes."

"The hands wouldn't stop."

"No."

She nodded, as though that were a reasonable answer. Then she put her hand into her bodice and drew out a small, soft, creased slip of paper.

She did not hold it out at once. She looked at it, and then at him. He had learned in a month to read the order of her decisions. She chose, and then she checked that the choice was still hers.

"You said you wouldn't ask," she said.

"I won't."

"I know. That's why." She crossed the earth between them and put the paper into his hand. Her fingers were cold. "Ittan. Councillor Ittan, the Keeper of the Passes. He bought the contract. He is the man who wants you dead."

He did not look down at the slip. He looked at her.

She had said it flat, the way she said sums. But her eyes were wide, a little wild, the eyes of a woman who has just stepped off a wall and not yet landed.

"I didn't buy it," he said. It came out stupid and he knew it.

"No. You didn't ask, either. I'm not trading, Devan. I'm owed nothing for this." She wrapped her arms round herself. "I just don't want you to die of a man I could have named."

Something in his chest turned over. He had offered her his silence for this name once, in a wet dawn, and she had refused it. He had never expected the name to follow him into the pit, unasked, in her hand.

"Thank you," he said. It was too small.

"Read it."

He unfolded the slip. A neat hand in black ink, tidy as an accounts book. *Balance due from the Keeper on the widow's seal.*

"Where did you get this?"

"A guild-sister tore it off a carrier's satchel. It's Chathu's hand. I won't say more about her." She said it quickly, then lowered her voice. "I'm sorry. That's the one thing I can't give you."

"I haven't asked."

"I know you haven't." The corner of her mouth moved. "It's very annoying."

He looked at the paper again, and the room he had lived in for eight years rearranged itself.

*The widow's seal.* The old charter line, the one the marriage writ had returned to him in plain words. If the warden of Kettamala died married, his wife held the pass-seal in trust until the council named a new man. No warden in living memory had married in office. Someone had read that line and seen what it was worth.

Someone had *sent* him a wife.

The pieces did not fall into place with a click. They slid, heavy and cold, one against the other, as if they had always lain in that order and he had been too close to see.

Council wax, brick red, poured with clove oil, pressed with a pepper sprig. The dot-and-split mark cut at the stem of one of three boxes, and the Keeper's office kept one of the three. Genial letters, season after season, six years of them, *my friend*, never once climbing the hill. And the match itself, pressed on him by the council, with Ittan's name first on the letter that proposed it. He had noted the name then. He had thought: *a man being kind.*

A bride to kill him in his own chamber, so that a woman who owned nothing would hold the one seal in Kettamala. And who would come to take it from her? The man who had arranged for her to have it.

"He doesn't want me dead," Devan said slowly. "Or not first. He wants the seal. Every cart that goes through the Monkey Pass on a wax tally goes through whoever holds it." His voice came from a long way off. "He wanted a widow, not a corpse."

"A widow who'd be given a kind man's help," Neela said. "A weeping girl from a pepper house, and a councillor who'd ride up to console her. Who'd take the seal off her hands for safekeeping."

"And if she'd refused?"

"I was to be gone by then. One more leopard in the hills."

He gripped the slip. For a moment he did not trust his hands near her, and he put them behind him.

"Asha found the first token," he said. "Six years ago. She was dead on the west stair within the month."

"Yes."

"He has been writing me kind letters for six years."

Neela did not tell him it was not his fault. She stood and let him have it. When he looked up she was a pace away, near enough to touch and not touching. It was what he had wanted from the beginning: to be told the thing, and not be soothed.

He went for the stair.

He didn't decide to. His body was already on the north steps, the paper in his fist. He thought: Kodiyur is four days at a hard ride. A warden's writ. The queen's household. Muthu to hold the gate.

"Devan."

"Four days. I can be in the capital before the moon is dark."

"Devan, wait."

"If I put the slip before the council—"

"It's Chathu's hand on a scrap that says *the Keeper*." She had come up the steps behind him, her voice low and fast. "That's a title, and he has a dozen men under it. You'd stand there with a torn strip from a guild satchel, and they'd ask where it came from, and you'd have to say a woman of the Resin House gave it you. In your own bed."

He turned on the third step.

"And look at the road. The pass is sealed in eight days, and the rains close it behind. A warden who rides to the capital with the monsoon at his back doesn't ride home. You'd leave Kettamala shut in with the sealing day coming and nobody but Muthu and a goat-boy." She drew breath. "And you don't need to go to him. He's coming to us."

He stood on the step and let that settle.

"The sealing," he said.

"Every year a clerk climbs. This year he wrote that he'd come himself." She had been in the room that night, on her side of the bed with her face to the wall, while he read the letter aloud to the dark without thinking. "Why does a man who has never climbed suddenly want to seal the pass with his own hand? Because there's a balance due, and he doesn't trust a clerk to collect it."

*I shall come myself this season, my friend, to see how the match has prospered.* He had thought it kindly meant.

"He's coming to see whether you're dead," Devan said.

"Or whether I'm still useful." She said it without pity for herself. "Either way he walks up the hill on his own feet. But there's a count, Devan. The new moon is six nights off. The Keeper comes two days after it. Chathu won't wait for him. At the moon the account closes, and he sends someone to close it." Her thumb found the nail of her forefinger. "So the trap has to live through the moon before it can catch anyone."

The anger went out of him, not gone but turned, the way a river is turned by a sluice. He had wanted something to hit. He sat down on the stair, because his legs had gone strange.

"There is a better place for this," he said. "The queen."

He saw her go still.

"Not a slip. You. In person, before the court. The Resin House, the contract, the whole account of who buys. No one knows it as you do. If the queen hears it from an odiyan's own mouth—"

"No."

"Neela—"

"No." She did not say it loudly. It had the weight of a thing she had known all her life. "Think what you're asking. A confessed odiyan before the crown, with no master to vouch for her. You know what the law calls that."

He knew.

"A rogue," she said. "Hunted by guild and crown both. The second I say what I am in that hall, I'm a thing your queen is entitled to cage. You'd have to choose between the crown and me, and I'd never do that to you." She flexed her hand. "And it isn't only me. Every page in that ledger is a life. If I stand up in the queen's hall and say *odiyan*, Chathu doesn't need to wait for the moon. He opens the book. My sister's page is on the next leaf."

She did not say a name. He did not reach for one.

He wanted to argue. Asha had written, in her fast, laughing hand, *The truth protects whoever finds it,* and Devan had built six years on it. Here was the truth, a woman who could lay out the whole crooked house, and she was telling him that in this country it was paid for with another person's neck.

*Two honest things,* he thought. *Neither of them wrong.* The law he served had a hole in it exactly her size.

"Your word is the one thing I can't take from you," he said. "I don't want it as evidence if that's the cost." He stopped. "I'm not saying I like it. I'm saying the stake is yours, and it's yours to name."

She let out a long breath. She looked at him as though she had braced for a blow and found a hand instead.

"You'd have made a good witness at the House," she said. "They'd have hated you for it."

"Caged me, I expect."

"Eventually." Her mouth tugged. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Help me. If I can't carry you to the queen and can't ride to the Keeper, I have to bring him here and catch him in front of my own men, under my own law. Tell me how."

She sat beside him on the stone step, with the oil lamps guttering and the red earth drying in patches.

"Your law," she said. "What's the oldest thing in it?"

He thought. It was good to think about something that was only a problem. "Border law. The pass stands under the warden's seal, and any cart at the padippura is the warden's to search. On sealing day it's a custom older than the charter: every cart that passes the gate that day, in or out, is opened. Nobody has ever been refused. Nobody has ever been a councillor."

"Does it say *every* cart?"

"It says every." He saw where she was going and felt the first spark of something he had not felt in a long time, a plan with a shape. "It doesn't say except the Keeper's."

"Then his cart comes up with his baggage and his clerks," Neela said, "and your men open every box. Not because you accuse him. Because it's the day, and it's the law, and it would be rude to wave one cart through." She smiled, small and hard. "In front of the whole garrison."

"And if he carries nothing?"

"A man who comes to collect a widow's seal brings his instruments. A seal box. A purse of tallies for the right hand. Whoever pours the wax needs the box, and whoever owns it keeps it close."

"He might leave it behind."

"Then we've lost a day. But he's walked up a hill to see a dead warden and found a live one." She leaned her shoulder against his, briefly, an offer. "And I'll watch the road at night. I'll know who's coming before the gate does."

"Muthu will hold the gate that day," Devan said. "He'll open the councillor's boxes himself, and do it smiling, because he can't help it. I'll tell him it's the custom."

"It *is* the custom."

"Yes. That's why it's good."

They sat a while. The sun had come over the wall and lay in a strip across the pit. He was aware of her shoulder, the dust on her hem. He thought of the nail she pressed against her thumb in sleep, and of the nights she counted toward something she had not named. She had given him a name unasked. She had not given him that. He did not ask.

It was strange to find that not asking could be an act as large as asking.

"Neela."

"Mm."

"This doesn't bind you. If Ittan walks into the trap and we take him, nothing is owed from you. No second price. I'll say so before anyone."

"I know." She was quiet. "That's why I gave it."

She stood and brushed the dust from her palms. She glanced round at the earth, the lamps, the worn marks of eight years of one man's feet. Then she said it casually, as if asking about weather.

"Spar with me."

The question went through him like a draught through a door.

She hadn't asked to win. She had watched him from the east steps and the rim of the pit, and from a feed bin in the rain. She had named what he was afraid of. Now she put her hand to the lock, lightly, and left the opening to him.

His hands had gone cold. In his mind there was a mud-bank and a man on his knees and two fingers going to a notch beneath an ear. And beside it, patient, a woman who had said *you stopped.*

"Not yet," he said.

He heard how it sounded. He braced for the flat look he deserved.

She only nodded, slowly. He watched her hear the difference. *Not yet*, not *no*. He had said it in a voice that left the door unbarred, and neither of them had to remark on it.

"All right," she said. "Not yet."

She went up the steps. At the top she looked back, the sun behind her, the burr still caught in her border.

"Eat something. You've been at it since the dark."

He gave her the count of fifty, then climbed after, the slip folded small in his fist. Six nights to the moon, he thought. Eight days to the seal. The plan needed a gate, and the gate needed a man who smiled.

At the head of the stair the fort lay open to him, the cistern chain clanking its hour. Down at the padippura a rider sat a lathered horse that had no business there. It was not the ninth day, not post day. Captain Muthu stood at the stirrup with his big back to the yard, pressing a letter sealed in plain grey wax into the rider's glove.

He was not laughing. Muthu, who laughed at everything, stood perfectly quiet.

Devan stood on the stair with the Keeper's name in his hand, and for the first time he wondered who else in Kettamala had been told he was coming.
