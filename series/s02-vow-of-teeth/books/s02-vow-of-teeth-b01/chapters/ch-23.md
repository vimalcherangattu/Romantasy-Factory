# Chapter 23: The Keeper's Name

By the time the sun cleared the east wall, Devan had run the long form eleven times and still could not make his hands stop.

Last night was in them. Her wrist under his thumb, the ridges at her throat, her fist in the sheet. He had never taken so long over a drill, and he had never been so far from the pit while standing in it. When the form ended for the eleventh time, he stood in the red earth with his chest heaving and the lamps burning pale in their niches, and he heard a step on the stairs cut into the north side.

He knew it. Light, even, taking the third stair a fraction short, the way she took every third thing, as if shortening a stride could make her smaller.

Neela came down into the pit in her day kasavu with a shawl over it. There was red dust on the hem and a burr caught in the border. She had been up the hill. Her face was set the way it was when she was counting something.

"You've been at it since the dark," she said.

"Yes."

"The hands wouldn't stop."

"No."

She nodded, as though that were a reasonable answer. Then she put her hand into her bodice, and what she drew out was a small, soft, creased slip of paper.

She did not hold it out at once. She looked at it, and then at him. He had learned in forty days to read the order of her decisions. She chose, and then she checked that the choice was still hers.

"You said you wouldn't ask," she said.

"I won't."

"I know. That's why." She crossed the earth between them, and put the paper into his hand. Her fingers were cold. "Ittan. Councillor Ittan, the Keeper of the Passes. He bought the contract. He is the man who wants you dead."

He did not look down at the slip. He looked at her.

She had said it flat, the way she said sums. But her eyes were not flat. They were wide, a little wild, the eyes of a woman who has just stepped off a wall and not yet landed.

"I didn't buy it," he said. It came out stupid and he knew it.

"No. You didn't ask, either. I'm not trading, Devan. There's no bargain on this. I'm not owed a thing for it." She wrapped her arms round herself. "I just don't want you to die of a man I could have named."

Something in his chest turned over. He had offered her his silence for this name once, in a wet dawn, and she had refused it. He had given the silence away afterwards for nothing. He had never expected the name to follow him down into the pit, unasked, in her hand.

"Thank you," he said. It was the only thing he had, and it was too small.

"Read it."

He unfolded the slip. A neat hand in black ink, tidy as an accounts book. *Balance due from the Keeper on the widow's seal.*

"Where did you get this?"

"A guild-sister tore it off a carrier's satchel. It's Chathu's hand. I won't say more about her." She said it quickly, and then lowered her voice. "I'm sorry. That's the one thing I can't give you."

"I haven't asked."

"I know you haven't." The corner of her mouth moved. "It's very annoying."

He looked at the paper again, and the room he had lived in for eight years rearranged itself.

*The widow's seal.* The old charter line, the one the marriage writ had returned to him in plain words. If the warden of Kettamala died married, his wife held the pass-seal in trust until the council named a new man. No warden in living memory had married in office. Someone had read that line and seen what it was worth.

Someone had *sent* him a wife.

He stood very still. The pieces did not fall into place with a click. They slid, heavy and cold, one against the other, as if they had always lain in that order and he had been too close to them.

Council wax, brick red, poured with clove oil, pressed with a pepper sprig. The dot-and-split mark on the forged tallies was cut at the stem of one of three boxes, and the Keeper's office kept one of the three. The genial letters, season after season, six years of them, *my friend*, never once climbing the hill. The match pressed on him by the council, and Ittan's name had stood first on the letter that proposed it. He had noted the name then. He had thought: *a man being kind.*

And a bride who was to kill him in his own chamber, so that a woman who owned nothing would hold the one seal in Kettamala. Who would come to take it from her? The man who had arranged for her to have it.

"He doesn't want me dead," Devan said slowly. "Or not first. He wants the seal. The pass. Every cart that goes through the Monkey Pass with a wax tally goes through whoever holds that seal." He heard his own voice come out of a long way off. "He wanted a widow, not a corpse."

"A widow who'd be given a kind man's help," Neela said. "A weeping girl from a pepper house, and a councillor who'd ride up to console her. Who'd take the seal off her hands for safekeeping."

"And if she'd refused?"

"I don't think the question came up." She said it drily. "I was to be gone by then. One more leopard in the hills."

He stopped. Gripped the slip. For a moment he did not trust his hands near her, and he put them behind him.

"Asha found the first token," he said. "Six years ago. She was killed on the west stair the month after."

"Yes."

"His wax. His box." He could hardly say it. "He has been writing me kind letters for six years."

Neela did not tell him it was not his fault, or that he could not have known. She stood and let him have it. When he looked up she was there, a pace away, near enough to touch and not touching. It was the thing he had wanted from the beginning: to be told the thing, and not be soothed.

He went for the stair.

He didn't decide to. His body was already on the north steps, the paper in his fist, his feet making the sound of a man going to saddle a horse. He thought: Kodiyur is four days at a hard ride. A warden's writ. The queen's household. Muthu to hold the gate. A man in a council robe has no place to hide when a warden comes at the gallop with the evidence in his teeth.

"Devan."

"Four days. I can be in the capital before the new moon."

"Devan, wait."

"If I put the slip before the council—"

"It's Chathu's hand on a scrap of paper that says *the Keeper*." She had come up the steps behind him. Her voice was low and fast. "That's not a name. That's a title, and he has a dozen under him. You'd stand in front of the council with a torn strip from a guild satchel, and they'd ask where it came from, and you'd have to say a woman of the Resin House gave it you. In your own bed."

He turned round on the third step.

"And look at the road," she went on. "The pass closes in eight days. Eight. A warden who rides down to the capital with the monsoon behind him doesn't ride back. You'd leave Kettamala shut in with the sealing day coming and nobody here but Muthu and a goat-boy." She drew breath. "And you don't need to go to him. He's coming to us."

He stood on the step and let that settle.

"The sealing," he said.

"Every year a clerk climbs. This year, you told me, he wrote that he'd come himself." She had remembered it. She had been in the room that night, on her side of the bed, with her face to the wall, and he had said it aloud to the dark without thinking. "Why does a man who has never climbed suddenly want to see the bride and seal the pass with his own hand? Because there's a balance due, and he doesn't trust a clerk to collect it."

He thought of the letter. *I shall come myself this season, my friend, to see how the match has prospered.* He had read it to her over supper and called it kindly meant.

"He's coming to see whether you're dead," Devan said.

"Or whether I'm still useful." She said it without pity for herself. "Either way he's walking up the hill with his own feet. All we have to do is be ready."

He came down off the step. He felt the anger go out of him, not gone but turned, the way a river is turned by a sluice. It was a relief and a loss at once. He had wanted something to hit.

"There is a better place to put this," he said. "The queen."

He saw her go still.

"Not a slip," he said, and tried to keep it level. "You. In person, before the queen's court. The Resin House, the contract, the whole account of what the guild is and who buys from it. There's no one who knows it as you do. If the queen hears it from an odiyan's own mouth—"

"No."

"Neela—"

"No." She said it not loudly. It had the weight of a thing she had known all her life. "Think what you're asking. A confessed odiyan, standing before the crown. Do you know what the crown calls one who says it aloud without a master to vouch for her?"

He knew.

"A rogue," she said. "Hunted by the guild and the crown both, by law. The second I say what I am in that hall, I'm a thing the warden's own queen is entitled to put in a cage. And you'd have to stand there and watch the guards come, with your whole honour on the floor beside me."

"I would not let them."

"You'd have to choose between the crown and me. And you wouldn't need to, because I'd never do that to you." She flexed her hand. "It isn't only me. Every page in that ledger is a life, and mine sits beside Ammu's. If I stand up and say *odiyan* in the queen's hall, Chathu doesn't need to wait for the moon. He opens the book. Ammu's page is on the next leaf."

Her voice cracked on the name. She did not seem to notice she'd said it, and he took care not to notice either.

He wanted to argue. Asha had written, in her fast, laughing hand, *The truth protects whoever finds it,* and Devan had believed it for six years and built a life on it. Say it anyway. It was his creed, the thing he would have said if anyone had asked him what he was for. Here was the truth, a woman who could lay out the whole crooked house, and she was telling him that in this country the truth was something you paid for with another person's neck.

*Two honest things,* he thought. *Neither of them wrong.* It was a cold way to find out that the law he served had a hole in it exactly her size.

He thought of the man who sat in his chair in the capital and asked for the whole story, and he thought of Ammu on the next leaf.

"Your word is the one thing I can't take from you," he said slowly. "I don't want it as evidence if the cost is that." He stopped. "I'm not saying I like it. I'm saying you hold the stake, and it's yours to name."

She let out a long breath. She looked at him as though she had braced for a blow and found a hand instead.

"You'd have made a good witness at the House," she said. "They'd have hated you for it."

"I'd have been caged for it, I expect."

"Eventually." Her mouth tugged. Then it eased. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Help me." He sat down on the stair. His legs had gone strange. "If I can't carry you to the queen and I can't ride to the Keeper, I have to bring the Keeper here and catch him with my own hands, in front of my own men, under my own law. Tell me how."

She sat beside him. They were two people on a stone step with the oil lamps guttering and the red earth drying in patches.

"Your law," she said. "What's the oldest thing in it?"

He thought. It was a relief to think about something that was only a problem. "Border law. The pass stands under the warden's seal, and any cart that comes through the padippura is the warden's to search. It's the first thing a recruit learns. A man is passed on a tally, but the warden can open the cart if he cares to."

"Any cart?"

"On a normal day I'd never exercise it on a councillor." He paused. "Sealing day. Every cart that goes through the padippura on the day the pass is sealed is searched before it leaves the gate. It's a custom, not a courtesy. The old wardens wrote it into the charter: no goods leave the hills unsealed on the day the seal goes down."

"Does it say *every* cart?"

"It says every." He saw where she was going and felt the first spark of something he had not felt in a long time, a plan with a shape. "It doesn't say except the Keeper's."

"Then the Keeper's cart comes in," Neela said, "with his baggage and his clerks, and your men open every box on it. Not because you accuse him. Because it's the day, and it's the law, and it would be rude to let one cart pass on the sealing day without the same courtesy as the rest." She smiled, a small hard curve. "In front of the whole garrison."

"And if he carries nothing?"

"A man who comes to collect a widow's seal brings his instruments. A seal box, I'd think. Or a purse of tallies to hand to the right man." She tilted her head. "You've said yourself. Real council wax, clove oil. Whoever pours it needs the box. Whoever sells it keeps the box close."

"He might leave it behind."

"He might. Then we've lost nothing but a day, and he's spent a week of his own scheme finding a warden who isn't dead." She leaned her shoulder against his, briefly, an offer. "I'll watch the road at night. I'll know who's coming before the gate does."

He turned the shape over. It was simple, which was why it might work. It used the oldest rule he owned. It put the law in front of a man who had spent his life treating the law as a price list.

"Muthu will have to hold the gate that day," he said. "He'll have to open the councillor's boxes himself, and he will do it with a smile, because he can't help it." He thought of the laugh. "I'll tell him it's the custom."

"It *is* the custom."

"Yes. That's why it's good."

They sat a while. The sun had come over the wall and lay in a strip across the pit floor. He was aware of her, the weight of the shoulder, the dust on her hem. He thought of the nail she pressed against her thumb in sleep, and of eight nights she counted toward something she had not named. She had given him the name unasked. She had not given him that. He did not ask.

It was an odd thing to find that not asking could be an act as large as asking.

"Neela."

"Mm."

"This doesn't bind you." He chose the words. "The name. If Ittan walks into my trap and we take him, nothing is owed from you. No second price. I'll say so before anyone."

"I know." She was quiet. "That's why I gave it."

She stood up. She brushed the dust off her palms, glanced down at the earth, the lamps, the old worn marks of eight years of one man's feet. Then she said it casually, as if asking about the weather.

"Spar with me."

He felt the question go through him like a blade through cloth.

She hadn't asked because she wanted to win. She had watched him from the east steps and from the rim of the pit, and from a feed bin in the rain. She had named what he was afraid of. Now she was putting her hand to the lock, lightly, and leaving the opening to him.

His hands had gone cold. His throat hurt. In his mind there was a mud-bank and a man on his knees and two fingers going to a notch beneath an ear. And beside it, standing there patiently, a woman who had said *you stopped.*

"Not yet," he said.

He heard how it sounded. He braced for the flat look he deserved.

She only nodded, slowly. Then she looked at him, properly, and he saw her hear the difference. He had said *not yet*, not *no*. He had said it in a voice that left the door unbarred, and neither of them had to remark on it.

"All right," she said. "Not yet."

She went up the steps. At the top she turned and looked back at him, the sun behind her, a burr still caught in her border.

"Eat something," she said. "You've been at it since the dark."

He stayed in the pit a long time after she had gone, with the slip in his fist and the lamps going out one by one. He was thinking of a councillor on a road, and a custom older than either of them. For the first time in eight years he was thinking of the hours ahead as something to be done with someone else.
