# Chapter 26: The Kennels

He did not finish the word. His hand opened on her wrist, and Neela caught his weight before the straw could.

"Muthu!"

The captain came at a run from the stable door, where he had been standing with his arms folded and his big face gone quiet. He took Devan under the shoulders without being told. Between them they got him down flat on a hay bale, his cheek against the sack.

"He's only gone under," she said. "The poison does that. Keep him cool. Vinegar, wet cloths, and the shutters open, whatever Janu says. Don't let Eravi bleed him again. He has no blood to spare."

Muthu nodded. He was not laughing. In five weeks of barracks noise she had never seen his mouth so still, and she knew what the stillness meant, because Devan had told her. She looked at him anyway, straight, and said the rest.

"I told him I'd be back before the third dusk. Forget waiting for me. At first light tomorrow, put him in a cart and start up the wood road. Meet me on it coming back. It saves a day he hasn't got."

"Neela—"

"First light. Not later." She pulled the girth tight, and the mare blew. "I'm trusting you with something I can't spare. Don't make me sorry."

"I won't." He said it low, with his eyes on Devan's face, and she could not tell what it was worth. She had no one else to ask.

She swung up. Bhaskaran opened the wicket at the padippura without a word. Then there was only the road and the dark, and the cistern chain clanking the hour behind her like a door being shut.

* * *

Rain began before dawn.

She rode the Monkey road downhill with the mare's breath smoking and her nails throbbing at the quick. By morning the ruts ran like streams. By noon the water had found her spine. She counted milestones, then breaths, then the mare's strides between breaths. It kept her from counting how many hours Devan had left.

Three days, Eravi had said. Perhaps.

She trusted no inn. She walked the mare through the fords and ate the heel of bread Janu had pushed into her saddlebag. A fever she had not given in to for weeks began to climb, and she let it climb, and rode.

By late afternoon the road forked at a leaning stone, and she took the narrower way. The trees changed. They went black at the foot, as though soaked in ink, then all the way up. Their bark hung in plates, and the plates wept. The smell reached her before the shadows did: bitter, scorched, like tamarind left on a fire, with pine sap running underneath. She had breathed it every day from six years old to twenty-seven. It was the smell of her bed and her bowl and the first time a man held her wrist still.

*Blackbark Wood,* she thought. *Home.*

She had never hated a word so much.

She tied the mare in a thicket a mile out and went in on foot along the old drain path she and Ammu had used as children, when the cook was asleep.

* * *

The Resin House rose out of the wet wood in tiers of dark roof, with tapping sheds along the east side and the long training yard lying open between them like a swept palm. No lamps burned in the yard. They never had. Chathu said light was for those with something to show.

She went over the wall by the old fig and crossed the dark without a sound. The third shed had a loose hasp and a lock she had learned to open at fourteen, on a dare, with a pin and a held breath. It still opened for her.

Inside, the smell was thick enough to chew. At the back stood the cupboard, plain deal, with a brass lock the size of her thumb. She worked her knife tip in and felt the wards give one by one. Shelves of small clay vials, stoppered with wax, each tagged in the same fine hand she had once copied letters from. *Fever, young. Fever, old. Black gall, counter.* Chathu labelled everything. She had never found it funny until now.

She took the last one, no longer than her finger, with a pinch in the wax where his thumb had pressed. She pocketed it and locked the cupboard behind her. Her skin crawled to the roots of her hair.

*Eleven for eleven,* Vellan had said. *You've never missed.* She had walked in, found it and taken it. It had all been too quiet and too easy.

She stepped out into the yard. The rain had stopped. A smell came across the wet earth that was not resin. Wet fur and old musk, it sat much lower in her mind than a smell had a right to, a long way down, where she kept being six.

The bear stood in the middle of the yard.

It was upright, which they seldom were, its black shoulders roped with wet, the pale Y of its chest showing. Its head swung slowly to find her. *It was on the wall that night,* she thought. *He ran to be here first.*

It did not charge. It stood and let her see it.

Behind it, lamps opened, and a dozen men came out of the long shed with hooked poles and nets and iron rings on chains. She knew three of them by name. She had eaten beside Pachan at the long table when she was ten, and he had given her his sweet.

She had the knife out before she had decided anything. She took the first man at the wrist, the second at the knee, the third under the nose with the heel of her hand. She was fast. She was always fast. But she had ridden a night and a day on bread and fever, her hands were shaking, and a net dropped across her head from behind like a sheet.

They pulled her down. A knee found her back. Someone wrenched the knife away, and someone else tore at the fold of her shawl. She heard herself make a sound as the vial came out and was held up in the lamplight.

"There it is," said Pachan. He did not sound unkind. "Master said she'd come for it."

*Master said.*

The bear dropped to all fours and went into the shadow without looking back.

* * *

The kennels lay under the oldest roof, behind the cook-house and a shuttered well. She had been told once what they were for. She had never been inside.

They carried her in by the arms, the net still tangled in her hair. A lamp burned at the end of a long aisle between iron cages, floor to roof, with straw and a bucket in each. A deer lay curled in the nearest, its hide gone patchy. Beyond it were a hare, a mongoose, something striped, and a very thin fox that did not lift its head.

Their eyes shone as she passed. They did not follow her. That was the worst of it. They looked at the lamp and the hunters' feet and the wall, as if they had once been looking for something and had forgotten what.

The hunters unlocked a cage near the end, put her inside, and knotted a leather cord from her wrists to a ring in the wall, high enough that she had to stand. Pachan tested the knot.

"I'm sorry, Seventeen," he said. He meant it, and it made no difference. They took the lamp away.

She waited until her eyes found the dark. Then she looked at the cage beside hers.

It was lower and smaller, the straw fresh that day. In it, curled with its nose against its tail, lay a civet: grey-brown, long-bodied, a dark mask across the face. It watched the corner of the cage, where nothing was. Round its hind leg, worn smooth and thin, was a silver anklet with no bells.

"Kirri," she said.

Nothing moved. She said it again, louder, and then in the voice she had used at eight, across the yard behind the sheds, calling to a twelve-year-old Kirri who was far too clever for her own good and who had always answered, *What?* The civet's ears turned a hair toward the sound. That was all. They turned back.

*Released.* She had lived twenty years on that word. Eleven contracts, and one more, and the page burned and a name given back. She had believed in it the way other people believed in rain.

Kirri had been the best liar in the House. Kirri had wanted a boat.

She hung there by her wrists in the smell of straw and old fear and bitter resin, and said quietly to nobody, "Well. That's the price, then."

She counted the cages. Forty-two, and one of them was hers. She counted them again.

* * *

Chathu came at dawn.

She had not slept. Grey light reached the aisle through a high grille, and then came a step she would have known blind: soft, unhurried, a little uneven on the left foot where he had once broken it. The lamp in his hand swung a slow circle on the straw.

He was an old man, round-shouldered, in a clean white mundu, a plain wooden comb in his hair. A big ledger in black cloth was tucked under his arm, and in his free hand was a tin. His eyes were wet.

"Child," he said. "You look half dead."

"I rode a long way."

"I know. I'm sorry for it." He set the tin on the cage bar. "Jaggery and sesame. I had the cook make them warm. Eat."

She did not look at it. She had learned, around the fifth contract, what a gift was.

"No. Never, when you're frightened." He sighed, sat on an upturned bucket, and laid his palm on the ledger the way another man would stroke a sleeping dog. "I want to tell you how it comes out, Seventeen. People are better for knowing. It is only a matter of accounts."

"Chathu—"

"You came for the antidote. It was well done. You always had a hand." He nodded as at a good sum. "I made that vial, and I know how long it takes. Your husband will be dead by the end of tomorrow, and not before. At dusk I will put the resin on you myself, as I did the first time. I will open this book to your leaf and read it aloud. Then you will be in the cage next to Kirri, and by the next rains you will not remember your own name."

He said it as a physician might say that the bone had to be set.

"Ammu is coming back to me," he added. "I have recalled her. She will carry the resin pot at dusk, and she will stand and watch what comes of a covered scent-post. A lesson is best learned with the eyes."

Neela said nothing. Her throat had closed.

"And Vellan will finish your warden after. Tonight, when the cart comes in at my gate. He has only to wait for it."

*The cart.*

She went cold from the scalp down. Nobody had said a cart in Chathu's hearing. Only two people alive had heard it, in a stable, over a sick man's body. She remembered Muthu's stillness, and *I won't,* said low, and how she had not been able to tell what it was worth.

"Who told you about a cart?"

"A house pays its debts in news, child." He smiled, sadly, and did not say more.

So the order had been hers. *Meet me on the road,* she had said, and had named the hour, and someone had carried it out of Kettamala faster than any cart. She had built the trap herself and sent a dying man to walk into it.

"You sold her," she said. Her voice, when it came, was hoarse. "Kirri. You told her it was release."

"I told her what she needed to hear to do her work." He did not flinch. "And when she was tired, and the work had cost her more than she could pay, I gave her a place. Do you know what happens to an odiyan with no ledger? The crown hunts her. A rogue dies in a ditch with a rope round her neck. Here she is fed. She is kept dry. She is not afraid."

"She doesn't know her own name."

"No. But she is not afraid." He turned the ledger. "Twenty-one years I have kept you, Seventeen. Rice, oil, salve, teachers. A tutor for your letters. I have written down what you cost me. I weep when one of you is lost, and I mean it, and the book balances all the same. Without a leash you would all be hunted dogs. The ledger is mercy."

The old voice was gentle as ever. And she saw, for the first time, that it had never once been lying. He believed it. That was the worst thing about him. He had believed it at the table when he gave her sweets, and he believed it now, at the bars.

"The twelfth contract," she said. "Release."

"Is a lovely word." His smile was sad. "Eleven you did. The twelfth you failed. Release has always been a word for what comes after."

He rose, tucking the book under his arm, and lingered looking down at her. She thought for a moment he would reach through the bars. He did not.

"You are thinking you were owned. You were. You are a clever girl, you have always known it." His head tilted. "But it comes to me you think this book is the whole of it."

She said nothing.

"The ledger isn't the only book, child," said Chathu softly. "It never has been."

He took up the lamp and went away down the aisle with his uneven step. The tin stayed on the bar, the steam still lifting from the lid.

* * *

They came for her an hour later and bound her better.

Three hunters, and Pachan not among them. They took off the soft cord and put on hard, wet leather that would shrink in the heat. They pinned her arms behind her at the elbows, tied her wrists together, and hauled the knot to a staple in the floor. They did not speak. One ran his thumb down the inside of her wrist, over the pale ridged scar, as though it were a mark on a cheese.

Then they left her alone with the light climbing the grille.

*Dusk,* she thought. *Maybe ten hours.*

*Second dusk is tonight. Muthu will have started at first light yesterday, as I told him. If he doesn't meet me, he keeps coming, and he reaches the gate at tonight's dusk.*

That was the arithmetic. A sick man in a cart, at the gate of a yard full of hunters, and Vellan waiting. She worked at the knot until the leather bit. It held.

Chathu had seen her turn at sixteen and written her into a book, and called it mercy. Devan had seen her ten times and more. On the nights since the rampart he had held a river pebble in his cheek, so that in sleep or fever the word could not slip, and he had never asked her to thank him for it.

Both of them held the same word. One kept it in a ledger. One kept it behind his teeth.

If the resin took her at dusk, she would not go into the straw with her mouth shut. She would say it herself, aloud, to someone who was looking. She did not know why that mattered. Only that the word should be hers, once.

Boots came across the yard outside, and a voice she knew, quick and low and out of breath. Not words. Only a tune.

*Pull and pull, the tide is coming in.*

The door at the end of the aisle opened. Light fell down the straw, and Ammu stood in it.

She was mud to the knees, in the plain blue cotton of Valli the pepper trader's girl, her braid half down. A hunter held her elbow, though she was not struggling, and Chathu's voice somewhere behind her said, *Let her see.* She had ridden all night, and her face was grey with it.

She looked down the aisle at the cages. At the fox. At the deer. At the small grey shape in its straw with the silver on its leg.

Then she looked at Neela.

Twenty years. Neela had seen that face laugh and lie, tease and steal and cry itself sick over a dead sparrow. She had never seen it like this: still, and white, with its mouth shut hard over something it was not going to say. Her lips began to shape a line of the tappers' verse, *the name given back,* and stopped before the sound.

Across all the bars, Ammu's eyes held hers, and did not look away.
