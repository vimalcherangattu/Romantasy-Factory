# Chapter 24: The Carved Bird

The second letter went into Sudhi's glove at the padippura an hour after sunrise, and this time Devan was close enough to see the wax.

Plain grey. No mark.

He had come down from the east wall with the cistern chain still clanking in his ears, meaning to check the carts for sealing day. Instead he stopped behind the gate pier, in the shade of the roof, and watched Muthu lean toward Sudhi's pony, a brown, bad-tempered beast called Biscuit, and say something low to the palace rider. Sudhi laughed. Muthu did not.

Two letters in two days. Muthu, who had told Devan every thought in his head since they were boys in the same kalari pit.

Devan waited until Sudhi had gone clattering down the Monkey road. Then he stepped out.

"Another one to your mother?"

Muthu turned. The big face was open and cheerful, and for a half-breath it stayed that way. Then something behind it went out, like a lamp carried past a window.

"Yes," Muthu said.

"She writes often."

"She worries." He rubbed the back of his neck. "I'm her only son. You know how it is."

Devan did know how it was. Muthu's mother wrote once a month, in a hand like hen tracks, and Muthu read the funny parts aloud over supper. Her letter had come and been answered on the last post day. Now there had been one yesterday, off post day, and one this morning. And Muthu stood in the sun with his hands hanging, silent as a post.

Devan had seen that silence before. At twelve, in the pit, Muthu had lost a bout he should have won, and when the master asked, he had gone exactly this quiet and said he'd slipped. He had not slipped. Muthu was loud when he told the truth. He only went still when he lied.

*Ittan's.* The thought was cold and complete. A kind man with a grey-wax hand in Kettamala, and a letter every season that never climbed the hill because it did not need to.

He might be wrong. He had been wrong about the oldest things before, about a girl who slept with her face to the wall, and a knife under her mattress that he had left where it lay. Accusing Muthu in the yard would warn whoever sat at the other end of the thread. Locking him up would blind Devan to everything the letters carried.

"Walk the wall with me," Devan said. "I want your eye on the east cistern before the chain jams again."

"Now?"

"Now. And on sealing day I want you at the gate. You'll open the Keeper's boxes yourself. It's an honour. The garrison should see my captain do it."

He said it lightly, as a kindness between friends. Muthu's laugh came late, and too big.

It was no kindness. If Muthu was Ittan's, then the Keeper's boxes were the one place he would want to fumble. So Devan would stand at the cart himself, an arm's length from those big hands, and Bhaskaran would take the second box whatever Muthu said. A man who meant to hide something could not hide it from both of them. And if Muthu's fingers hesitated over a lid, that would answer Devan better than any letter.

They walked the wall together. Devan kept his face easy and his own counsel. Below them the hounds ran the yard in the morning light, all fourteen, and for the first time in years he saw how few people in this fort he would have trusted with his back.

One of them was still asleep in his bed. He had slid her knife back under her side of the mattress the night after she gave it him, and she had let it lie.

* * *

The stone was warm under Neela's hand, and the cup in its top was empty.

She had come up the second spur in daylight with a plan, which was a thing she did not often have. She would leave a mark. She would wait for Ammu, and give her a message for Chathu: *the warden is near breaking, give me the full moon.* Five nights to the new moon, and she needed every one of them.

Ammu was not there. A man sat on the far side of the slab with his back against the lightning scar, and he was whittling.

"Seventeen," Vellan said.

She had taken a breath to call out. She let it go. Nothing in her face moved. Eleven contracts had taught her that much.

He was near forty, and he had been grey at the temples since she was fifteen. A big man, with a gate's shoulders. His hands were the surprise, as they had always been: thick and scarred, and as careful with a knife as a goldsmith's. A shaving curled off the wood under his thumb and dropped to the red earth between his feet.

"The jackal-girl's at her inn," he said. "I told her I'd keep the stone today. She didn't argue." A grumble that might have been a laugh. "You never could teach her to argue with a man my size."

"What do you want, Vellan?"

"What Chathu wants. New moon." He did not look up from the wood. "He sent me up to see the state of things. I see a bride in a good kasavu with burrs in the border, who's slept soft and eaten well, and who hasn't put a hand to her husband's throat in thirty-odd days."

"The pass doesn't close until the rains."

"The rains aren't the deadline. The moon is." The knife turned. "New moon, Seventeen. Do it, or I do it, and you go home in a collar."

*Collar.* It was not a jest. She saw the kennels, the iron rings in the wall, Kirri's silver anklet with its bells cut off.

Neela made her voice dry. "He sleeps with a sword on the floor."

"Not on your side. He lays it hilt toward you. The watcher says." He glanced up, light eyes under a heavy brow. "A man who gives his wife a sword in the dark is either a fool or he's got something he wants you to see."

She said nothing. He went back to the wood.

But her mind had caught on the last thing he said and would not let go. The watcher walked the hill edge each dawn and read scent-posts. That was all he did. He had never seen the inside of a barred chamber, and he could not know where a sword lay on a floor. Someone inside the fort had told the guild.

She put that away, carefully, where her face would not find it.

"Give me till the full moon."

"No."

"One more cycle. He's closer than he was. I can—"

"I know what you can." There was no heat in it. "I have watched you for twenty years, girl. You've never missed. You've never been slow. Eleven for eleven, and then you stand on a hill and leave a mark in a stone and ask for time." His thumb ran down the grain. "Chathu counts. So do I."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I do it." Calm as weather. "I'll be quicker than you. And cleaner, and he won't know me when I come."

A small shape was coming out of the block under his hands now. A bird. A fat, short-winged thing with its head turned, no larger than a plum.

"You carve for the little ones," she said.

"Somebody should." He blew the dust from its back. "They sleep better with a thing on the mat. It doesn't matter what."

"And the ones you kill? Do they sleep?"

The knife did not stop. "A blade isn't guilty, Seventeen. Only the hand that throws it. I learned that young. It makes a man restful." He turned the bird in his fingers. "You carry it too, whether you say it or not."

She did. She had said it to herself on every road for twenty years. It tasted different in his mouth. In his mouth it tasted like the last thing a man says before the door is shut.

She sat down on the edge of the stone, because standing was beginning to show.

"Vellan." She kept her voice level. "Kirri."

The knife stopped.

"You've seen her. Haven't you? Ammu writes and gets no answer. Chathu says she's where she wished to be. You go to the Resin House more than any of us. Where is she?"

He did not answer. The wind moved the grove behind them, and far down the cistern chain clanked the hour. Vellan looked at the bird in his palm for a good while, then went back to the wood and took a very small curl from the wing.

It was the worst answer he could have given. She sat with it, and did not move.

"Three times," he said at last, to the bird. "Three times I've earned the release. Chathu says there's always one more." He closed his hand. "Go home, Seventeen. Eat something. Do whatever a woman does with five nights."

He was gone before she had counted ten. He did not seem to move. One moment the big shape sat against the lightning scar, and the next there were only shavings on the earth and a smell of scorched tamarind drifting on the wind.

She stayed until her hands stopped shaking. Then she went down the hill and did the thing she had not done for any person since she was six years old.

She broke a line.

* * *

"His name is Vellan," she said.

It was dusk, and he had found her on the east rampart by the housing door, as he did now at the end of most days. He did not touch her. He had a way of standing at an angle that left the wall at her back and the open air at his.

"The guild's enforcer," she went on. "Near forty. Grey at the temples. If you see a very large man who walks without a sound, don't ask him his business." She stared at the terraces going dark. "He's the one who comes if I don't. At the new moon."

Devan was quiet a moment. "You met him."

"At the stone, this morning."

"And he let you walk away."

"He wants me to do it first. It's cleaner." She made herself say the next part. "He gave me five nights. He said, *Do it, or I do it, and you go home in a collar.*"

Something crossed his face that was not fear. It was older than fear. He looked at her wrists, at the pale bands of scar at the edge of her sleeves.

"What does he become?" he said.

She had known the question was coming. She had walked it up the hill and down again all afternoon.

"He's a—"

Her mouth had already shaped the first hard, rounded sound. She bit it off. Devan's eyes had gone, without his seeming to know it, across the yard to the west stair and the dark knob of the newel at its foot. They stayed there a moment. Then they came back to her.

"I can't tell you that."

"Can't."

"Won't. It's the one line I have left, Devan." She heard how her voice sounded. "Everyone in that House has a shape, and the only thing none of us has is the right to say another's out loud. You wouldn't carry the thing in your hands if I gave it you. You'd carry it in your mouth."

He took a breath, and she waited for the argument. It did not come.

"I won't ask," he said. "That one I understand." A pause. "You've told me more than I had any right to."

"There's more. He knew where your sword lies at night. Hilt toward me. He said the watcher told him." She turned from the wall. "The watcher walks the hill edge and sniffs at posts. He can't see through a barred shutter. Someone in your house talks to mine."

He went still. "Someone inside."

"Someone who's seen the floor of that room."

"Janu's girls. Raiu. Half the fort has carried water through it." His voice was flat. "I'll find out."

"I've told you his name and his day. He'll come on the new moon. He's no soldier. He's quiet, and he's patient, and he's done it for twenty years."

"I'll double the watch." He was already counting under his breath. "Two more on the west stair. Another pair on the wall. Raiu on your door tonight, and two men at the stair head."

"Watches don't stop him."

"Everything stops somebody."

"Not him. I've seen him walk through a sentried yard that was full of lamps and leave a carving on a sleeper's mat. Nobody saw him go or come. Double the watch if it helps the men sleep. But don't think a line of spears is a wall."

He looked at her while the dusk went blue behind him.

"Then what is?"

"I don't know. I wish I did." She almost smiled. "Someone inside who doesn't want him there. That's all I've ever seen stop him, and it wasn't me."

The wind came over the rampart. He weighed it, as he weighed everything, the way a man tests a plank bridge before he trusts his weight to it.

"Muthu has been sending letters," he said. "Grey wax, no mark. Two in two days. His mother writes once a month, and he goes quiet when he lies. I think he's Ittan's man."

She considered it honestly before she answered. "He could be. He could be the one who told them about the sword." It made her point harder to say, and she said it anyway. "But a man who writes letters is a man who talks, and talk is slow. Vellan doesn't write. If you spend five nights watching your friend's hands, you'll be looking the wrong way when the other one steps out."

He did not answer at once. His jaw worked, and his eyes went down to the yard, where a big shape crossed the lamplight with a laugh she could hear from here. Whatever he had built about Muthu, he had not built it lightly. He held his own wrist, hard, as if it were something he might drop.

"Then I'll keep Muthu close and watch the stair," he said. "Both. But I'll set the stair first, because you asked."

*Because you asked.* It was a plain thing, said plainly. She had given him a name she owed to nobody, and he had weighed it against a man he had known since boyhood, and it had come down on her side.

"Thank you," she said.

"Don't." But his ears had gone dark, and he looked away, down at the housing, where the lamp in its niche was just catching. "You'll be in the chamber tonight?"

"Yes."

"I'll bar the shutters. Raiu's at the door."

* * *

The shutter bar went across at full dark. She heard it settle in its brackets, and the third slat gave its small creak, and Devan went to see the west stair for himself. He would be gone an hour. He had kissed her hand at the door, just the knuckles, and gone down the passage with a sword at his hip and fourteen hounds' worth of worry on his shoulders.

Raiu stood outside with his cowlick and his torch. She had heard him cough twice.

She took her time. She undid her braid. She set the kindi right on its side of the bed, spout to the pillow, as every night, and sat on the edge to unwind the border from her day cloth.

The pillow was wrong.

It was only a little wrong. The cloth lay as Janu's girl had smoothed it. But in the dip where her head would go, small and pale in the lamplight, sat a thing the size of a plum.

A bird.

She did not touch it. She sat very still and counted. The door: watched by a man she knew by name. The shutters: barred on the inside by a man she trusted. The third slat: untouched, or it would have sung. A chamber with one lamp and one bed and no place at all for a very big man to hide.

Then she looked, and she made herself see it.

Fresh-cut wood. The body round, the wing folded close, the head turned a little to one side, the way it had been turning in his hands on the hill. The beak was whole. Under it, on the cloth, lay one curl of shaving, still smelling of scorched tamarind.

He had finished it on the hill, under her eyes. Then he had carried it down and into the fort.

She laid her palm over the bird and then took it up. It was cold. So was the cloth beneath it. It had lain here a good while, in a bed no one had touched since morning.

*He sat in this room while I sat on his hill.*

Sometime between the hour she went up the spur and the hour she came back, in a fort of sixty soldiers and fourteen hounds, Vellan had walked through the warden's chamber and set a gift where she would put her head. He had come straight from her. He had not troubled to hide it. He had wanted her to find it. And he had walked in past someone, or been let in by someone, who knew where the sword lay.

She heard Raiu cough again, on the other side of the door. He was nineteen. He was still on his feet.

*Five nights,* she thought. And then, because she had counted all her life and did not know how to stop: *He means four.*
